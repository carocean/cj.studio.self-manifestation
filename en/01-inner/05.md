# Intelligence Theory

Someone said to the Creator:

“The paramecium is as crude as can be. Its form is like a thin garment; inside, as if soup had been poured into it. It eats through one opening and expels through that same opening. It takes in food and stores it in bubbles within, almost as though it had a stomach.

This tiny thing is born in damp foam. It waves its cilia to move toward food; it wriggles its body to avoid harm. It avoids light; it enters shade. It mates when it meets its kind; it detours when it meets obstacles.

Now I could make one in its likeness and give it the ability to approach, to avoid harm, and to distinguish same from different. If so, would it not be as though it had spirit and were born alive? To me it would be nothing but a few segments of program.

And if I feed it with electricity, I may spare it the trouble of digestion. If I change its program, I may increase what it cannot do. Yet the paramecium must wait ten thousand years of evolution before it gains a single change—why is it so slow? Is not the intelligence I make greater than its own?”

The Creator smiled and replied:

“Take the Turing Test: a machine answers every question put to it—can that almost be called intelligence?

The snake-head butterfly uses the form of a snake’s head to ward off its natural enemies. Must it be that without being a snake-head butterfly it could not live? Why should such a thing arise?

A man can paint a snake’s head with ease. Yet in nature, how could one know the harm among these—the butterfly to the snake, the snake to its enemy?

For example, take a photograph of a person.  
The image and the real are alike in form.  
Who can say which is the real,  
and which is not?  
Why not know that intelligence, in relation to the real,  
is as image to its body,  
truly as shadow to form?

When wood is chiseled, does the chisel know the woodpecker’s knowing of chiseling?”

Someone asked:

“Then what is intelligence, in relation to its real?”

The Creator answered:

“What you make may be made to approach, yet it does not know the presence of what surrounds it. It may be made to eat, yet it does not know the taste of food. Thus the environment does not manifest within it. It is like a waterwheel working through water yet not knowing water’s softness; like an airplane riding on wind yet not knowing wind’s buoyancy.

Therefore intelligence cannot serve as the measure of intelligence: its intending-to-point does not reach its pointed-to.  
Therefore, though you may model billions upon billions of states, not a single thing is made manifest; though you may model billions upon billions of abilities, you do not know one ability—can that suffice?

So: what determines that a thing is not a thing—is it not Self-Manifestation? The intelligence you make is mistaking clothes for a person.”

Gathering the point, CJ thus says:

“When one speaks of observer and intelligence: intelligence is the outer; Self-Manifestation is the inner. To take the outer for the inner, to pursue in vain—one must be cautious in such pursuit.”
