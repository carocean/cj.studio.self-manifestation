# Language

Clever words shift with ease—  
they may call it east, they may call it west—this is rhetoric.  
In speaking of things and recounting affairs, there are often deviations;  
words may be exhausted and yet fail to reach the meaning—  
not because speech is unskilled.  
What Imaginal Form would convey to another often loses its tone;  
this is the helpless in-between of Imaginal Form transmission.

What Imaginal Form refers to is directly seen, as if alive;  
in return, it may take form.  
Shallow thought moves within inner illumination;  
deep thought moves within language.  
What gains its affect within—Imaginal Form is like a lamp.

Tracing response,  
it turns into form, into sound, into color, into taste,  
and is privately stored within the Field.  
Each held within the Private,  
it is externally named through shared reference.  
Named by sound, it is called speech;  
named in writing, it is called character;  
taken together, they are called language.  
Language holds shared reference outward,  
yet is held as privately shared—  
thus speech often does not arise from within,  
and it is difficult to reach another’s affect.

Therefore, Imaginal Form refers to response;  
language also refers to response;  
once language is taken, it falls into each one’s own Imaginal Form.

Because language refers outward and is shared,  
it may stand outside the Imaginal Realm and yet remain.  
Read or heard,  
it comes to be understood within each Imaginal Realm.

CJ often sighs:  
Why has evolution not allowed Imaginal Form to pass directly,  
but must turn outward in detour?  
Yet if not so,  
who could make the Private non-private and yet keep it private?

Language may refer with precision,  
may refer coarsely,  
may refer to shifting affect,  
may speak signs of what can and cannot be spoken.  
Thus in its beginning it was precise in stating—  
one sound, one response;  
later it unfolded into coarseness,  
to carry affect.

Thus, in the beginning it tends toward precision,  
fixing names upon features;  
later it tends toward coarseness,  
broadening its use.

At first, tending to precision—number arises;  
thus number serves to state the features of response:  
as measure, as boundary, as large, as small,  
as inclusion, as before and after—  
each corresponding in its reference.

Later, tending to coarseness—affairs arise;  
thus there are films, poems, songs, histories,  
carried in sound and character,  
responding to you and me,  
forming private affect—  
high-order Imaginal Forms arise, each on its own.

Therefore, the boundary of language  
is the boundary of symbolic reference  
of Imaginal Form of response.  
What is called symbol—  
may be sound, may be character.  
If it remains outside, it loses what it refers to;  
only when it falls into the Rest-Realm does it move of itself—  
this is called the Language-Realm.  
Because what it refers to rests within private affect,  
the Language-Realm of each Self-Manifesting Being differs.  
Thus when it is said “the boundary of language is the boundary of the world,”  
that Language-Realm is only that of one.

Language—outside, it has no meaning;  
only when concretized within the Imaginal Realm does it live.  
Thus Imaginal Form is the ground upon which language runs.  
Otherwise, it stands outside no different from other things.  
Because it carries symbolic reference,  
it may fall into a fixed domain and operate its reference—  
thus there are computers.  
Yet within a fixed domain it cannot intend its own symbols,  
thus it does not know what its symbols refer to;  
it follows symbolic rules and operates by them.  
Therefore, the computer does not know language;  
it has no Language-Realm,  
but only turns within the Symbol-Realm by rule.

What one sees alone is narrow;  
what many see is broad.  
Thus language may enlarge each one’s Imaginal Form.  
Those clear see more;  
those who think deeply know more.  
Their speech cannot align with those who have less;  
thus it is better not to speak, but to know by direct seeing.  
If one could know by direct seeing,  
Imaginal Form would advance of itself—  
why must language arise?

When CJ thinks deeply,  
language arises of itself,  
leaping among Imaginal Forms,  
turning along the path of thought,  
until thought is spent into emptiness,  
and must be thought again.  
Using language to tune Imaginal Form and bring it forth,  
then unfolding the next step—  
this is the common course of thought.

Yet must the tuning of Imaginal Form in thought  
be by language?

Try thinking without language:  
there is image-thinking.  
Its advance is like seeing a picture;  
one must practice it again and again,  
until the image stands mirror-clear within—  
then thought flows quietly.

Do not children all think thus?  
Do not beasts all think thus?  
Do not those of highest mind perhaps also think thus?

Thus I know:  
language is not necessary for thought;  
and therefore,  
it is not necessary for the Self-Manifesting Being.
