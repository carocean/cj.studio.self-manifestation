Introduction

The world we perceive daily seems to consist of stable objects and structures. Yet at the level of signals, input is in fact a continuously changing flow.

The fundamental question is already posed: why does a cognitive system "see stability" in changing input? The direction is also set: stability is not a property of the world, but a result produced by the cognitive system under constraint.

But in pursuing "where does stability come from," static attribution necessarily comes to a halt: none of the three paths of explanation can finally explain stability, and stability can only be assumed. The work of this chapter is to supply what is missing at that halting point — not by finding yet another static path of explanation, but by converting the question from "from which more-stable layer does stability come" into "in what dynamical process does stability emerge."

So the question is no longer:

If stability must be had, what minimal conditions are required? (the retreated form of the question)

but the earlier one:

Why does stability necessarily emerge, rather than having to be assumed?

A preliminary distinction: two treatments of the source of stability

Before the argument begins, two ways of treating "the source of stability" must be distinguished, for the fundamental difference between this chapter and static attribution lies exactly here.

Treatment 1: the source of stability as a problem of static attribution.

That is, take stability as a state and ask to which more basic layer it belongs — statistical distribution, physical constraint, or the outcome of selection. Static attribution is exactly this path. Its conclusion: to whichever layer one attributes it, that layer's own stability needs explaining in turn, so that one either regresses infinitely, introduces a "quasi-absolute," or circles. This road necessarily fails — not because the arguing fell short, but because the very form of the question, "explain stability by a more stable layer," is destined to regress or circle.

Treatment 2: the source of stability as a problem of dynamical emergence.

That is, do not ask to which more-stable layer stability belongs; ask in what process stability is generated. This chapter walks this road. The source of stability is not some higher stable layer, but the process of competition between Trace-Response deepening and dispersal by Field Quality: this process itself presupposes no stable layer; it sets out from continuous flow (the unstable) and, through the competition of deepening and dispersal, presses out the stable segment.

The key difference between the two treatments: Treatment 1 takes stability as a result awaiting attribution and necessarily regresses to some assumed stable layer; Treatment 2 takes stability as a result awaiting generation and presses it out of the unstable continuous flow, needing no prior stable layer at all.

This chapter adopts Treatment 2. The meaning of the phrase "the source of stability" is hereby fixed: it names the dynamical conditions of stability's generation, not a static layer to which stability is attributed.

Definition 1: stability (structural stratum)

"Stability" is not "the invariance locked by some higher layer," nor "the constancy of a statistical mean," but "the sedimentary segment formed and maintained, within the competition of deepening and dispersal, by continuous flow acting repeatedly upon the Sedimentation-Field through Trace-Response": when deepening presses past dispersal the Sedimentation-Degree grows, and when the two balance at the fixed point δ\* the segment is maintained (Appendix 2); a stable segment is a "segment" only if it also keeps a discriminable sedimentary difference from its surroundings (counter-example 1; Appendix 2, Section 3, supplementary note). Note: this definition presupposes no higher stable layer — the source of stability is a dynamical process, not a more basic stability.

The stream of argument

We proceed by sustained questioning, step by step.

I. Static attribution: what it establishes and what it lacks

First examine the argumentative basis of static attribution.

That treatment fixed the direction (stability is produced by cognition under constraint, not inherent in the world) and proved: a system that can act necessarily contains minimal stable structure, whose minimal condition is the conjunction of temporal continuity, detectable difference, and repeatable pattern. This part this chapter preserves in full.

But it must be stated clearly: what was proven is "granted that stability is wanted, what is required," not "why stability exists at all"; this is admitted in its own conclusion — "stability is assumed, not explained."

The gap lies exactly here: between "continuous flow" and "stability necessarily exists" a generative link is missing. This chapter does not go around it but answers it directly: stability is generated by deepening pressing past dispersal.

Therefore:

continuous flow + finite resources ⇏ (by static attribution alone) the necessary emergence of stability.

This is where the gap lies. This chapter supplies it.

II. Continuous flow and Trace-Response (the dynamical starting point)

To supply the gap, one must set out from something lower than "stability" — from Trace-Response.

Definition 2: Trace-Response

Trace-Response is the trace left by continuous flow acting upon the field (Field Chapter 1, Equation 1: τₜ(x)=Tτ(πₜ,Fₜ,x)). Trace-Response presupposes no stability — it is merely action leaving trace, a fact of the physical stratum; every action of the continuous flow produces Trace-Response.

The continuous flow is not homogeneous: flows have strength, direction, and repetition. Repeating flow leaves traces at the same Structural Position again and again, and the traces stack; non-repeating flow scatters its traces. This is the basic non-uniformity of Trace-Response.

【The real boundary · Gap One: why continuous flow must contain repeated structure】

But here one must stop and point out a place that may not be slid past. All the deepening logic that follows depends on "the flow containing persistently repeated structure" — only repetition stacks traces, and only stacking deepens. Yet continuous flow does not equal repeating flow: a continuously changing flow can, in principle, differ everywhere and never repeat.

To derive "must repeat" from "difference detectable" by arguing "not being fully uniform random noise, it must carry repeated structure" is a substitution: detectable difference requires only that the flow not be fully uniform noise; it does not entail that the flow contains repetition — a flow that differs everywhere and whose direction drifts without cease is difference-detectable, yet no Structural Position need ever be hit repeatedly. Between "non-uniform" and "contains repetition" an independent argument is missing.

Therefore, the passage from "continuous flow" to "repeated structure" is this chapter's first gap, and it lies before the deepening logic. To supply it, one must answer: under what conditions does a continuous input flow necessarily hit certain Structural Positions repeatedly (rather than scattering its traces forever)? This question is resolved in Appendix 1: the field's Structural Positions are finite (the set of Structural Positions is finite — a premise of this chapter), the continuous flow does not cease (a premise of this chapter), and finitely many positions receiving sustained input must, by the pigeonhole principle, have some position hit repeatedly with an unbounded count; the field's merging mechanism (the Sedimentation-Field "favors what is near to it") further presses similar inputs into same-position repetition, raising the density of repetition far above the pigeonhole bound (a mechanistic direction, not rigorously proven). The existence and unboundedness of Gap One are thereby proven, and persistence is listed as specification H0; see Appendix 1.

This chapter therefore carries two gaps, not one:

Gap One (before repetition): why must continuous flow repeatedly hit certain Structural Positions? — raised in this section; proven in Appendix 1.

Gap Two (after repetition): why must repeated flow, through the deepening–dispersal competition, converge to a stable fixed point δ\*? — raised in Section IV; closed to conditional form in Appendix 2.

The complete chain is: continuous flow → (Gap One) → repeated structure → trace accumulation → deepening → (Gap Two) → stability. To mark only Gap Two and miss Gap One is an error of the same kind: believing that one place is lacking when in fact two are. Both must be marked and handled: Gap One's existence proven unconditionally and its persistence listed as specification H0 (Appendix 1); Gap Two closed to conditional form (Appendix 2); the specifications awaiting the Field Chapters' confirmation number six (Appendix 2, Section 4).

III. The competition of deepening and dispersal

Trace-Response acts repeatedly and deepens the Sedimentation-Field; at the same time, Field Quality disperses what has been accumulated. These are two opposed processes.

Definition 3: deepening and dispersal

Deepening (Dτ): the increase of Sedimentation-Degree caused by Trace-Response (the Dτ term of Field Chapter 1, Equation 2).

Dispersal (Eλ): the decrease of Sedimentation-Degree caused by Field Quality (the Eλ term of Field Chapter 1, Equation 2).

The evolution equation of Sedimentation-Degree (Field Chapter 1, Equation 2):

δₜ₊₁(x) = δₜ(x) + Dτₜ(x) − Eλₜ(x)

This equation is the core of this chapter. It says: the change of Sedimentation-Degree is the difference of deepening and dispersal.

IV. The direction of stability's emergence, and Gap Two (repeated structure → fixed point)

With the competition of deepening and dispersal in hand, the question of the source of stability turns from "to which more-stable layer does it belong" into "how is it generated within the deepening–dispersal competition." This is the substantive advance of this chapter over static attribution: that treatment took stability as an irreducible, only-assumable starting point; this chapter locates it as the result of the competition of deepening and dispersal within sedimentation dynamics (Field Chapter 1, Equation 2).

But it must be strictly pointed out: locating "the source of stability" in the deepening–dispersal competition is not the same as having proven the emergence of stability. Here is a distinction that may not be blurred.

The formulation that does not stand (an assumption in place of a proof):

To write directly "at Structural Positions where Dτ \> Eλ, δ grows monotonically, hence stability emerges" does not stand. Assuming net growth (Dτ \> Eλ) directly yields only a conditional conclusion of growth: δ grows. But monotone growth guarantees neither convergence to a finite level nor the formation of a discriminable difference from the surroundings, so the proof that stability is generated has not been completed; what is assumed has merely moved down from "stability" to "Dτ \> Eλ," and where it comes from is left unexplained.

What this section (Gap Two) really has to prove:

For the emergence of stability to be proven, it must be shown that Dτ \> Eλ is not assumed but necessarily derived from "repeated flow + finite resources" — concretely, that the sedimentary evolution has a stable fixed point δ\* \> 0:

From Field Chapter 1, Equation 2, δₜ₊₁ = δₜ + Dτ(δ) − Eλ(δ), the fixed-point argument should take this shape: if deepening Dτ increases with repeated inflow and dispersal Eλ increases with δ, then on δ \< δ\* one has Dτ \> Eλ (δ grows of itself) and on δ \> δ\* one has Dτ \< Eλ (δ falls back of itself), and δ\* is a fixed point approached (from below). The Sedimentation-Degree of the position then converges to δ\* — whereupon "Dτ \> Eλ" is no assumed condition, but a natural consequence of the fixed-point dynamics on δ \< δ\*; a discriminable stable segment further requires an inter-position differential (Appendix 2, Section 3, supplementary note).

But it must be pointed out at once: in the sentence above, "dispersal Eλ increases with δ" is a hypothetical functional form not yet confirmed by the Field Chapters; it is used here only to sketch the shape the fixed-point argument ought to have and constitutes no existing basis. A check of Field Chapter 1 shows that the dispersal term Eλ presently has only qualitative descriptions ("Structural Positions shallow through Field Quality," "Field Quality decides the speed of forgetting," "makes the trace fade"), with no functional dependence of Eλ on δ given. "Eλ increases with δ" is therefore neither proven nor refuted — it is a piece the fixed-point argument needs and the Field Chapters have not yet supplied. To treat it silently as known (just as the formulation above treated Dτ \> Eλ as known) would be a repetition of the same error. The breach argument

Counter-example 1: the fully random flow system.

The input is fully uniform random noise. A point easily mis-stated must be cleared first: by the pigeonhole theorem of Appendix 1, even with random noise, finitely many Structural Positions receiving a sustained flow are hit repeatedly all the same (the pigeonhole uses only finiteness and sustainedness and does not ask whether the input is random) — so one may not say that the random flow "has no repeated hits." The difficulty of the random flow lies in the discriminable stable segment: under uniformly random input, every position has the same hit statistics; under the illustrative setting in which all positions share the same evolution functions, the same initial values (or the same initial distribution), and an input process symmetric across positions, the distributions of their Sedimentation-Degrees are the same, providing no systematic inter-position differential sourced in hit statistics; where these symmetry conditions fail, one can say only that hit statistics provide no systematic differential (actual trajectories may fluctuate randomly; whether fluctuations can become a discriminable stable segment this chapter does not discuss). Yet a "stable segment" is a segment precisely in requiring a discriminable sedimentary difference from its surroundings. This is a boundary of the form, not a counter-example: the emergence of a stable segment requires not only the convergence of a single position's Sedimentation-Degree but also an inter-position differential (Appendix 2, Section 3, supplementary note). Non-uniform hitting is one possible source of inter-position differential; but whether non-uniform statistics lead to a discriminable differential, and whether they satisfy H0 (bounded hit intervals), must be proved separately — hit frequency and hit intervals are not the same thing.

Counter-example 2: the infinite-resource system.

If resources were infinite, the system could retain every moment's full input without compression. Then C (compression structure) would be unnecessary, and stability need not be pressed out by deepening — it could be stored item by item. This shows that compression and deepening are products of finite resources. But an infinite-resource system does not exist in the physical world (finiteness is a base premise), so this is a conceptual boundary and does not overturn the theorem.

## Appendix 1: The Repeated-Hit Theorem (the proof of Gap One)

1\. Proposition

Let the system satisfy this chapter's premises: continuous input (the flow does not cease) and finite resources (the set of Structural Positions X is finite, \|X\| = N \< ∞). Then some Structural Position is necessarily hit repeatedly, and the count of hits is unbounded.

2\. Proof

Let the input flow act upon the field at discrete moments t = 1, 2, 3, … without ceasing. Each moment's action lands on at least one Structural Position (Trace-Response leaves trace, Field Chapter 1, Equation 1 — action must leave trace, and a trace has a position).

Consider the first N + 1 moments: N + 1 actions land on N Structural Positions; by the pigeonhole principle, at least one Structural Position is hit at least twice.

Further, consider an arbitrarily long period T: T actions land on N Structural Positions, so at least one Structural Position is hit at least ⌈T/N⌉ times. As T → ∞ (continuous input; the flow does not stop), the maximal hit count → ∞.

Hence: upon finitely many Structural Positions receiving a sustained input flow, repeated hitting is not merely possible but necessary, and grows without bound over time. ∎

3\. Merging reinforcement (a field-level mechanism that makes repetition denser)

The proof above used only finiteness and sustainedness, and no active mechanism of the field. The field in fact also has a merging mechanism that makes repetition denser: the Sedimentation-Field "favors what is near to it" (the tension principle among the five meta-principles of creation of Sedimentation-Field Theory; the convergence of resolution flow in the Field Chapters) — inputs that are similar but not identical are drawn by the field to similar Structural Positions. This means: even if no two inputs in the flow are exactly the same, the field's merging presses "similar" into "same-position repetition." The pigeonhole gives a lower bound on repetition (a mathematical necessity); merging pushes the actual density of repetition far above that bound (a field mechanism).

4\. Boundary remark

The theorem depends on "Structural Positions are finite." Should someone offer "infinitely many Structural Positions" as a counter-example: an infinite-position system does not satisfy this chapter's finite-resource premise and lies outside its domain. A physically realized field has finitely many Structural Positions. (The Field Capacity Cap(F) of Field Chapter 1 counts stably distinguishable Domains; it points the same way as the finiteness of Structural Positions but is not the same quantity; this appendix uses only that the set X of Structural Positions is finite.)

The theorem gives "some position is necessarily hit repeatedly," and does not guarantee "every position is hit repeatedly" — nor is the latter needed: repeated hits provide candidate positions for sustained deepening; Appendix 2 proves, under the stated conditions, the convergence of a single position's Sedimentation-Degree; a discriminable stable segment still requires a separate proof of inter-position differentials (Appendix 2, Section 3, supplementary note). The non-uniform distribution of repeated hits is one possible source of inter-position differentials; whether it forms a differential must be proved separately.

The degree of closure of Gap One (marked exactly as it stands): what this theorem closes is the existence and unboundedness of repeated hits — some position is hit infinitely many times. But a seam between it and Appendix 2 must be pointed out: Appendix 2 takes a time window of bounded length as one step and requires a hit within every step ("sustained repeated hitting"), whereas "infinitely many in total" does not entail this — hit intervals may lengthen without bound (e.g., at moments 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, …), the count infinite while the frequency tends to zero, while dispersal keeps acting, so that δ may be pulled back forever and never reach δ\*. "A positive lower bound on frequency" (as a long-run average) does not suffice either: it allows unbounded intervals. Persistence must therefore be listed as an independent condition, recorded as H0 (bounded hit intervals): there exists a finite Δ ≥ 1 such that the position is hit at least once in every time window of length Δ (hits counted after the field's merging). The physical basis of H0 lies in the merging mechanism (Section 3) — a sustained input flow, through "favoring what is near," is pressed toward a few principal positions, whose hits are maintained by the sustainedness of the flow — but this argument depends on the statistical properties of the input flow and of merging; this appendix does not pretend it proven, and lists it as one of the specifications for the Field Chapters.

The exact conclusion of Appendix 1 is therefore: the existence and unboundedness of repeated hits are proven unconditionally (pigeonhole); the persistence of hits (bounded hit intervals, H0) has a definite mechanistic basis (merging) but is not rigorously proven, and is listed as a specification.

## Appendix 2: The Conditional Fixed-Point Theorem (the handling of Gap Two)

1\. The manner of handling

The complete closure of Gap Two requires the concrete functional forms of Dτ(δ) and Eλ(δ), which the Field Chapters have not yet given; this appendix does not assume concrete functions on its own authority.

This appendix does what can be done rigorously: it gives a group of conditions on the evolution functions and the hitting process (condition group H, ultimately the clauses listed among H0–H6) together with illustrative settings, and proves that for every Dτ(δ), Eλ(δ) satisfying them, the sedimentary evolution necessarily has a fixed point δ\* \> 0 (existence needing only H1–H4) and, under H6, converges to it (H6 being a sufficient condition) — that is, the Sedimentation-Degree of that position converges to a positive fixed point. Gap Two is thereby split in halves: the mathematical half (H ⇒ δ\* exists, converged to one-sidedly from below under H6) is proven here; the physical half (whether the actual field satisfies H and the illustrative settings) is narrowed to explicit specifications upon the Field Chapters — once the Field Chapters supply the functions, one checks whether they satisfy H and the illustrative settings; if so, the Sedimentation-Degree of that position is known to converge over the period considered; a discriminable stable segment further requires an inter-position differential condition (Section 3, supplementary note).

2\. Condition group H

Consider a Structural Position x under repeated hitting (its existence guaranteed by Appendix 1). Illustrative setting: take a time window of length Δ from H0 as one step, with the deepening and dispersal within the window depending only on the current Sedimentation-Degree (autonomy); the sedimentary evolution is then δₜ₊₁ = δₜ + Dτ(δₜ) − Eλ(δₜ). Autonomy is the illustrative setting of this appendix, and the theorem holds only for this class of evolutions. The conditions must hold throughout the iteration; if they hold only within a finite declared window, the conclusion is confined to that window (convergence then yields only monotone approach within the window). δ\_max is taken as an inner-sinking floor fixed within the declared window (the δmax of Field Chapter 1 Definition 9 generally varies with the field state; here its fixed value over the period considered is taken). Posit:

H1 (bounds on deepening; a specification): at a position satisfying H0, taking a window of length Δ as one step, d₀ ≤ Dτ(δ) ≤ D\_max (d₀ \> 0) for all δ ∈ \[0, δ\_max).

Sense: Field Chapter 1 Definition 12, "recurrence has a deepening effect (Dτ \> 0, within the deepenable range)," gives the direction for positivity; but a hit does not automatically guarantee a positive deepening at every step — the concrete deepening rule and the deepenable condition (δ \< δmax) must be satisfied — and neither the uniform floor d₀ nor the uniform ceiling D\_max is given by Field Chapter 1. H1 as a whole is therefore listed as a specification; the physical direction for D\_max is that the input of a single step is finite.

H2 (dispersal from zero and increasing with depth): Eλ(0) = 0, and Eλ(δ) is monotone non-decreasing in δ.

Sense: no sediment, nothing to disperse (dispersal is the fading of accumulated trace; zero trace, zero dispersal — this stands directly by the definition of dispersal and is no extra assumption); the deeper the accumulation, the dispersable quantity per unit time does not decrease.

H3 (dispersal finally presses past deepening): there exists δ\_c \< δ\_max such that Eλ(δ) \> D\_max for all δ \> δ\_c.

Sense: at sufficient depth, dispersal presses past any bounded deepening. Field Chapter 1 gives an inner-sinking floor δmax (Definition 9), but does not give that dispersal presses past deepening at depth; the Field Capacity Cap(F) counts distinguishable Domains and is not a depth ceiling, so this clause cannot be derived from Cap \< ∞. H3 is therefore listed as a specification; its physical direction rests on crowding, covering, and saturation at depth.

H4 (continuity): Dτ(δ) − Eλ(δ) is continuous in δ.

Sense: this clause requires g to be continuous in the sedimentary state δ; that physical processes are continuous in time does not directly imply continuous dependence on δ, so H4 is listed as a specification.

3\. The conditional fixed-point theorem

Theorem: if a Structural Position satisfying H0 satisfies H1–H4 under the illustrative settings, then there exists δ\* ∈ (0, δ\_c\] such that:

(i) Existence: Dτ(δ*) = Eλ(δ*) (a balance point of zero net growth exists).

Proof: let g(δ) = Dτ(δ) − Eλ(δ). By H1 and H2: g(0) = Dτ(0) − 0 ≥ d₀ \> 0. By H3: g(δ) \< 0 for δ \> δ\_c. By H4: g is continuous. By the intermediate-value theorem, there exists δ\* ∈ (0, δ\_c\] with g(δ\*) = 0. ∎

(ii) Attraction (under H6): take δ\* to be the least zero of g. Then g \> 0 on \[0, δ*) (δ grows of itself toward δ*); under H6 (the iteration does not overshoot), starting from any δ₀ ∈ \[0, δ*), δₜ converges to δ*.

Proof: on \[0, δ*), since δ* is the least zero, g(0) \> 0, and g is continuous, g has no zero on this interval and is positive throughout. Under H6, if δₜ ∈ \[0, δ*), then δₜ \< δₜ₊₁ = δₜ + g(δₜ) ≤ δ*; if δₜ = δ\* at some step, it stays there thereafter. Hence (δₜ) is non-decreasing and bounded above by δ*, and has a limit ℓ ∈ \[δ₀, δ*\]. By the continuity of g, taking limits in δₜ₊₁ = δₜ + g(δₜ) gives ℓ = ℓ + g(ℓ), i.e., g(ℓ) = 0; since g is positive on \[0, δ*), ℓ = δ*. ∎

The discrete overshoot problem (marked exactly as it stands): the proof above uses "the iteration does not pass beyond δ*." Equation 2 is a discrete difference equation (δₜ₊₁ = δₜ + g(δₜ)); if the increment at some δₜ \< δ* is too large, the discrete iteration can jump past δ\* in one step, or even oscillate (the discrete logistic map is of this kind). The increment condition for no overshoot: g(δ) ≤ δ\* − δ for δ ∈ \[0, δ*) (or an equivalent local step bound), recorded as H6. H6 is the sufficient condition adopted by this proof, not a necessary condition for convergence: e.g., Dτ ≡ 1, Eλ(δ) = 1.5δ, δₜ₊₁ = 1 − 0.5δₜ; starting from 0 the iteration passes beyond δ* = 2/3 (H6 fails) and still converges to δ\* with oscillation. Without H6 this proof does not guarantee convergence, though other conditions may still guarantee it. The physical sense of H6 is the small-step character of sedimentary evolution — a single step's deepening is far smaller than the depth scale of the structure; this appendix does not pretend it proven and lists it as a specification. The undershoot condition (H5, see the next paragraph) cannot replace H6: e.g., Dτ ≡ 1, Eλ(δ) = 2δ (H1–H4 hold, taking δ\_max \> 0.75, D\_max = 1, δ\_c = 0.5), δₜ₊₁ = 1 − δₜ, with g crossing downward at δ\* = 0.5; starting from 0.25 the iteration alternates in a two-cycle between 0.25 and 0.75, always non-negative, and never converges.

The strict boundary of attraction (marked exactly as it stands): what the proof above (under H6) gives is "one-sided convergence to δ\* from below" — that is, the Sedimentation-Degree of a single position rises from a low initial value to δ*; this is not the formation of a discriminable stable segment (Section 3, supplementary note). For perturbations from above: H5 (g crosses downward at δ*) and H6′ (g(δ) ≥ δ\* − δ for δ ∈ (δ*, δ*+η\], η \> 0) together guarantee falling back from above without overshoot, monotonically, and converging to δ\* (an iteration starting in (δ*, δ*+η\] decreases monotonically, is bounded below by δ*, and its limit is a zero; this requires g to have no zero in (δ*, δ*+η\], which is the sense taken by the downward crossing of H5). This pair is a sufficient condition for this proof, not a necessary condition for attraction in general: e.g., δₜ₊₁ = 1 − 0.5δₜ, starting above δ* = 2/3, overshoots (H6′ fails) and still converges with oscillation. Where this pair fails, this proof does not apply, though other conditions may still guarantee attraction. If g is tangent at δ\* (a zero of even multiplicity), δ\* is semi-stable against perturbations from above. In sum: what this chapter has proven is one-sided convergence from below under H1–H4 plus H6; it does not claim two-sided attraction; with H1–H4 alone, or with only H5 added, convergence of the discrete iteration is not guaranteed (see the two-cycle example in the preceding paragraph).

(iii) The single-position conclusion (conditional): under H0, H1–H4, H6, and the illustrative settings (the conditions holding throughout the iteration), the Sedimentation-Degree of the position, starting in \[0, δ*), converges monotonically to δ* \> 0, and "Dτ \> Eλ" on \[0, δ\*) is a consequence of the theorem, not an assumption. The substitution of an assumption for a proof is thereby removed: what is posited is no longer Dτ \> Eλ, but condition group H and the illustrative settings — each clause with its own independent physical sense (bounded hit intervals, bounds on deepening, no accumulation no dispersal, dispersal at depth pressing past deepening, continuity in δ, small-step evolution), not a rewriting of the conclusion.

Supplementary note (the discriminable stable segment): the above proves only that the Sedimentation-Degree of a single Structural Position converges to a positive fixed point, not that a discriminable stable segment forms. A stable segment is a "segment" only if it has a discriminable sedimentary difference from its surroundings (counter-example 1): a comparison set K (the position and its surrounding positions) and a differential threshold ε\_δ (of the same dimension as δ, in the form of Field Chapter 1 Equation 11 (4a)) must be declared, and δ\_x − δ\_y \> ε\_δ must hold over the period considered (y ranging over the surrounding positions). The existence of a positive fixed point does not imply this: e.g., several positions with the same initial value and the same input, each evolving by δₜ₊₁ = 0.1 + 0.5δₜ, all converge to 0.2, and their differences are always zero. The conditions under which inter-position differentials hold (e.g., differences in the positions' evolution functions or hit statistics, and their persistence under dispersal) are not proven in this chapter and are registered as to be proved.
