# Objectification Limit Form

〔v1.1 note: relative to v1.0, this version alters no mathematical argument (the pigeonhole-style proofs of compression's inevitability, equivalence classes, truncation, and reuse are all retained and stood already); it makes only three alignments of precision, occasioned by the terminological drop created when Form Chapter 1 was upgraded to v2.0. The three are: (1) the rectification of "stability" in Section V, "the emergence of stability" — this chapter's "stability" is set-theoretic (an equivalence class revisited infinitely often), and Form Chapter 1 v2.0's dynamical stability (the emergence of the fixed point δ\*) is a different level; this version cuts the two apart expressly, lest a reader take this chapter to fill Form Chapter 1's Gap Two; (2) the quantifier of Section V made precise, as the infinite pigeonhole over the actual state sequence; (3) the mutual reference with Form Chapter 1 v2.0's Appendix 1 connected — the two are one and the same pigeonhole, and this chapter is its original source.〕

I. Basic Setup

Let a system S satisfy:

1\. The input is a temporal sequence F(1), F(2), …, F(t)  
2\. The input history is denoted h\_t = (F(1), …, F(t))  
3\. The set of all possible histories is denoted 𝓗\_t  
4\. The state space is R, with \|R\| \< ∞  
5\. The state is given by a mapping M\_t : 𝓗\_t → R

As input continues to generate new information, \|𝓗\_t\| grows strictly with t, and for sufficiently large t:

\|𝓗\_t\| \> \|R\|

II. Inevitability of Compression

Since M\_t : 𝓗\_t → R and \|𝓗\_t\| \> \|R\|, the mapping cannot be injective. Therefore there exist:

∃ h₁ ≠ h₂ such that M\_t(h₁) = M\_t(h₂)

Hence: the system must map multiple histories into a single state; compression is unavoidable.

III. Definition of Compression

If there exist h₁ ≠ h₂ such that M\_t(h₁) = M\_t(h₂), the mapping M\_t is said to compress histories;  
no compression ⟺ M\_t is injective.

IV. Equivalence Class Structure

For any r ∈ R, define:

O(r) = { h ∈ 𝓗\_t ∣ M\_t(h) = r }

That is: the system partitions histories into equivalence classes, each state r corresponding to a set of histories O(r).

V. Emergence of Repeated-Hit Structure (v1.1 rectification: formerly "Emergence of Stability")

【Terminological declaration (new in v1.1, key)】 The "stability" spoken of in this section and throughout this chapter is stability in the set-theoretic sense: some state r\* is repeatedly hit infinitely often in the system's actual running, its equivalence class recurring persistently. This is not the same concept as the dynamical stability proven in Form Chapter 1 v2.0 — the latter meaning the sedimentation depth δ converging to the attracting fixed point δ*, which requires condition group H (the existence and convergence of the fixed point of the depth–dispersal competition). This chapter's "repeated hit" sits at the Gap One layer of Form Chapter 1 v2.0's chain (continuous flow → repeated hit) and does not touch Gap Two (repeated hit → the emergence of δ*). In other words: this chapter proves that some state is revisited infinitely often; whether "the sedimentary structure corresponding to that state is dynamically stable" lies outside this chapter's scope. To avoid confusion, this version renames the former "stable structure" as "repeated-hit structure / repeated-hit equivalence class."

Argument (v1.1 quantifier made precise): consider the state sequence the system actually traverses, r₁, r₂, r₃, …, where r\_t = M\_t(h\_t), h\_t being the history the system actually undergoes at moment t (not an arbitrary possible history). This sequence is infinite (by the setup of Section I: input continues to generate new information, t grows without bound, the system responds without cease — hence the state sequence is infinite), and every term takes its value in the finite set R (Section I: \|R\| \< ∞). By the infinite pigeonhole principle (an infinite sequence valued in a finite set must take some value infinitely often), there exists r\* ∈ R such that r\_t = r\* at infinitely many t.

Therefore: there exists r\* such that M\_t(h\_t) = r\* at infinitely many t — and that equivalence class forms a repeated-hit structure.

(Note: what is asserted here is only "infinitely many repeated hits" — a purely combinatorial fact, holding unconditionally. It does not assert that the sedimentary structure corresponding to those hits is dynamically stable — that is the content of Form Chapter 1 v2.0's Gap Two, requiring condition group H.)

VI. Truncation Property

If the system's current decision explicitly depends on the full history h\_t, the size of its processing object grows with t. If the system must complete its updates and responses within finite time, such dependence cannot expand without bound. Therefore the system's current decision can depend only on:

r\_t = M\_t(h\_t)

That is: when history enters the present, it must pass through the mapping and be truncated into a finite state.

VII. Inevitability of Reuse

Since \|R\| \< ∞ and \|𝓗\_t\| grows with time, it is impossible to assign a distinct state to every distinct history. Thus there must exist:

∃ h₁ ≠ h₂ such that M\_t(h₁) = M\_t(h₂) = r

That is: states must be reused; structural reuse is unavoidable.

VIII. Main Conclusion

Under the following conditions holding at once:

1\. The input history grows continuously  
2\. The state space is finite  
3\. The system must respond continuously within finite time

it necessarily follows that: histories are compressed; histories are truncated; states are reused; equivalence class structures are formed; repeated-hit structures emerge (in the set-theoretic sense — see the terminological declaration of Section V).

IX. Uniqueness Conclusion

Suppose there exists a mapping N\_t : 𝓗\_t → R such that h₁ ≠ h₂ ⟹ N\_t(h₁) ≠ N\_t(h₂); then N\_t is injective. But when \|𝓗\_t\| \> \|R\|, no injective mapping can exist; hence the supposition fails.

Therefore: there exists no system that avoids compression (that is, remains injective).

(Remark: what this section proves is "compression is unavoidable" — the impossibility of injection — an equivalent restatement of Section II's conclusion. The formation of repeated-hit structure is a separate matter, proven separately by Section V's infinite pigeonhole, and is not given by this section. The v1.0 text wrote the conclusion here as "there exists no system that can form stable structures without compression," mixing what Section II proves — compression — with what Section V proves — repeated hitting — in one sentence; v1.1 pulls this section's conclusion back to what it actually proves: compression is necessary, without crossing over into the formation of repeated hits.)

X. Minimal Expression

O(r) = { h ∈ 𝓗\_t ∣ M\_t(h) = r }

XI. Interpretation (not part of the formal structure)

O(r) corresponds to the "given"; an object is the recognition of O(r).

XII. Final Conclusion

Under finite state constraints, a system must, through the compression and truncation of history, form repeated-hit equivalence class structures.

XIII. The Junction with Form Chapter 1 v2.0 (new in v1.1)

This chapter and Form Chapter 1 v2.0's Appendix 1 (the Repeated-Hit Theorem) are two occurrences of one and the same pigeonhole argument; hitherto neither cited the other — a disconnection within the system. It is now connected:

First, this chapter is the original source of that pigeonhole argument. What Form Chapter 1 v2.0's Appendix 1 — "finite structural positions receiving a sustained input flow: some position is necessarily hit repeatedly and without bound" — uses to close its Gap One is precisely this chapter's compression–repeated-hit argument of Sections II, V, and VII. Appendix 1 should cite this chapter as source; this chapter declares the relation here.

Second, this chapter's precise position within Form Chapter 1 v2.0's chain. The complete chain is: continuous flow → 〔Gap One: repeated hit〕 → repeated structure → trace accumulation → depth → 〔Gap Two: the emergence of δ*〕 → dynamical stability. What this chapter covers is the Gap One layer — it proves that repeated hitting (the equivalence class being revisited infinitely often) holds unconditionally. This chapter does not touch Gap Two: from "hit infinitely often" to "that position's sedimentation depth converging to δ*, dynamically stable" requires Form Chapter 1 v2.0's Appendix 2 and its condition group H — not this chapter.

Third, the division of the two "stabilities" is thereby fixed: this chapter's stability = repeated hitting at the state level (unconditional; a combinatorial fact); Form Chapter 1 v2.0's stability = the fixed point δ\* at the sedimentary level (conditional; requiring H). The former is the necessary precursor of the latter (no repeated hitting, no sustained deepening), but not sufficient — above repeated hitting, the depth–dispersal competition must further converge before there is dynamical stability. This chapter stops here, and does not cross the line.
