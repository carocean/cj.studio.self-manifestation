# The Mute-Manifest Group

Why these nine Forms must compose the Mute-Manifest Group

Because what they treat is not nine parallel topics but one object — a content passing from the mute state into manifestation — under nine distinct propositions to be proven, yielding tasks that cannot substitute for one another.

The General Form answers what a first manifestation must minimally have. The Mathematical Form answers how these structures are to be precisely represented and run. The Phase-Transition Form answers why the 0→1 is a D/L organizational phase transition. The Source Form answers where the load-bearing structure of the posterior L-phase comes from. The Reachability Form answers whether L can be legitimately reached from a given deeply mute initial state. The Necessity Form answers under what additional conditions L cannot fail to be reached. The Grading Form answers how, once a stable L is established, it develops from 1 to 100. The Determination Form answers whether a candidate system in reality has already satisfied these theoretical conditions. The Construction Form gives the explicit construction by which the L stable-organization construction class reaches construction strength.

The nine Forms share one axis of object, and stand in relations of direct succession, interface delivery, H-propagation, and downstream read-back; no one of them can substitute for the proof task of the others. They must therefore compose one load-bearing group form.

This Master Map only collects the member boundaries, direct successions, interface deliveries, H-propagations, and degrees of answer already borne by the member Forms. It produces no new group-level theoretical proposition.

VII bis. Declaration of Group-Level Form

First — the format of H entries. This group uses an eight-field format for H: number, name, content, form, function, what it does not affect, attribution, current status. The field "what it does not affect" bears a delimiting duty: it records which conclusions still stand independently while that H remains unverified, lest a reader infer from "H pending" that the whole chapter is void. The four fields required by Part Seven of Canon Chapter 1 v1.8 — content, what is needed, what it does not affect, where it falls — correspond here to, respectively, the content field, the attribution field (which records the route of verification and the attribution of responsibility), the field of what it does not affect, and the function field (which records where the load falls).

Second — the regime of declaring relational properties. Under the Mathematical Form clause of Canon Chapter 1 v2.1, wherever a definition sequence contains a relation, its properties must be declared item by item and none assumed by default. The relations of this group's chapters and their properties are as follows.

In listing them, three standings must be distinguished: what the chapter proves; what it requires of instantiation; what it receives from upstream. The three may not be recorded as one.

Mute-Manifest Mathematical Form, ∼\_ε and ∼\_r^org: equivalence relations, the three properties being requirements upon instantiation — that chapter does not itself prescribe the content of "equivalent," and the three properties therefore do not follow from its definitions.

Mute-Manifest Phase-Transition Form, ∼\_land^r: an equivalence relation, the three properties being requirements upon instantiation, as above. ⪯\_gen^r: proven by that chapter — transitivity and reflexivity hold, antisymmetry does not (the four checking conditions contain no requirement of unidirectionality and so admit cycles), and it is therefore a preorder. ∼\_B^r: should a particular model introduce it, it must be an equivalence relation; that chapter does not presuppose it.

Mute-Manifest Source Form, ⪯\_H^r and its instances ⪯\_src^r and ⪯\_{H\_actual}^r: proven by that chapter — transitivity and antisymmetry hold, the definition explicitly containing the temporal partial order τ\_H and therefore admitting no cycle; reflexivity depends on the particular model's convention concerning paths, which that chapter does not decide on its behalf. ∼\_hist^r and ∼\_src^r: equivalence relations, the three properties being requirements upon instantiation.

Mute-Manifest Reachability Form, ↝\_π^r: proven by that chapter — transitivity holds within one permitted policy and for finitely many concatenations, its conditions being more than those of the concatenation lemma (three further conditions are required: manner of generation, finite duration, and non-use of forbidden inputs); antisymmetry does not hold, and it is therefore a preorder; reflexivity depends on convention, on which that chapter does not rely.

Mute-Manifest Grading Form, ≼\_j: a requirement upon the comparison protocol — every (𝒟\_j, ≼\_j) must satisfy reflexivity, antisymmetry, and transitivity, which that chapter does not prove on behalf of any particular protocol. ≼\_𝔊: proven by that chapter — composed as the product of the ≼\_j, and since every dimension is a partial order the product relation forms a partial order on the portrait space. ∼\_𝔊: proven by that chapter — defined by equality of portraits, so the three properties follow directly.

Mute-Manifest Determination Form, ∼\_ε: received from the Mathematical Form; that chapter neither redefines it nor alters its content; should the declared ∼\_ε fail the three properties, the determinations that chapter grounds upon quotient classes output ⊥.

Mute-Manifest General Form and Mute-Manifest Necessity Form: no binary relation of their own.

Three directional relations must be distinguished: ⪯\_gen^r and ↝\_π^r are of one kind (generative reachability, admitting cycles, preorders); ⪯\_H^r is not (it explicitly contains a temporal partial order, admits no cycle, and antisymmetry holds). The three are alike in notation and unlike in property; cross-chapter citation may not infer one from another.

Third — the classification of load. This group establishes no separate section for the three-way classification of load, using instead each chapter's field of "degree of answer." That field records item by item: what is proven without dependence on any H; what rests on some numbered H; what is unanswered and handed downstream. Its correspondence with the Canon's three-way classification is: the proven items are the chapter's own forced-out items together with its closed inherited items; the H-dependent items are the H-conditioned items; the unanswered-and-handed-downstream items are the interface deliveries.

VIII. Principles of Group-Level Propagation

A change in a member's H status does not of itself alter the group version; only a change in the member table, the formal interface diagram, the H-propagation table, the adjudication of the eight questions, or a group-level declaration itself raises the group version.

An H passing from pending or open to verified does not of itself upgrade all downstream chapters; each responsible chapter must review afresh according to its own premises.

When an H is withdrawn or refuted, the Master Map only locates the affected paths; it does not adjudicate in place of the member Forms.

When an upstream definition, theorem, H, or permitted domain is upgraded, downstream chapters check only the items actually cited; nothing propagates automatically on grounds of adjacency of chapter names.

A determination protocol may not flow back into the definitions of the General Form, the Phase-Transition Form, or the Grading Form.

Grading may not flow 1→100 back into 0→1.

Source closure may not be exchanged for reachability or necessity.

Reachability may not be exchanged for necessity or realization.

IX. The Group-Level Answer

The Mute-Manifest Group has divided "from mute to manifest" into nine proof problems that cannot substitute for one another:

necessary constitution; the formal system; the structure of phase transition; the source of formation; legitimate reachability; conditional necessity; the grading of the manifest phase; determination in reality; and explicit construction.

By this it becomes possible to distinguish strictly:

what must minimally be there, from how it is precisely represented;  
that it is a phase transition, from that a path exists;  
that a path exists, from that it cannot fail to occur;  
that a first manifestation has occurred, from how far it has been manifested;  
the source of formation, from present reachability;  
that it holds in theory, from that the evidence in reality suffices to adjudicate.

X. The Boundary of the Master Map

This Master Map proves no new group-level theoretical proposition.

The following remain borne by the member Forms alone:

the strict completeness of P/M;  
whether General Form-H1 holds;  
the particular organizational criteria and criticality of the D/L phase transition;  
whether a source history is closed;  
whether a particular initial state is reachable;  
whether particular conditions suffice to constitute necessity;  
how a given L organization is to be graded;  
whether a real Case\_r is to be judged 1, 0, or ⊥.

XI. Breach Conditions of the Master Map

Should any of the following occur, this Master Map fails:

the member table disagrees with the actual formal members;  
the task-arising diagram is taken for the formal dependency diagram;  
the formal interface diagram registers a premise absent from a member's own text;  
an actual load-bearing H or downstream propagation is omitted;  
the Master Map alters, on its own authority, a member's H number, content, status, or attribution of responsibility;  
source is written as reachability;  
reachability is written as necessity;  
theoretical necessity is written as having actually occurred;  
1→100 is written as 0→1;  
GradeDecision or TransitionHistoryDecision is folded into the minimal positive adjudication of Dec\_SM;  
a result of determination in reality is written back as a theoretical definition.
