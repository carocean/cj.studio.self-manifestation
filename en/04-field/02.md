Definition 4: Lock  
Notation: Lock(𝒟)  
denotes the persistent historical bias imposed upon present convergence.  
Lock is not memory storage. It is: the continued structural constraint that historical accumulation exerts upon present convergence.  
Note: Lock differs from Ω\_W (Stable Recurrence Capacity) of Field Chapter 1. Ω\_W is the historical qualification predicate that a path has exhibited stable recurrence within a declared window W (taking 0 or 1, derived from the recurrence record, recurrence events in each sub-interval, and connection integrity; Field Chapter 1 Definition 12), and belongs to the path level; Lock is the overall bias that historical layering imposes on the direction of present convergence within a Domain, and is a degree quantity at the Domain level. Note: Lock and bias. Whether Lock holds rests on Continuation Bias B (dependence on sedimentation differences) of Form Chapter 20 v1.2: B\_m(T\_𝒟) is determined under a comparison task T\_𝒟 declared within the Domain; if B does not hold, Lock = 0, and if B is undefined, Lock is undefined (see the Lock Formation Equation). Its magnitude is given by the illustrative equation (historical layering × sustaining flow) and is not an effect size of B; Lock is therefore not called a degree of B. Logical direction: B\_m(T\_𝒟) false ⇒ Lock = 0; hence Lock \> 0 ⇒ B\_m(T\_𝒟) true. The converse fails: when B is true but HistLayer or MaintainFlow is 0, Lock = 0, so Lock = 0 does not imply that B is false. Witness (Form Chapter 20): when the deep route is blocked, sedimentation differences exist and HistLayer and MaintainFlow may both be positive, yet B does not hold, and then Lock = 0. Nor does Lock \> 0 imply Structural Bias: Structural Bias requires (4a) and (4b) of Field Chapter 1 Equation 11 over a long window. The functional correspondence between Lock and Continuation Binding S stated in the note to Definition 10 of Form Chapter 20 v1.2 has not been re-verified under the present definition of Lock; this chapter does not carry it over and registers it as to be proved.

As Pulses continuously InFlow: local continuation potential increases.

When local continuation potential remains persistently higher than surrounding regions: subsequent convergence increasingly returns along prior continuations.

Thus: stable continuation emerges.

Note: Continuation potential here is Continuation Potential κ, a derived quantity (Field Chapter 1 Definition 11, Equation 3a): under identical comparison conditions κ does not reverse the δ-order. ## Section 4 Historical Accumulation and Lock

History within the Sedimentation-Field does not completely disappear.

New Trace-Effects do not overwrite prior Trace-Effects. They accumulate upon them.

Definition 11: Historical Layering  
Notation: HistLayer(𝒟) (Domain level; field-wide, HistLayer(F))  
denotes the layered historical structure within the Sedimentation-Field.

Historical Layering Equation (illustrative model):  
HistLayerₜ₊₁ ＝ max(0, HistLayerₜ + τₜ − DecayRate(𝒟)ₜ × HistLayerₜ)  
Note: DecayRate(𝒟) is a dispersal rate (a dimensionless ratio); multiplied by the current Historical Layering quantity, it yields the actual dispersal amount at the current moment. The max(0,…) truncation ensures that Historical Layering cannot become negative—under extreme dispersal conditions, Historical Layering approaches zero rather than going negative, consistent with the physical constraints of the Sedimentation-Field.

Note: This equation, like Field Chapter 1 Equation 2 (δₜ₊₁=δₜ+Dτₜ−Eλₜ), is additive bookkeeping: τₜ here denotes the Domain-level deepening produced by Trace-Effects in the current step (the Domain-wide aggregate of Dτ in Equation 2), and DecayRate×HistLayer corresponds to Eλ; that shallowing is proportional to the current layering is an assumption of the illustrative model. The method of aggregating HistLayer from the δ of individual Structural Positions is not given in this chapter and must be declared by the specific field.

Proposition 1 (Bounds on Historical Layering): Let HistLayer₀ ≥ 0 and τₜ ≥ 0 for all t. (a) If r ≤ DecayRateₜ ≤ 1 (r \> 0) and τₜ ≤ τ\_max for all t, then HistLayerₜ ≤ M for all t, where M = max(HistLayer₀, τ\_max/r). (b) If DecayRateₜ ≤ d \< 1 for all t, then HistLayerₜ \> 0 implies HistLayerₜ₊₁ \> 0: historical layering is not exhausted in finitely many steps. (c) If DecayRateₜ = 0 and τₜ ≥ τ\_min \> 0 for all t, then HistLayerₜ ≥ HistLayer₀ + t·τ\_min, which is unbounded. (d) If τₜ = 0 and 0 ≤ DecayRateₜ \< 1 for all t, then HistLayerₜ = HistLayer₀ · Πₛ₌₀^{t−1}(1 − DecayRateₛ); when HistLayer₀ \> 0, HistLayerₜ → 0 if and only if Σₛ DecayRateₛ = ∞. Special cases: with DecayRate constantly d ∈ (0,1), HistLayerₜ = (1 − d)^t · HistLayer₀, tending to zero geometrically; with DecayRate constantly 0, HistLayer stays unchanged.

Proof: (a) By induction on t. HistLayer₀ ≤ M. Suppose 0 ≤ HistLayerₜ ≤ M. Since r ≤ DecayRateₜ ≤ 1, 0 ≤ (1 − DecayRateₜ)·HistLayerₜ ≤ (1 − r)·M, so HistLayerₜ₊₁ ≤ (1 − r)·M + τ\_max ≤ (1 − r)·M + r·M = M (the last step uses τ\_max ≤ r·M). (b) (1 − DecayRateₜ)·HistLayerₜ ≥ (1 − d)·HistLayerₜ \> 0 and τₜ ≥ 0, so the truncation is inactive and HistLayerₜ₊₁ \> 0. (c) The truncation is inactive, and each step adds at least τ\_min. (d) With τₜ = 0 and DecayRateₜ \< 1 the truncation is inactive, and the product form follows from the recursion. If Σ DecayRateₛ = ∞, then since 1 − x ≤ e^{−x}, the product is ≤ exp(−Σ DecayRateₛ) → 0. If Σ DecayRateₛ \< ∞, then DecayRateₛ → 0, so only finitely many s have DecayRateₛ \> 1/2; the remaining terms satisfy 0 ≤ x ≤ 1/2, where 1 − x ≥ e^{−2x}, so their product is ≥ exp(−2Σ DecayRateₛ) \> 0; the finitely many other factors are each \> 0, so the product has a positive lower bound and does not tend to zero. ∎

Note: The opening sentence of this section, “History within the Sedimentation-Field does not completely disappear,” corresponds in this illustrative equation only to Proposition 1(b): with DecayRate ≤ d \< 1, layering is not exhausted in finitely many steps. It does not mean that HistLayer fails to tend to zero. Without input: with DecayRate constantly d ∈ (0,1), it tends to zero geometrically; under a general varying dispersal rate, it tends to zero if and only if the cumulative dispersal diverges (Proposition 1(d)); with DecayRate constantly 0, it stays unchanged. The impossibility of complete recovery is a separate matter; see Field Chapter 1 Theorem 15.

Lock Formation Equation (illustrative model):  
Lock(𝒟) ＝ χ\_B(𝒟) × c\_L × HistLayer(𝒟) × MaintainFlow(𝒟) / (1 + DecayRate(𝒟))

where: χ\_B(𝒟) = 1 when B\_m(T\_𝒟) is true and χ\_B(𝒟) = 0 when it is false; when B\_m(T\_𝒟) is undefined, Lock(𝒟) is undefined (recorded as ⊥). T\_𝒟 is the comparison task declared within the Domain (comparison objects, levelling value m, reading criterion, and window, following Section 5 of Form Chapter 20 v1.2). c\_L \> 0 is a declared fixed proportionality constant.  
Note: The denominator uses 1+DecayRate(𝒟) rather than DecayRate(𝒟) alone, which only removes the singularity at DecayRate→0 and does not by itself imply boundedness. Corollary: under the conditions of Proposition 1(a), if MaintainFlow ≤ MF\_max, then Lock ≤ c\_L · M · MF\_max (since χ\_B ≤ 1 and 1 + DecayRate ≥ 1). If DecayRate = 0 while input persists (Proposition 1(c)), HistLayer grows without bound; when χ\_B = 1 and MaintainFlow has a positive lower bound, Lock is likewise unbounded.

Proposition 2 (Condition for Lock Decline): Suppose χ\_B = 1 at both steps t and t+1, Lockₜ \> 0, τₜ \< DecayRateₜ × HistLayerₜ, MaintainFlowₜ₊₁ ≤ MaintainFlowₜ, and DecayRateₜ₊₁ ≥ DecayRateₜ. Then Lockₜ₊₁ \< Lockₜ.

Proof: Lockₜ \> 0 implies HistLayerₜ \> 0 and aₜ = MaintainFlowₜ / (1 + DecayRateₜ) \> 0. Since τₜ \< DecayRateₜ · HistLayerₜ, HistLayerₜ + τₜ − DecayRateₜ · HistLayerₜ \< HistLayerₜ; as HistLayerₜ \> 0, after truncation still HistLayerₜ₊₁ \< HistLayerₜ. By assumption, aₜ₊₁ ≤ aₜ. Hence Lockₜ₊₁ = c\_L · HistLayerₜ₊₁ · aₜ₊₁ ≤ c\_L · HistLayerₜ₊₁ · aₜ \< c\_L · HistLayerₜ · aₜ = Lockₜ. ∎

Note: When χ\_B changes from 1 to 0, Lock drops directly to 0. When historical input suffices to offset dispersal (τₜ ≥ DecayRateₜ × HistLayerₜ), Lock need not decline even if MaintainFlow is very low. Example: c\_L = 1, HistLayer = 20, DecayRate = 0.5, τ = 10, MaintainFlow = 1; historical layering stays at 20 every step and Lock is unchanged, while MaintainFlow \< DecayRate × HistLayer holds throughout.

Law 3: Historical Lock Law  
The deeper the historical layering: the more current convergence tends to return along prior Familiar Routes.  
Note: This law is the expansion, at the level of Domain dynamics, of Outer Chapter 3 (Number Theory): “what is deep first receives the flow”; the original text gives only the direction. Its provable content takes the precedence criterion: “deeper historical layering” here means that the prior Familiar Route being compared has the greater Sedimentation-Degree; under the comparison condition C₌, the deeper satisfies the outflow condition no later than the shallower, and under C₌ and P₁₇ strictly first (Field Chapter 1 Law 5, Theorem 17). Familiar Routes follow Field Chapter 1 Definition 13 (existing deep sediment, and frequently travelled within a declared window). A larger amount or share of convergence does not follow from this and requires separate proof. κ is a derived quantity and serves as no separate intermediary.

Law 4: Deep-Lock Inertia Law  
The stronger the Lock: the more resistant the Domain becomes to structural change.

Note: Outer Chapter 16 (“deep sediment is hard to change, shallow is easy to move”) attributes difficulty of change to deep sediment (Sedimentation-Degree); this law restates it at the Domain level in terms of Lock, as a model-level declaration.

Law 5: Insufficient-Supply Lock Regression Law  
If: χ\_B is 1 at two consecutive steps, Lock \> 0 at the current step, historical input is insufficient to offset dispersal (τ \< DecayRate × HistLayer), MaintainFlow does not rise, and DecayRate does not fall, then: Lock declines (Proposition 2). When χ\_B changes from 0 to 1, Lock may rise from 0; this lies outside the scope of this law.

Memory Bias: the stable recurrence tendency produced by long-term Lock.

## Section 5 MainCore and Domain Governance

During early formation, multiple convergence centers may coexist.

Over time, certain convergence centers strengthen continuously. Thus: a MainCore emerges.

Definition 12: MainCore  
Notation: MainCore(𝒟)  
denotes the core structure governing long-term convergence within a Domain.  
The MainCore is not a subject. It is: the long-term center of convergent governance.

MainCore Formation Equation (illustrative model):  
MainCoreᵢ(𝒟) ＝ MaintainFlowᵢ(𝒟) × Lockᵢ(𝒟) / Σⱼ(MaintainFlowⱼ(𝒟) × Lockⱼ(𝒟))  
Note: Lockᵢ(𝒟) and MaintainFlowᵢ(𝒟) denote the parts borne by the i-th convergence center (illustrative notation). Domain of definition: the denominator Σⱼ(MaintainFlowⱼ(𝒟) × Lockⱼ(𝒟)) must be finite and strictly positive; otherwise MainCore is undefined (⊥). The normalized form reflects multi-core competition—the MainCore strength of each convergence center within a Domain is determined by its share relative to all convergence centers. If only one convergence center exists and its weight is positive, MainCore = 1 and governance is complete. If multiple cores coexist in balance, each core’s weight is similar and MainCore governance weakens.

Law 6: MainCore Governance Law  
The more stable the MainCore: the more unified convergence within the Domain becomes.

Law 7: Hierarchical Multi-Core Law  
Stable multi-core structures do not necessarily produce Domain fracture.  
If: the MainCore successfully governs subsidiary cores, then: hierarchical Domains emerge.

Law 8: MainCore Collapse Law  
If: Sync ↓ and Lock ↓ simultaneously, then: MainCore begins losing convergence governance.

Personality: the stable convergence-flow structure formed under MainCore governance through long-term high Lock and high cross-domain Sync.

## Section 6 Domain Bridges and Synchronization

Domains are not completely isolated.

Through prolonged cross-domain continuation: Domain Bridges emerge.

Once Domain Bridges form: multiple Domains begin synchronizing.

Definition 13: Domain Bridge  
Notation: Bridge(𝒟₁⇝𝒟₂)  
denotes a stable cross-domain continuation structure.

Bridge Equation (illustrative model):  
BridgeStr(𝒟₁⇝𝒟₂) ∝ CohereFlow\_N(𝒟₁) × √(Lock(𝒟₁) × Lock(𝒟₂))  
Note: The geometric mean of both Locks makes Bridge strength jointly constrained by the historical stability of both Domains: if either Lock is 0, BridgeStr = 0; if 𝒟₁ has no historical layering, stable bridging cannot form even under strong CohereFlow. CohereFlow\_N(𝒟₁) = CohereFlow(𝒟₁) / Rcap(F) (requiring Rcap(F) \> 0) is a dimensionless reading (dimensional convention U); BridgeStr therefore remains dimensionless and compatible with the dimensionless FragmentRate in the Synchronization Equation.

Synchronization Equation (illustrative model):  
Sync(𝒟₁,𝒟₂) ∝ BridgeStr(𝒟₁⇝𝒟₂) / (1 + FragmentRate(𝒟₁,𝒟₂))  
Note: The denominator uses 1+FragmentRate, which only removes the singularity at FragmentRate→0. When BridgeStr is bounded, Sync is bounded; if BridgeStr additionally tends to a finite value N and FragmentRate tends to 0, then Sync tends to the proportionality constant times N (likewise when the other inputs are held fixed and only FragmentRate tends to zero). When the numerator is bounded but oscillates, Sync need not converge. Adding 1 to the denominator does not by itself imply global boundedness.

Fragmentation Source Equation (illustrative model):  
FragmentRate(𝒟) ∝ PhaseConflict + SyncCollapse + LockDecay  
where: PhaseConflict denotes phase-difference-induced instability between Domains; SyncCollapse denotes the breakdown of existing cross-domain synchronization; LockDecay denotes the weakening of Lock under low MaintainFlow or high DecayRate. FragmentRate(𝒟) is a Domain-level quantity; FragmentRate(𝒟₁,𝒟₂) in the Synchronization Equation is a pairwise quantity; each is declared separately.

Law 9: Strong Bridge Synchronization Law  
The more stable the Domain Bridge: the more sustainable cross-domain synchronization becomes.

Association: stable cross-domain synchronized continuation.

## Section 7 Temporal Scales and Phase

Different Domains do not continue at identical rates. Thus: Domains possess different temporal scales.

Definition 14: Temporal Scale  
Notation: TimeScale(𝒟)  
denotes the time required for a Domain to complete one stable convergence cycle.

Temporal Scale Equation (illustrative model):  
TimeScale(𝒟) ∝ Lock(𝒟) / MaintainFlow(𝒟)  
Note: When MaintainFlow = 0, this equation is undefined (⊥). Taken alone: with Lock fixed, the stronger the MaintainFlow, the smaller the temporal scale; with MaintainFlow fixed, the stronger the Lock, the larger the temporal scale. Combined with the Lock Formation Equation (MaintainFlow \> 0), it gives TimeScale ∝ χ\_B × c\_L × HistLayer / (1 + DecayRate), and MaintainFlow cancels: the sustaining effect passed through Lock offsets the sustaining effect in the denominator. This chapter therefore does not use this equation to assert that “the stronger the MaintainFlow, the faster the Domain updates.” In the combined equation (χ\_B = 1), the temporal scale increases with HistLayer and decreases as DecayRate increases.

Notation: Phase(𝒟)

Note: When combined with the Lock Formation Equation (see the note to the Temporal Scale Equation), this law holds only with MaintainFlow fixed.

## Section 8 Conflict and Phase Transition

Different historical accumulations may contain mutually exclusive convergence tendencies. Thus: historical conflict emerges.

Definition 16: Historical Conflict  
Notation: Conflict(Hist₁,Hist₂)  
denotes the presence of mutually exclusive convergence between different historical accumulations.

## Section 10 Resources and Energetic Cost

Stable synchronization requires sustained resource support.

Definition 19: Resource Capacity  
Notation: Rcap(F)  
(Not denoted Res(F), to avoid conflict with the Resolution symbol Res(F) in Field Chapter 1.)  
denotes the total effective convergence capacity maintainable within a Sedimentation-Field.  
Note: Rcap(F) differs from Field Capacity Cap(F)=Vol(F)×Res(F) in Field Chapter 1. The two have different dimensions (one counts Domains, the other counts convergent flow), and this chapter establishes no quantitative relation between them; for the release condition when Cap(F) is exceeded, see Field Chapter 1 Constraint 20.

## Section 12 Recognition-Realm Continuity

The continuity of a Recognition-Realm is not naturally given by the world. It is a generative result: it emerges when large-scale Sync suppresses FragmentRate across the Domain network.

Continuity Equation (illustrative model):  
Continuity(R) = Sync\_N(R) × Lock\_N(R) / (1 + FragmentRate(R))  
Note: Sync\_N = Sync / S\_ref and Lock\_N = Lock / L\_ref are normalized readings; the raw quantities Sync and Lock are not substituted directly into this equation. S\_ref, L\_ref \> 0 are declared common scales, which must be no smaller than the suprema of Sync and Lock over the range considered (for the bound on Lock, see the corollary in the note to the Lock Formation Equation; where Lock is unbounded, it cannot be normalized and Continuity is undefined). FragmentRate ≥ 0. Then Continuity(R) ∈ \[0,1\], and Continuity(R) = 1 if and only if Sync\_N = Lock\_N = 1 and FragmentRate = 0; this chapter makes no claim on whether this ideal is attainable.

Law 20: High-Sync Stable-Reality Law  
Persistent Sync stabilizes Recognition-Realm continuity.

Law 21: High-Fragmentation Reality-Shattering Law  
As FragmentRate persistently rises: Recognition-Realm continuity fractures.

Dreams, hallucinations, and ruptures in reality continuity are all forms of: cross-domain synchronization instability.
