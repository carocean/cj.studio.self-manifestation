# Field Dynamic Equations

Field dynamics consists of three parts:

\- Coordination Compression Evolution  
\- Continuation Cost Evolution  
\- Minimum Coordination Constraint

Their total relation is as follows.

I. Coordination Compression Evolution Equation

∂Ω\_c(q,t)/∂t = αΩ\_c(q,t)/𝒵(q,t) − μ𝒵(q,t)/Ω\_c(q,t)

Meaning:

The rate of change of a path’s Coordination Compression Degree depends on the competition between low-cost recurrence gain and high-cost dispersal loss.

Under the same reference conditions, the higher Ω\_c: the more easily subsequent continuation recurs stably along the path.  
The higher 𝒵: the more easily stable recurrence fails.

Therefore: in this chapter’s model, change in Coordination Compression is competition between low-cost recurrence and high-cost dispersal.

This is: the main evolution equation of field dynamics. (Category: illustrative model; see Section 5.)

II. Continuation Cost Evolution Equation

∂𝒵(q,t)/∂t = η𝒵(q,t)/Ω\_c(q,t) − νΩ\_c(q,t)

Meaning:

Continuation cost is not a static background. High-cost instability further increases cost; Coordination Compression formation continuously reduces cost.

Therefore: continuation cost itself is in dynamic change.

This is: the coordination evolution equation of field dynamics. (Category: illustrative model; see Section 6.)

III. Minimum Cost Constraint

𝒵(q,t) ≥ 𝒵\_min

Meaning:

Every stable continuation has a minimum coordination requirement; continuation cost therefore cannot decrease without limit, or stable structure will lose its boundary.

This is: the minimum coordination constraint of field dynamics.

Note: 𝒵\_min is an external constraint, not embedded in the evolution equations. It is applied as follows: when 𝒵(q,t) = 𝒵\_min and the right-hand side of the Continuation Cost Evolution Equation is negative, take ∂𝒵(q,t)/∂t = 0 (truncation). The cost-reduction term νΩ\_c in the illustrative equation does not itself saturate; that “even when Coordination Compression continues strengthening, the minimum coordination cost (such as the basic costs of discrimination and synchronization) cannot be compressed to zero” is expressed by this external constraint, not derived from the equation.

Note: The main evolution equation has a singularity at Ω\_c=0 (in the μ𝒵/Ω\_c term) and at 𝒵=0 (in the αΩ\_c/𝒵 term). The 𝒵=0 singularity is excluded by the 𝒵\_min constraint. Ω\_c=0 cannot be excluded by the applicability precondition: as Ω\_c becomes small the loss term μ𝒵/Ω\_c diverges, and in the region described in Section 8, Ω\_c falls to 0 in finite time (Theorem 6). This chapter takes Ω\_c \> 0 as its applicability precondition; when Ω\_c reaches 0, this is read as the path’s Coordination Compression having been exhausted, the path leaves this chapter’s domain of applicability, and the model fails there and is not continued. The equation is defined on the domain Ω\_c \> 0 and 𝒵 ≥ 𝒵\_min \> 0.

where:

Ω\_c(q,t): the Coordination Compression Degree of path q at time t (see symbol note in Section 2);

𝒵(q,t): the Continuation Cost of path q;

𝒵\_min: the minimum coordination cost required for stable continuation;

α: low-cost recurrence gain coefficient (positive real number; see Section 5);

μ: high-cost dispersal loss coefficient (positive real number; see Section 5);

η: instability-driven cost growth coefficient (positive real number; see Section 6);

ν: compression-driven cost reduction coefficient (positive real number; see Section 6).

These three equations establish that:

Field dynamics as treated in this chapter is not “structure moving through space.” It is: the persistent competition between Coordination Compression and continuation cost; the evolution of Sedimentation-Degree is given separately by Field Chapter 1, Equation 2.

Coordination Compression produces a cost-reduction effect (νΩ\_c); the net change of continuation cost also depends on the cost-growth term, and 𝒵 can still rise while Ω\_c rises. High cost accelerates the dispersal of Coordination Compression.

Therefore: at the coordination level, structure continuously changes between low-cost recurrence and high-cost dispersal.

1\. Purpose

The preceding three chapters have established how the Sedimentation-Field forms stable structures: Groove-Lines, Familiar Routes, Domains, Co-Nuclei, and Closures.

But: once formed, structure does not remain permanently still.

Familiar Routes both reinforce and shallow. Closures both sustain and break down. Co-Nuclei both strengthen and lose flow. The present both continues and fractures.

Therefore: this chapter no longer asks how structure forms. It asks why structure, once formed, continues to change. This chapter treats only the coordination level of that change: change in Coordination Compression and continuation cost.

This is: field dynamics.

1\. Dynamics and Number

Number Theory has established:

Mathematical principles act upon the already-formed. Before differentiation, nothing can be counted. Before stabilization, nothing can be measured.

Therefore: the field dynamic equations do not imply that the Sedimentation-Field is naturally countable, differentiable, or continuous prior to generation. They only express: after stable sedimentation, a compressed measure of change in continuation relations.

Therefore: Ω\_c and 𝒵 are not a priori ontological quantities. They are measurable expressions of continuation relations after stable structure has formed.

1\. Coordination Compression

Field Mathematics has established: the deeper receives the flow first (Law 5: under the comparison conditions C₌, the deeper satisfies the outflow condition no later than the shallower; under C₌ and P₁₇, the deeper satisfies it strictly first; see Theorem 17).

Depth is Sedimentation-Degree, borne by the hosting mechanism (Field Chapter 1, Definition 9). This chapter treats a further matter: when stable recurrence is accompanied by the sedimentation of calibration parameters (Inner Chapter 16), besides deepening it pre-organizes coordination relations and compresses future coordination cost under the same reference conditions. This effect runs alongside depth; it does not replace depth, nor does it change the determination that the deeper receives the flow first.

The more mature the path, the more easily subsequent continuation completes tuning, synchronization, discrimination, and return. Therefore: new continuation need not complete all coordination from scratch; stable recurrence cost decreases.

Thus: under the same reference conditions, the more mature a path, the lower the coordination cost required for subsequent continuation to sustain stable recurrence on that path.

Therefore: Ω\_c denotes neither geometric depth nor Sedimentation-Degree. It denotes: the degree to which historical recurrence compresses future coordination cost.

This is: Coordination Compression Degree.

The formation mechanism of Coordination Compression is: the sedimentation of historical tuning (Inner Chapter 16).

When stable recurrence is accompanied by the sedimentation of calibration parameters (the case of completed alignment described in Inner Chapter 16), it does not merely mean continuation re-entering the same structure; it also means: part of the coordination relation has already been pre-organized by history. Therefore: when subsequent continuation enters that structure, it need not complete all discrimination, tuning, synchronization, and return coordination from scratch. Thus: under the same reference conditions, future coordination cost is compressed. Whether recurrence in general has this gain is not asserted in this chapter; it is listed as an assumption of the illustrative model (Section 5).

Therefore: in the case above, tuning is not a momentary action; calibration parameters sediment with stable recurrence and lower the Familiar Route’s coordination cost under the same reference conditions. The Familiar Route itself is determined according to Field Chapter 1, Definition 13 (existing deep sedimentation and a frequent-travel record within the declared window), not by Ω\_c.

Therefore: one route by which history influences the future is: the tuning completed in the past is still pre-compressing the coordination cost of future continuation. Another route is Sedimentation-Degree: the deeper receives the flow first (Field Chapter 1, Law 5).

Theorem 1: Coordination Compression Theorem (by definition)

Under the same reference conditions, the higher a path’s Coordination Compression Degree, the lower the cost of stable recurrence for subsequent continuation.

Note: Ω\_c is the degree to which coordination cost has been compressed under the same reference conditions; this theorem directly restates that definition and is not separately proved. It is not an ordering theorem for the freely evolving 𝒵: current costs 𝒵 at different times or on different paths must be compared directly.

But: recurrence does not strengthen without limit. Because: stable continuation always requires discrimination, synchronization, tuning, and return consistency—and these forms of coordination cannot be completed by a single path alone.

The true source of coordination pressure is multi-continuation competition. The more complex the path, the denser the convergence, and the more overlapping the returns—the higher the discrimination pressure, synchronization pressure, and tuning pressure. Thus: stable coordination cost continuously increases.

Definition: 𝒵(q,t) denotes the coordination pressure required for stable recurrence of path q. This is: Continuation Cost.

1\. Setting of the Main Dynamic Equation

The main evolution equation given in this section is an illustrative model, not a theorem derived from the preceding sections. This chapter requires only the following directions (model assumptions):

M1: the low-cost recurrence gain is non-decreasing in Ω\_c and non-increasing in 𝒵;  
M2: the high-cost dispersal loss is non-decreasing in 𝒵 and non-increasing in Ω\_c.

An illustrative form satisfying M1 and M2 is adopted below. The heuristic account of the setting is as follows:

Illustrative-model assumption: stable recurrence produces a coordination-compression gain effect; a net increase of Ω\_c requires the gain to exceed the loss (Theorem 3), and when the loss exceeds the gain Ω\_c decreases net (Theorem 6). Inner Chapter 16 gives only the case where recurrence is accompanied by the sedimentation of calibration parameters; whether recurrence in general has this gain is an assumption of this model. With increased Compression, subsequent continuation recurs more easily; thus the low-cost recurrence gain strengthens with Ω\_c. But recurrence still requires coordination, and coordination entails continuation cost 𝒵; therefore the recurrence gain is limited by 𝒵.

Therefore: the illustrative form takes the low-cost recurrence gain as αΩ\_c(q,t)/𝒵(q,t).

Simultaneously: higher continuation cost makes stable recurrence more likely to fail; lower Compression makes structure harder to sustain. Therefore the illustrative form takes the high-cost dispersal loss as μ𝒵(q,t)/Ω\_c(q,t).

Here 𝒵(q,t)/Ω\_c(q,t) is an illustrative expression of the failure tendency of stable recurrence: as 𝒵→∞ (extreme high cost), the ratio tends to infinity and instability accelerates; as Ω\_c→0 (extreme low compression), the denominator approaches zero, likewise driving the ratio to infinity—corresponding to the condition where coordination cost has not yet accumulated and structure cannot be sustained.

Therefore: in the illustrative model, the change in path Coordination Compression satisfies:

∂Ω\_c(q,t)/∂t = αΩ\_c(q,t)/𝒵(q,t) − μ𝒵(q,t)/Ω\_c(q,t)

Other functional forms satisfying M1 and M2 are also admissible; conclusions below that use only M1 and M2 do not depend on this illustrative form; where the illustrative form is used, this is stated.

Dimensional Note:

To ensure dimensional consistency on both sides of the equation, we take Ω\_c and 𝒵 to be dimensionless relative quantities (i.e., ratios relative to their respective reference baseline values). Then:

Left side: \[t⁻¹\] (rate of change)  
Right side, first term: α × \[Ω\_c/𝒵\] = α × \[dimensionless\], so α has dimension \[t⁻¹\]  
Right side, second term: μ × \[𝒵/Ω\_c\] = μ × \[dimensionless\], so μ has dimension \[t⁻¹\]

Both terms therefore have dimension \[t⁻¹\], consistent with the left side.

Coefficient Note: α \> 0 (low-cost recurrence gain coefficient), μ \> 0 (high-cost dispersal loss coefficient). Both are positive real-number constants, taken as time-invariant during the stable sedimentation phase of a given field. Larger α indicates stronger gain of historical recurrence on subsequent continuation; larger μ indicates more rapid acceleration of the dispersal of Coordination Compression by high cost.

Theorem 3: Coordination Compression Dynamic Theorem (directly from the model)

If, throughout some interval, the low-cost recurrence gain exceeds the high-cost dispersal loss, Coordination Compression continuously strengthens during that interval.

Proof: by the main evolution equation, ∂Ω\_c/∂t \> 0 there. ∎

1\. Continuation Cost Evolution

But: continuation cost 𝒵 is not a fixed background.

Coordination Compression reduces continuation cost. The more mature a path, the more stable its tuning pre-alignment, discrimination pre-contraction, and return pre-synchronization—and the lower the coordination cost for subsequent continuation. Therefore Ω\_c exerts a cost-reduction effect on 𝒵; the net change of 𝒵 also depends on the cost-growth term.

Conversely: higher continuation cost makes stable coordination more difficult; multi-continuation competition intensifies; instability further increases. Therefore 𝒵 continues to rise.

This chapter requires only the following direction (model assumption):

M3: the cost-growth term is non-decreasing in 𝒵 and non-increasing in Ω\_c; the cost-reduction term is non-decreasing in Ω\_c.

The illustrative form takes:

∂𝒵(q,t)/∂t = η𝒵(q,t)/Ω\_c(q,t) − νΩ\_c(q,t)

where: η𝒵/Ω\_c denotes instability-driven cost growth; νΩ\_c denotes compression-driven cost reduction.

Dimensional Note:

Applying the dimensionless convention from Section 5 (Ω\_c and 𝒵 are both dimensionless relative quantities):

Left side: \[t⁻¹\]  
Right side, first term: η × \[𝒵/Ω\_c\] = η × \[dimensionless\], so η has dimension \[t⁻¹\]  
Right side, second term: ν × \[Ω\_c\] = ν × \[dimensionless\], so ν has dimension \[t⁻¹\]

Both terms have dimension \[t⁻¹\], consistent with the left side.

Coefficient Note: η \> 0 (instability-driven cost growth coefficient), ν \> 0 (compression-driven cost reduction coefficient). Both are positive real-number constants. Larger η means high-cost paths self-amplify more aggressively; larger ν means Coordination Compression suppresses continuation cost more effectively.

But: compression-driven cost reduction does not strengthen without limit. Because: historical cost reduction exhibits diminishing returns. The first stable recurrence greatly lowers coordination cost; but once a path has reached high maturity, each additional recurrence contributes progressively less to cost reduction.

Theorem 4: Historical Diminishing-Returns Assumption

Although historical Coordination Compression persistently lowers continuation cost, its marginal cost-reduction contribution gradually decreases.

Note: this is a model assumption, not derived from the illustrative equation: the cost-reduction term νΩ\_c in the illustrative equation is linear in Ω\_c and shows no diminishing returns. When this assumption is adopted, the cost-reduction term must be replaced by νφ(Ω\_c), with φ increasing and concave; this chapter’s illustrative equation does not have this property. The fall of continuation cost is further limited by the 𝒵\_min constraint.

1\. Coordination Reinforcement of Familiar Routes

In this section and the next two, any path called a Familiar Route is determined according to Field Chapter 1, Definition 13; the rise and fall of Ω\_c is a change in the Familiar Route’s coordination-compression effect (its current cost 𝒵 is given separately by the cost evolution equation), not a change in its depth. The depth of a Familiar Route is its Sedimentation-Degree, which evolves according to Field Chapter 1, Equation 2.

Let the region R⁺ = {(Ω\_c, 𝒵): αΩ\_c² \> μ𝒵², and νΩ\_c² \> η𝒵}. The first condition says the low-cost recurrence gain exceeds the high-cost dispersal loss (αΩ\_c/𝒵 \> μ𝒵/Ω\_c); the second says the cost-reduction term exceeds the cost-growth term (νΩ\_c \> η𝒵/Ω\_c).

If a path is in R⁺, then ∂Ω\_c/∂t \> 0 and ∂𝒵/∂t \< 0 (taken as 0 by truncation when 𝒵 = 𝒵\_min).

After Coordination Compression strengthens, continuation cost continues to fall; after continuation cost falls, subsequent continuation recurs more easily. Thus:

Ω\_c ↑ → 𝒵 ↓ → Ω\_c ↑

A positive feedback loop forms: coordination reinforcement of the Familiar Route.

Theorem 5: Familiar-Route Coordination Reinforcement Theorem (within the illustrative model)

If a path is in R⁺ at some moment, it remains in R⁺ thereafter: Ω\_c strictly increases and 𝒵 does not increase.

Proof: in R⁺, Ω\_c increases and 𝒵 does not increase, so αΩ\_c² and νΩ\_c² increase while μ𝒵² and η𝒵 do not increase; both inequalities can only strengthen, and the path does not leave R⁺. ∎

Note: the single condition “low-cost recurrence exceeds high-cost dispersal” is not sufficient to guarantee positive feedback; the cost-reduction term must also exceed the cost-growth term. v1.1 listed only the former; this version completes it.

1\. Coordination Decay of Familiar Routes

Let the region R⁻ = {(Ω\_c, 𝒵): αΩ\_c² \< μ𝒵², and νΩ\_c² \< η𝒵}.

If a path is in R⁻, then ∂Ω\_c/∂t \< 0 and ∂𝒵/∂t \> 0.

After Coordination Compression falls, continuation cost rises again; subsequent continuation recurs stably less easily; Coordination Compression continues to decay.

Theorem 6: Familiar-Route Coordination Decay Theorem (within the illustrative model)

If a path is in R⁻ at some moment t₀, it remains in R⁻ until Ω\_c falls to 0 (leaving the domain of applicability), and Ω\_c falls to 0 in finite time, the time required not exceeding Ω\_c(t₀)² / \[2(μ𝒵(t₀) − αΩ\_c(t₀)²/𝒵(t₀))\].

Proof: in R⁻, Ω\_c decreases and 𝒵 increases; both inequalities can only strengthen, and the path does not leave R⁻. Moreover d(Ω\_c²)/dt = 2αΩ\_c²/𝒵 − 2μ𝒵, where αΩ\_c²/𝒵 does not increase and μ𝒵 does not decrease; hence d(Ω\_c²)/dt ≤ 2αΩ\_c(t₀)²/𝒵(t₀) − 2μ𝒵(t₀) \< 0 (the last inequality is the first condition of R⁻), and Ω\_c² decreases at least linearly at this rate. ∎

Ω\_c falling to 0 means the path’s Coordination Compression is exhausted and it leaves this chapter’s domain of applicability (note to Equation III). This is decay at the coordination level; whether the Familiar Route’s Sedimentation-Degree shallows at the same time is determined separately by Field Chapter 1, Equation 2.

1\. Coordination-Compression-Advantage Migration

Coordination-Compression-Advantage Migration is not movement of a path. It is: transfer of the order of Coordination Compression.

If old path q₁ satisfies: ∂Ω\_c(q₁,t)/∂t \< 0

and new path q₂ satisfies: ∂Ω\_c(q₂,t)/∂t \> 0

and at some moment t\* satisfies: Ω\_c(q₂,t*) \> Ω\_c(q₁,t*)

then, as long as the above monotone conditions persist, Ω\_c(q₂,t) \> Ω\_c(q₁,t) for all t ≥ t\*: Coordination-Compression advantage transfers from q₁ to q₂. The two paths’ Ω\_c must be comparable under the same reference conditions.

Note: Merely satisfying “old path decaying, new path reinforcing” is insufficient to guarantee transfer of Coordination-Compression advantage—the new path’s Coordination Compression must also exceed the old path’s at some moment.

Moreover, transfer of Coordination-Compression advantage is not the same as transfer of current low-cost advantage: in the illustrative model with α = μ = 1, two paths with (Ω\_c, 𝒵) = (1, 2) and (10, 5) are such that the former’s Ω\_c decays and the latter’s strengthens and is higher, yet the latter’s current cost 𝒵 is still higher. To infer transfer of low-cost advantage, a cross-path comparable cost baseline and cost-ordering conditions must be set up separately (for example, both paths being in R⁺ or R⁻ throughout the window, with 𝒵 compared directly).

Moreover, transfer of Coordination-Compression advantage is not the same as transfer of dominant continuation: which path carries dominant continuation is determined according to Field Chapter 1 (receiving flow first, as treated in Law 5 and Theorem 17; Structural Bias in Equation 11), and is not inferred from the order of Ω\_c alone. v1.1 asserted directly from the order of Ω\_c that “dominant continuation shifts from q₁ to q₂”; this version narrows that.

Theorem 7: Coordination-Compression-Advantage Migration Theorem

If the old path decays persistently, the new path reinforces persistently, and the new path’s Coordination Compression exceeds the old path’s at some moment, then thereafter the new path’s Coordination Compression always exceeds the old path’s: the order of Coordination-Compression advantage is kept on the new path’s side.

Proof: for t ≥ t*, Ω\_c(q₂,t) ≥ Ω\_c(q₂,t*) \> Ω\_c(q₁,t\*) ≥ Ω\_c(q₁,t). ∎

1\. Closure Retention

A Closure is formed through mutual return among multiple paths; therefore whether a Closure is stable depends on whether paths sufficient to carry its return can sustain stable recurrence over time.

Define a return-carrying set: a set G of paths in Closure C such that, when every path in G is available, the Closure condition holds (Field Chapter 1, Equation 16). G must be declared.

Define a return cut set: a set X of paths in Closure C such that, when all paths in X are interrupted, the remaining paths no longer satisfy the Closure condition. The “irreplaceable carrying path” of Formula Chapter 20, Appendix 1, Section 9, is a cut set with a single element.

Checking only single irreplaceable paths is not sufficient to guarantee Closure retention: a Closure may have several mutually substitutable return routes, so that removing any one path alone does not break return and there is no single irreplaceable path; if these substitute routes are interrupted by coordination failure at the same time, the Closure still loses return. Closure retention must therefore take a return-carrying set as its object.

Ω\_c is a coordination-level quantity; a Closure holds through long-term mutual-return continuation (Field Chapter 1, Equation 16). A bridge is required between them:

Bridge assumption H\_c: if a path’s Ω\_c does not decrease during some interval, its stable recurrence is not interrupted by coordination failure during that interval.

H\_c excludes only coordination failure; other causes of interruption, such as channel blockage (Inner Chapter 15, “meeting obstruction, it goes around”) and tuning suppression, are not covered.

Note: H\_c and H\_b below are bridge assumptions of this chapter; they are neither the original meaning of the Inner and Outer Chapters nor proved conclusions (author’s decision). Theorems 8, 9, and 12, which depend on them, and any downstream citation of these theorems must retain the corresponding conditions.

If every path in a declared return-carrying set G satisfies, within the window: ∂Ω\_c(q,t)/∂t ≥ 0, and no other cause interrupts the stable recurrence of the paths in G

then under H\_c, G remains available within the window, the Closure condition holds within the window, and the Closure is retained.

Theorem 8: Closure Retention Theorem (conditional)

Under bridge assumption H\_c, if a Closure has a declared return-carrying set G whose paths keep Ω\_c non-decreasing within the window, and no other cause interrupts the stable recurrence of the paths in G, then the Closure is retained within the window.

1\. Closure Breakdown

Let a Closure have a finite return cut set X = {q₁,…,q\_k}, each path timed from the same comparison start t₀ and in R⁻ (Section 8) at t₀. By Theorem 6, each path’s Ω\_c falls to 0 in finite time, the time of exhaustion not exceeding t₀ + T\_i (T\_i being the upper bound on the duration until exhaustion, counted from t₀) = Ω\_c(q\_i,t₀)² / \[2(μ𝒵(q\_i,t₀) − αΩ\_c(q\_i,t₀)²/𝒵(q\_i,t₀))\]. Since X is finite, T\_X = t₀ + max\_i T\_i exists.

Bridge assumption H\_b: if a path’s Coordination Compression is exhausted, its stable recurrence is interrupted by coordination failure.

Paths being interrupted separately is not the same as all paths in X being interrupted at once: a path interrupted earlier may recover before another is interrupted; and since the model leaves its domain of applicability once Ω\_c = 0, it cannot prove that the path does not recover. Hence it is further required:

Joint-interruption condition J: once interrupted, each path in X remains interrupted at least until T\_X; or it is declared directly that at some common time T ≤ T\_X all paths in X are interrupted.

Cut-efficacy condition: throughout \[t₀, T\_X\], X remains a cut set.

Under H\_b, J, and the cut-efficacy condition, by T\_X at the latest all paths in X are interrupted at once, the remaining paths no longer satisfy the Closure condition, and the Closure loses stable return.

Thus: Closure(C) → Collapse

Theorem 9: Closure Breakdown Theorem (conditional)

Under the illustrative model and bridge assumption H\_b, if a Closure has a finite return cut set X whose paths are in R⁻ from a common start t₀, condition J holds, and X remains a cut set throughout \[t₀, T\_X\], then the Closure breaks down by T\_X at the latest, where T\_X = t₀ + max\_i T\_i. An irreplaceable carrying path is the case where X has a single element; condition J then requires only that its interruption last until T\_X, and its irreplaceability must hold throughout \[t₀, T\_X\].

Note: v1.1 wrote “If the primary internal paths of a Closure shallow persistently, the Closure necessarily breaks down,” without a bridge or time bound, and “shallow” properly refers to Sedimentation-Degree; this version conditions it on a finite cut set, R⁻, H\_b, the joint-interruption condition, and cut efficacy. Decay of paths that do not form a cut set need not break the Closure down.

1\. Coordination Reinforcement of Co-Nuclei

A Co-Nucleus is not a single path. It is: a high-Coordination-Compression center formed through prolonged co-continuation across multiple Domains. The depth of a Co-Nucleus is determined according to Field Chapter 1, Law 9.

If the primary internal paths of a Co-Nucleus are long in R⁺ (Section 7)

then by Theorem 5 these paths’ Ω\_c increases and their continuation cost 𝒵 does not increase. ∂Ω\_c/∂t \> 0 alone is not sufficient to infer that 𝒵 decreases: the condition required by the cost evolution equation must also hold, which in the illustrative model is νΩ\_c² \> η𝒵 (for a counterexample in which Ω\_c rises and 𝒵 rises, see the symbol note in Section 2).

Theorem 10: Co-Nucleus Coordination Reinforcement Theorem (within the illustrative model)

If the primary internal paths of a Co-Nucleus are long in R⁺, their Coordination Compression strengthens continuously and their continuation cost does not increase.

Note: whether the Co-Nucleus thereby better attracts peripheral continuation, i.e., receives the flow first, is determined according to Field Chapter 1, Laws 9 and 5 (the Co-Nucleus’s depth and the comparison conditions), not by Ω\_c alone. v1.1 wrote “peripheral continuation more readily converges toward the Co-Nucleus … the Co-Nucleus’s attraction capacity strengthens”; this version narrows that.

1\. Field Phase Transition

A Field Phase Transition, as this chapter uses the term, is a conventional name whose operational meaning is: the Coordination-Compression advantage of old and new path groups changes places as a whole. This chapter does not claim that it differs from ordinary continuous change; to claim a stronger phase-transition conclusion (a change of phase identity, stability, or mode of organization), separate criteria and proof are required.

Group comparison rule: the Coordination Compression of a path group Q is read by a declared aggregate A(Q,t) (e.g., the minimum or mean of Ω\_c within the group), non-decreasing in each member’s Ω\_c; the paths’ Ω\_c within the groups must be comparable under the same reference conditions. Within the comparison window, the two groups’ membership, reference baseline, and aggregation rule (including weights) are fixed; if they are allowed to vary over time, it must be separately required and verified that the aggregate readings A(Q\_old,t) do not increase and A(Q\_new,t) does not decrease before the proof below can be used.

If every path of the old dominant path group Q\_old long satisfies: ∂Ω\_c(q,t)/∂t \< 0

and every path of the new path group Q\_new long satisfies: ∂Ω\_c(q,t)/∂t \> 0

then A(Q\_old,t) does not increase and A(Q\_new,t) does not decrease. If at some moment t\* A(Q\_new,t*) \> A(Q\_old,t*), then A(Q\_new,t) \> A(Q\_old,t) thereafter (by the reasoning of Theorem 7).

This is: a Field Phase Transition (under this chapter’s conventional name, i.e., the wholesale exchange of Coordination-Compression advantage).

Theorem 11: Field Phase Transition Theorem (wholesale exchange of Coordination-Compression advantage)

Under the declared group comparison rule, fixed within the comparison window, if every path of the old dominant path group decays persistently, every path of the new path group reinforces persistently, and at some moment the new group’s aggregate Coordination Compression exceeds the old group’s, then thereafter the new group always exceeds the old: Coordination-Compression advantage changes places as a whole.

Proof: with membership and rule fixed and A monotone in each member, while each member’s Ω\_c does not decrease in the new group and does not increase in the old, A(Q\_new,t) ≥ A(Q\_new,t*) \> A(Q\_old,t*) ≥ A(Q\_old,t). ∎

Note: whether the convergence center transfers wholesale along with it must be determined separately according to Field Chapter 1 (receiving flow first and Structural Bias), and is not inferred from Coordination-Compression advantage alone; current costs must be compared directly through 𝒵 (see Section 9). v1.1 wrote “the convergence center transfers wholesale” and called the Field Phase Transition “not ordinary gradual change”; this version withdraws both.

1\. Present Continuity

This section treats only the coordination-support conditions of Present Continuity, not Present Continuity itself or the sense of continuity directly.

The Recognition-Realm, defined in Field Chapter 2, is the recognition-continuation network formed after the Domain network achieves long-term stable synchronization.

Define the present-carrying path group: the path group G\_now that carries the “present” of the Recognition-Realm, which must be declared; return-carrying sets and cut sets may be declared within it (Sections 10 and 11).

Declare the coordination role carried by G\_now: maintaining the return of the Recognition-Realm’s present, i.e., the Closure condition within G\_now (Section 10).

Coordination support of the present: within the window, G\_now contains at least one set of paths sufficient to carry the declared coordination role that remains continuously available (its stable recurrence not interrupted by coordination failure or other causes).

A return-carrying set meets this role: by definition, when all its paths are available the Closure condition holds, i.e., the declared return is maintained. Hence if any return-carrying set remains continuously available within the window, the coordination support of the present holds; it is not required that no path of G\_now be interrupted.

If every path of a return-carrying set of G\_now satisfies, within the window: ∂Ω\_c(q,t)/∂t ≥ 0, and no other cause interrupts its continuation

then under bridge assumption H\_c, that carrying set remains continuously available within the window, and the coordination support of the present is maintained within the window.

The meaning of a cut set here: a finite path set X in G\_now such that, when all paths in X are interrupted, the remaining paths contain no set sufficient to carry the declared role.

If X satisfies all the conditions of Theorem 9 (the illustrative model, bridge assumption H\_b, X a finite cut set whose paths are in R⁻ from a common start t₀, the joint-interruption condition J, and cut efficacy throughout \[t₀, T\_X\]), then the coordination support of the present is lost by T\_X at the latest.

Whether maintaining or losing coordination support amounts to maintaining or diminishing Present Continuity, and whether the sense of reality’s continuity changes accordingly, must be bridged separately through the cross-temporal binding of the present (Continuation Binding S in Formula Chapter 20) and the established conclusions of the Observer chapters, stating whether substitute carriers exist once G\_now is interrupted. This chapter makes no such assertion.

Theorem 12: Coordination-Support Theorem of Present Continuity (conditional)

Coordination support of the present means that within the window G\_now contains at least one set of paths sufficient to carry the declared coordination role that remains continuously available. Under bridge assumption H\_c, if a return-carrying set of G\_now keeps Ω\_c non-decreasing within the window, and no other cause interrupts its continuation, the coordination support of the present is maintained within the window; if a finite cut set X of G\_now satisfies all the conditions of Theorem 9, the coordination support of the present is lost by T\_X at the latest.

Note: v1.1 wrote “Present Continuity derives from the persistent sustenance of the dominant low-impedance structure; when the dominant structure shallows overall, Present Continuity decreases,” and said that “present structure begins to fragment, and the sense of reality’s continuity diminishes”; this version narrows these to coordination-support conditions.

1\. Final Compression

One route by which history influences the future is: the past still lowering the coordination cost of the future.

Therefore: at the coordination level, every structure is a temporary equilibrium between Coordination Compression and high-cost dispersal.

That is:

∂Ω\_c/∂t = low-cost recurrence − high-cost dispersal

∂𝒵/∂t = instability-driven cost growth − compression-driven cost reduction

(Both equations are illustrative models; conclusions that use only directions are premised on M1 to M3. Ω\_c reads the compression effect and 𝒵 reads current cost; the two must be compared separately.)

Therefore: field dynamics does not study what structures are called. It studies why structures, once formed, continue to reinforce, decay, migrate, sustain, break down, and undergo phase transitions.
