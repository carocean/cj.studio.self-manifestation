# Deep-Layer Field Dynamics Equations

1\. Purpose

The preceding six chapters have established:

\- Field Chapter 1: how sedimentation structures (Groove-Lines, Domains, Familiar Routes, Co-Nuclei, Closures) form;  
\- Field Chapter 2: how Domain networks synchronize and the Recognition-Realm forms;  
\- Field Chapter 3: how Co-Nuclei stabilize;  
\- Field Chapter 4: path-layer coordination-compression dynamics (the Ω\_c evolution equation);  
\- Field Chapter 5: why history is irreversible;  
\- Field Chapter 6: why differentiation emerges from the Undifferentiated State.

But Field Chapter 4 describes, at the path layer, the coordination-compression evolution of a single path; it has not yet established domain-layer dynamics equations for the following questions:

Why sedimentation does not accumulate without limit.

Why structure can both stabilize and fracture.

Why new Domains form after Boundary Saturation Outgrowth.

Why Familiar Routes can persist over time.

Why structure both converges and spreads diffusely.

If there were no degradation (which differs from a net Sedimentation-Degree that never decreases: when growth offsets degradation, the net value may also not decrease), then:

\- old traces, once left, would not exit through degradation;  
\- local resolution capacity could not be released by degradation, only redistributed by migration and diffusion;  
\- whether new differences can enter would depend only on residual capacity and migration, with no capacity freed by degradation;  
\- whether new structure can form would likewise lose capacity freed by degradation as a source.

(No degradation is also not the same as unbounded growth: the gate makes Effective Input tend to zero as residual capacity tends to zero.)

Therefore: degradation is not an incidental phenomenon. Degradation is the process by which a field of finite resolution releases local resolution capacity.

Relation to Field Chapter 4: Field Chapter 4 v1.2 establishes, at the path layer, an illustrative model of Coordination Compression Ω\_c, where Ω\_c is the coordination-compression effect under the same reference conditions; this chapter establishes, at the domain-spatial layer, a system of partial differential equations (an illustrative model) for the local-domain Sedimentation-Degree δ(x,t), introducing spatial differential operators (∇, ∇²) and kernel functions. The two have different objects: Ω\_c is not a Sedimentation-Degree, and this chapter sets up no correspondence between them.

This chapter will: bring input gating, competitive degradation, flow, diffusion, and neighborhood transfer into a single illustrative dynamical system, and give the condition for the path of a Familiar Route to remain in the main resolution zone, the condition for the total sedimentation to be bounded, and the Sedimentation-Degree conditions for domain-formation candidacy and nuclear stability. Whether a new Domain, a Closure, or a Nucleus exists must be certified separately by structural conditions, which lie outside this chapter’s model (Sections 13 and 14). The status of each conclusion is given in the “Status Table of Conclusions” at the end of Section 18.

1\. Local Resolution Occupancy Equation

Local Resolution Occupancy Equation:

ρ(x,t) = ∫K(x−y)δ(y,t)dy

This means: the resolution pressure at local Domain x is jointly determined by the sedimentation in nearby related Domains, not by the field as a whole uniformly. Therefore: only nearby, overlapping, related structures significantly compete for the same local resolution capacity.

1\. Residual Resolution Capacity Equation

Residual Resolution Capacity Equation:

G(x,t) = Λ(x) − ρ(x,t)

where Λ(x) is the stable Local Resolution Capacity of local Domain x. This chapter does not elaborate on the evolution of Λ; it merely acknowledges that different local Domains may have different local resolution capacities.

When G \> 0: the local Domain can still absorb new differences.  
When G → 0: the local Domain approaches Domain Fullness.  
When G ≤ 0: the resolution capacity of the local Domain is used up; this chapter calls this capacity fullness (Domain Fullness in Field Chapter 1, Definition 20, further requires that it can neither deepen further, nor be further differentiated, nor admit further resolution).

1\. Local Capacity Load Equation

Local Capacity Load Equation:

C(x,t) = ∫W(x−y)δ(y,t)dy

Under N₊ and W ≥ 0, C ≥ 0. This means: the input attenuation of a local Domain is jointly determined by the neighboring sedimentation load, not by a single total capacity shared across the whole field. Therefore: field load is local in character.

1\. Structural Overlap Matching Equation

Structural Overlap Matching Equation:

In this chapter’s model, the source of sedimentation deepening has two layers. First, precedence: the deeper receives the flow first (Outer Chapter 3; Field Chapter 1, Law 5: under the comparison conditions C₌, the deeper satisfies the outflow condition no later than the shallower; under C₌ and P₁₇, strictly first, Theorem 17). Second, the amount of Effective Input after entry: this chapter models it by the degree of overlap M between the current input structure and the existing sedimentation structure. Neither replaces the other: M does not determine precedence, and precedence is determined according to Field Chapter 1.

Define the matching rate:

M(x,t) = ∫\_Nx min(S(y,t), δ(y,t))dy / ∫\_Nx max(S(y,t), δ(y,t))dy

where Nx denotes the neighborhood range of local Domain x (i.e., integration is performed over the local neighborhood of x, not the whole field). S(y,t) and δ(y,t) are both defined on the same continuous space, so the integration domains are unified. If the denominator is 0 (i.e., S and δ vanish almost everywhere on Nx), M(x,t) = 0 by convention.

Non-negativity constraint: this chapter requires S(y,t) ≥ 0, and N₊ ensures δ(y,t) ≥ 0. This constraint ensures the min/max operations are well-defined (if negative values were allowed, min/max would lose their semantic meaning as overlap measures).

Under the conventions above, 0 ≤ M ≤ 1.

When M → 0: input and the existing Domain have almost no overlap.  
When M → 1: input and the existing Domain have high overlap.

This M is only the minimal computable overlap approximation. Higher structural matching can further introduce temporal, topological, and directional structure.

Note: M need not be monotone in δ: where δ already exceeds S, a further increase of δ lowers M. Hence this model does not imply “the deeper, the more Effective Input”; this does not conflict with the deeper receiving the flow first, which concerns precedence and is determined according to Field Chapter 1.

Therefore: in this chapter’s model, overlap is an enhancing factor of Effective Input: the larger M, the larger the pre-gate input by the factor 1 + p·M; when M = 0, Effective Input can still be positive. The Familiar Route itself is determined according to Field Chapter 1, Definition 13: existing deep sedimentation and a frequent-travel record within the declared window (Outer Chapter 3: “because the deeper receives the flow first, signals travel easily and it is commonly familiar; this is what is called the commonly familiar route”).

1\. Finite-Field Input Attenuation Equation

Finite-Field Input Attenuation Equation:

A = I·(1 + p·M)·exp(−C(x,t)/C₀)

I\_eff = A·σ(G(x,t))

R = A·(1 − σ(G(x,t)))

where σ(G) = max(G,0)/(max(G,0)+η) (η \> 0, the capacity smoothing coefficient) is the residual-capacity gating function. A is the pre-gate input after overlap enhancement and load attenuation; I\_eff is the part absorbed by the local Domain; R is the part blocked by the capacity gate and not absorbed. The part removed by the load attenuation exp(−C/C₀) is treated in this chapter as attenuation loss and is not counted further.

Role of σ(G): when G is ample (G \>\> η), σ(G) ≈ 1, and the capacity constraint is not significant; when G → 0 (Domain approaching fullness), σ(G) → 0, Effective Input tends to zero and the unabsorbed input tends to A; when G ≤ 0, σ(G) = 0, I\_eff = 0, R = A. This gate is defined and bounded everywhere. p is a fixed finite constant; if p → ∞, then at points where G \> 0, M \> 0, and I \> 0, I\_eff still grows without bound in p, and σ(G) cannot prevent it.

This means: the higher the overlap between input and the existing Domain, the larger the pre-gate input. But as local capacity load rises or residual capacity approaches zero, Effective Input attenuates to zero overall, and the unabsorbed part becomes R.

Neighborhood transfer term (new model content):

B\_out(x,t) = σ(G(x,t))·∫T(x,y,t)·R(y,t)dy

where T(x,y,t) ≥ 0 is the neighborhood transfer kernel, which must be declared and satisfies: (i) T(x,y,t) is nonzero only in a neighborhood of y; (ii) for each y, ∫T(x,y,t)dx ≤ 1, i.e., the amount transferred out does not exceed the unabsorbed input at y; (iii) direction declaration: the weight of T in x is nondecreasing in G(x) and M(x) (for example, T(x,y) ∝ K\_T(x−y)·σ(G(x))·(1 + p\_T·M(x)), then normalized).

That is: the unabsorbed input R(y) is distributed according to the declared transfer kernel; the inflow actually absorbed at neighboring x is given by B\_out(x) (through x’s own capacity gate σ(G(x))) and may be zero; the remainder leaves the field and is not counted further.

This is the neighborhood transfer model of unabsorbed input, new model content of this chapter; with T = 0 the model reverts to one without neighborhood transfer. Since η \> 0, wherever A \> 0 one always has R = A·η/(max(G,0)+η) \> 0: when residual capacity is ample, R is small and the transfer is negligible; only when G → 0 or G ≤ 0 does R approach or equal A, and the transfer becomes significant. G ≤ 0 and A \> 0 also do not guarantee a nonzero actual inflow: T may be 0, and the neighbors may all be at capacity fullness (σ(G(x)) = 0).

Relation to Boundary Saturation Outgrowth: this model is a candidate implementation mechanism for Boundary Saturation Outgrowth in Field Chapter 1, Definition 21, not a certification of it. Boundary Saturation Outgrowth in Definition 21—“after Domain Fullness, new Trace-Effects, unable to continue sinking inward, overflow along the Domain boundary and generate new Structural Positions”—requires: Domain Fullness (Definition 20, not merely capacity fullness G ≤ 0); new Trace-Effects unable to continue sinking inward; overflow along the Domain boundary; generation of new Structural Positions. This model expresses only the part “input continues to enter while this place cannot absorb it, so it is distributed to the neighborhood according to the transfer kernel.” Capacity fullness is not Domain Fullness; R is positive even when capacity is not full; absorption by a neighbor is not generation of a new Structural Position. Hence the existence of transfer is not the occurrence of Boundary Saturation Outgrowth. Only when the structural conditions above are separately satisfied is it discussed whether this transfer bears Boundary Saturation Outgrowth.

1\. Resolution Flow Equation

Resolution Flow Equation:

J(x,t) = −r·∇ρ(x,t) + γχ·∇M(x,t)

where χ is a positive constant, so γ·∇(χ·M) = γχ·∇M. This chapter does not elaborate on flow inertia or delay; only the instantaneous local resolution flow approximation is used.

−r·∇ρ: Resolution Flow tends toward low-occupancy regions.  
γχ·∇M: Resolution Flow tends toward high-match regions.

Therefore: the direction of the Resolution Flow is a weighted combination of the occupancy-decreasing direction and the match-increasing direction (Theorem 4). Where the two agree, the flow goes toward neighboring Domains of low occupancy and high match; where they conflict, the direction is decided by the weighted sum.

1\. Main Sedimentation-Field Dynamics Equation

Main Sedimentation-Field Dynamics Equation:

∂δ/∂t = I\_eff + B\_out − q₀δ − β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ − ∇·J + f·∇²δ

(Category: illustrative model. The functional forms of the terms, together with ρ, G, C, M, A, I\_eff, R, J, and B\_out in Sections 3 to 8, are model choices of this chapter, not derived from the Inner and Outer Chapters or from Field Chapter 1.)

where φ(u) is the Local Resolution Competition Function, defined as:

φ(u) = u + c\_φ·max(u−1, 0)²

where c\_φ ≥ 0 is the nonlinear competition enhancement coefficient.

When c\_φ = 0: φ(u) = u, fully linear throughout—the minimal computable approximation.  
When c\_φ \> 0: for u ≤ 1, φ(u) = u (consistent with linear); for u \> 1, φ(u) = u + c\_φ(u−1)² (nonlinear enhancement after oversaturation).

Smoothness note: at u = 1, φ(1) = 1 (continuous), φ’(1) = 1 (first derivative continuous), φ’’(1⁺) = 2c\_φ (right second derivative exists at u = 1). The function is C¹ continuous at u = 1, avoiding derivative jumps, suitable for PDE stability analysis. The precise functional form (including higher-order smooth versions) is left to subsequent chapters to determine by specific field type. For u ≥ 0, φ(u) ≥ 0.

Meaning of each term:

I\_eff: input absorbed locally.

B\_out: unabsorbed input from the neighborhood, transferred in by neighborhood transfer and absorbed locally.

−q₀δ: baseline degradation. Sedimentation naturally degrades even when local resolution competition is very weak.

−β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ: extra degradation caused by local resolution competition. The higher the local resolution occupancy, the higher the cost of maintaining old sedimentation, and the more easily old traces degrade. (This model writes the acceleration of Field-Quality shallowing under local occupancy pressure as this term; it is not given in the original; see Section 2.)

−∇·J: sedimentation migration due to local Resolution Flow. ∇·J \> 0 means sedimentation in that local Domain flows outward; ∇·J \< 0 means neighboring sedimentation flows into that local Domain. Therefore sedimentation not only increases or decreases locally, but also migrates and converges within the field.

f·∇²δ: undirected local diffusion. Sedimentation has a local diffuse-spreading tendency; diffusion weakens local difference gradients.

Therefore: J represents directed convergent flow; f·∇²δ represents undirected local diffuse spreading; B\_out represents neighborhood inflow.

Dimensional Note (under model declaration (7)):

Left side ∂δ/∂t: \[T⁻¹\]  
I\_eff, B\_out: \[T⁻¹\] (absorption rates); A, R, I: \[T⁻¹\]  
q₀δ: q₀ has dimension \[T⁻¹\], term dimension \[T⁻¹\]  
β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ: β has dimension \[T⁻¹\], term dimension \[T⁻¹\]  
J: \[L·T⁻¹\]; ∇·J: \[T⁻¹\]. From J = −r·∇ρ + γχ·∇M, ∇ρ and ∇M have dimension \[L⁻¹\], so r and γχ have dimension \[L²·T⁻¹\]  
f·∇²δ: f has dimension \[L²·T⁻¹\], ∇² has dimension \[L⁻²\], term dimension \[T⁻¹\]  
T(x,y,t): \[L⁻ⁿ\] (so that ∫T dx is dimensionless)

All terms are dimensionally consistent, each \[T⁻¹\].

Coefficient Note:

q₀ \> 0 (baseline degradation coefficient): determines the baseline degradation half-life, t₁/₂ = ln2/q₀.  
β ≥ 0 (local competition degradation coefficient): larger β means high resolution occupancy accelerates sedimentation degradation more severely.  
p ≥ 0 (match-enhancement coefficient, fixed and finite): larger p means input-structure overlap enhances the pre-gate input more strongly.  
γ \> 0 (match-gradient flow coefficient): larger γ means Resolution Flow tends more strongly toward high-match regions.  
r \> 0 (occupancy-gradient flow coefficient): larger r means Resolution Flow tends more strongly toward low-occupancy regions.  
f \> 0 (local diffusion coefficient): larger f means the local diffuse-spreading tendency of sedimentation is stronger.

1\. Baseline Degradation

When I\_eff = B\_out = 0 (for example, if I ≡ 0 on Ω\_s throughout the period considered, then A, I\_eff, R, and B\_out vanish everywhere; I = 0 at this point alone does not imply B\_out = 0), β·φ(ρ/Λ) = 0, ∇·J = 0, and ∇²δ = 0:

∂δ/∂t = −q₀δ

with solution: δ(t) = δ₀·e^(−q₀t)

Therefore: q₀ determines the baseline degradation half-life: t₁/₂ = ln2/q₀ (Theorem 2). If only I\_eff = B\_out = 0, ρ ≈ 0, and flow and diffusion are negligible, then ∂δ/∂t ≈ −q₀δ, and the half-life is approximately ln2/q₀.

This indicates: Field Quality has a measurable component. Different fields have different baseline degradation rates.

1\. Growth, Stability, and Degradation

If I\_eff + B\_out \> q₀δ + β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ + ∇·J − f·∇²δ, sedimentation increases.

If I\_eff + B\_out = q₀δ + β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ + ∇·J − f·∇²δ, sedimentation is stable.

If I\_eff + B\_out \< q₀δ + β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ + ∇·J − f·∇²δ, sedimentation degrades.

Note: this rearranges the main equation, placing ∇·J on the degradation side. ∇·J \> 0 denotes net outflow (sedimentation migrating outward, contributing to decrease in δ); ∇·J \< 0 denotes net inflow (sedimentation flowing in from neighboring Domains, contributing to increase in δ).

Therefore: whether the path of a Familiar Route can remain in the main resolution zone during the period considered depends on whether, amid persistent input, structural overlap, competition, flow, diffusion, and degradation, its Sedimentation-Degree avoids persistent decline (Theorem 3). The determination of a Familiar Route still follows Field Chapter 1, Definition 13.

1\. Main-Resolution-Zone Threshold

Let δᵣ denote the declared main-resolution-zone threshold. Its operational use is: to determine whether a path is in the main resolution zone. It does not replace the general meaning of familiarity (Field Chapter 1, Definition 13: “if a specific field sets up a separate threshold such as δᵣ, its operational use must be declared, and it does not replace the general meaning of familiarity”).

If δ ≥ δᵣ, the path is said to be in the main resolution zone; if the path is also a Familiar Route (existing deep sedimentation and a frequent-travel record within the declared window), it is called a main-resolution-zone Familiar Route. Whether it forms a stable continuation bias for subsequent input is determined according to Field Chapter 1, Equation 11 (Structural Bias), and is not inferred from δ ≥ δᵣ alone.

If δ \< δᵣ, the path gradually exits the main resolution zone.

Therefore: whether history can persist depends not only on whether something occurred, but more on whether that path can maintain sufficient Sedimentation-Degree over time.

1\. Local Source Surplus

Local Source Surplus:

O(x,t) = max(I\_eff + B\_out − q₀δ − β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ, 0)

By the main equation, I\_eff + B\_out − q₀δ − β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ = ∂δ/∂t + ∇·J − f·∇²δ, so the two expressions are identical, with no need for a steady state or vanishing flow terms. O is the local source surplus after subtracting degradation and before subtracting net migration and diffusion; it is not the actual net growth. For example, with Effective Input 3, degradation 1, net outflow 2, and diffusion 0, ∂δ/∂t = 0 while O = 2.

O is not used as the transfer criterion: where capacity is full (G ≤ 0), I\_eff = 0 and, under N₊, the degradation terms are nonnegative; if B\_out is also 0 there, then O = 0. Whether transfer is significant is judged by the unabsorbed input R (Section 7): G → 0 or G ≤ 0, and A \> 0 (then R → A or R = A); whether the actual inflow is nonzero further depends on T and the neighbors’ capacity gates.

Capacity-Full Transfer State: when G(x,t) → 0 or G(x,t) ≤ 0, and A(x,t) \> 0, this chapter calls it the Capacity-Full Transfer State. This is only a candidate state: the unabsorbed input R here approaches or equals A and is distributed according to the transfer kernel; the actual inflow is given by B\_out and may be zero. To assert that a nonzero inflow actually occurs at some time, one further needs ∫\_{Ω\_s} B\_out(x,t)dx \> 0. At a neighboring x that receives inflow, B\_out enters the main equation and enlarges the source term for sedimentation increase there.

But: transfer does not necessarily form a stable new Domain. If the transfer exists only briefly, it will degrade and dissipate. Persistent growth of the neighboring Sedimentation-Degree is only a candidate condition for domain formation (Criterion 5); whether a new Domain or a new Closure forms must be certified separately by structural conditions: a Domain in Field Chapter 1, Definition 8, must stably maintain the five conditions of accommodation, resolution, differential sedimentation depth, continuation, and bundle boundary; a Closure in Equation 16 requires long-term mutual return-continuation with repeatable return. This chapter’s model does not derive these structural conditions.

Therefore: in this model, neighborhood transfer is not random: the unabsorbed input is distributed according to the declared transfer kernel, its direction given by the direction declaration of T (in comparisons with other factors equal, see Theorem 4); the actual inflow is given by B\_out and may be zero. Whether this transfer bears Boundary Saturation Outgrowth must be certified separately by the structural conditions of Field Chapter 1, Definition 21 (Section 7).

1\. Nuclear Stabilization

After a new Domain forms, it is not necessarily stable over the long term. \|∂δ/∂t\| \< ε persisting for longer than τs is the Sedimentation-Degree stability condition for an existing Nucleus (Criterion 6).

This condition only means that within a finite window the change of Sedimentation-Degree there is small; it allows static equilibrium (all terms zero, for example, no input and δ = 0) and also allows the terms to be nonzero and cancel each other (dynamic equilibrium). The window itself does not guarantee long-term stability after the window ends. Hence it cannot by itself prove that a Nucleus exists. The existence of a Nucleus must be certified separately by structural conditions (for example, the Co-Nucleus of Field Chapter 1, Definition 15, or the five stability conditions of a Domain in Definition 8), which this chapter’s model does not derive.

Therefore: given that a Nucleus already exists, Criterion 6 characterizes a small change of Sedimentation-Degree within a finite window; it allows static equilibrium, and also allows the terms of input, competition, degradation, flow, and diffusion to be nonzero and cancel each other.

1\. Extreme Cases

The following are conditional model trends at two levels: the change of instantaneous rates at a fixed state; and the behavior of the whole solution as the parameters vary.

(1) q₀ → 0 and β → 0. At a fixed state: the degradation rate q₀δ + β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ tends to 0 with the coefficients. Small but positive degradation still releases capacity: under persistent input with no migratory dispersal, input and degradation may still balance before capacity is full. For example, take a spatially uniform state with Λ = η = I = 1, p = β = 0, W = 0, T = 0, and zero migration and diffusion; then dδ/dt = (1 − δ)/(2 − δ) − q₀δ (0 ≤ δ \< 1); for q₀ = 0.1 there is a stable equilibrium δ\* ≈ 0.90098, G\* ≈ 0.09902 \> 0; for any small positive q₀, the right side equals 1/2 at δ = 0 and tends to −q₀ as δ → 1, so an equilibrium below 1 likewise exists. Zero degradation (q₀ = β = 0) is discussed separately as a limiting model: then capacity cannot be released by degradation, only redistributed by migration and diffusion; but whether capacity fullness is reached must still be proved separately and cannot be inferred from persistent input alone (the gate makes Effective Input tend to zero as G → 0). Zero degradation lies outside q\_min \> 0 in model declaration (6), so Theorem 1′ does not apply; its bound tends to infinity as q\_min → 0, so the total estimate no longer gives a finite bound (this does not mean the total is necessarily unbounded).

(2) q\_min → ∞. Declaration of the parameter limit: consider a family of parameters with q\_min → ∞, with the initial total m(0) and Ī(1+p) uniformly bounded, \|Ω\_s\| fixed, all solutions existing on a common interval \[0, T\_c), and the other declarations unchanged. A general pointwise q₀(x) → ∞ is not the same as q\_min → ∞. At a fixed state: the degradation rate grows without bound in q₀. Whole solution: from dm/dt ≤ Ī(1+p)\|Ω\_s\| − q\_min·m in the proof of Theorem 1′, m(t) ≤ m(0)·e^(−q\_min·t) + (Ī(1+p)\|Ω\_s\|/q\_min)·(1 − e^(−q\_min·t)); at any fixed t in (0, T\_c), the right side tends to 0 as q\_min → ∞. That is, under N₊ and the uniform conditions above, total sedimentation tends to zero at any fixed positive time.

(3) β → ∞. At a fixed state: only where φ(ρ/Λ) \> 0 and δ \> 0 does the competition degradation rate grow without bound in β; where φ(ρ/Λ) = 0 (ρ = 0), increasing β has no effect. The behavior of the whole solution in β is not derived in this chapter.

(4) Whether Familiar Routes can form, whether history can be retained, and whether reality can update are judgments at the structural and observer levels; the above are only trends at the Sedimentation-Degree level, and this chapter’s model does not derive those conclusions.

1\. Core Conclusions

First: degradation is not a defect. Degradation is the process by which a field of finite resolution releases local resolution capacity.

Second: forgetting is not deletion. In the original meaning, forgetting is the fading and shallowing of traces (Inner Chapter 6: “little used, the trace fades”; Outer Chapter 3: “Field Quality is what determines the rate of forgetting”); in this model, the operational determination of forgetting is the Sedimentation-Degree exiting the main resolution zone (δ \< δᵣ).

Third: a Familiar Route is a commonly familiar route (Field Chapter 1, Definition 13); in this chapter’s model, overlap is an enhancing factor of its Effective Input, not a necessary condition.

Fourth: field fullness is not an excessively high total field quantity. In this model, capacity fullness is local resolution occupancy reaching local resolution capacity (G ≤ 0).

Fifth: in this model, unabsorbed input is distributed according to the declared transfer kernel, not randomly (new model content); the inflow actually absorbed by neighbors is given by B\_out and may be zero. This is a candidate implementation mechanism for Boundary Saturation Outgrowth; whether it bears Boundary Saturation Outgrowth and whether a new Closure forms must be certified separately by Field Chapter 1, Definition 21, and structural conditions.

Sixth: Resolution Flow and diffusion determine how sedimentation migrates and spreads diffusely; domain formation must be established separately by structural conditions.

Seventh: given that a Nucleus already exists, Criterion 6 characterizes a small change of Sedimentation-Degree within a finite window; it allows static equilibrium, and also allows the terms to be nonzero and cancel each other. The window does not guarantee long-term stability afterward. The existence of a Nucleus must be certified separately by structural conditions.

1\. On Model Level

The equations given in this chapter constitute an illustrative minimal closed model of sedimentation-field dynamics; the functional forms are model choices of this chapter. Their purpose is to describe the minimal dynamical relations among input gating, competitive degradation, flow, diffusion, and neighborhood transfer, and to give the Sedimentation-Degree conditions for domain-formation candidacy and nuclear stability.

Equation structure positioning: this chapter’s main equation

∂δ/∂t = I\_eff + B\_out − q₀δ − β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ − ∇·J + f·∇²δ

belongs to the Competition–Flow–Diffusion–Decay closed system, with an added nonlocal neighborhood transfer term. Its structure is of the same family as the Reaction-Diffusion Equation, but with the addition of a directional Resolution Flow term −∇·J, a nonlinear competition degradation term −β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ, and the transfer term B\_out.

Therefore: the structural overlap matching, local resolution competition, capacity influence kernels, Resolution Flow, and transfer kernel in this chapter all use minimal computable approximations. Subsequent field mathematics may further introduce: temporal structure, topological structure, directional structure, flow inertia, time-delay dynamics, multi-scale Domains, and higher-order structural matching.

1\. Theorems and Criteria

Theorem 1: Input Gating Theorem (within the illustrative model)

Under model declaration (6), 0 ≤ I\_eff ≤ Ī(1 + p)·σ(G) and 0 ≤ A ≤ Ī(1 + p). As G(x,t) → 0⁺, I\_eff(x,t) → 0; when G(x,t) ≤ 0, I\_eff(x,t) = 0 and R(x,t) = A(x,t).

Proof sketch: 0 ≤ M ≤ 1, so 1 ≤ 1 + p·M ≤ 1 + p; under N₊ and W ≥ 0, C ≥ 0, so 0 \< exp(−C/C₀) ≤ 1. Hence 0 ≤ A ≤ Ī(1 + p), and I\_eff = A·σ(G). By the definition of σ, σ(G) → 0 as G → 0⁺, and σ(G) = 0 when G ≤ 0. ∎

This theorem proves only input gating: Effective Input is bounded and tends to zero when capacity is exhausted. It does not control migration inflow, local concentration, or the long-term behavior of solutions, and does not imply that the local Sedimentation-Degree is bounded.

Theorem 1′: Total Sedimentation Boundedness Theorem (within the illustrative model)

Under model declarations (1) to (6) and N₊, let the total sedimentation be m(t) = ∫\_{Ω\_s} δ(x,t)dx, and \|Ω\_s\| the volume of Ω\_s. Then for all t ∈ \[0, T\_end):

m(t) ≤ max( m(0), Ī(1 + p)\|Ω\_s\| / q\_min )

Proof sketch: integrate the main equation over Ω\_s. By the no-flux boundary condition and f constant, ∫(−∇·J + f·∇²δ)dx = −∫\_{∂Ω\_s}(J − f·∇δ)·n dS = 0. By condition (ii) on T, ∫B\_out dx ≤ ∫∫T(x,y)R(y)dydx ≤ ∫R dy. Hence ∫(I\_eff + B\_out)dx ≤ ∫(A·σ + A·(1 − σ))dx = ∫A dx ≤ Ī(1 + p)\|Ω\_s\|. Under N₊, ρ ≥ 0, φ(ρ/Λ) ≥ 0, β ≥ 0, so ∫β·φ·δ dx ≥ 0; and ∫q₀δ dx ≥ q\_min·m. Thus dm/dt ≤ Ī(1 + p)\|Ω\_s\| − q\_min·m, and the comparison principle gives the result. ∎

Whether the local Sedimentation-Degree δ(x,t) is bounded (whether it can concentrate locally through migration inflow) is not proved in this chapter and is registered as an open problem.

Theorem 2: Baseline Degradation Half-Life Theorem (within the illustrative model)

Under the conditions of no absorbed input at this point (I\_eff = B\_out = 0; for example, I ≡ 0 on Ω\_s throughout the period considered), a zero local competition degradation term (β·φ(ρ/Λ) = 0), no net Resolution Flow (∇·J = 0), and spatially uniform Sedimentation-Degree (∇²δ = 0), sedimentation decays exponentially with half-life t₁/₂ = ln2/q₀. If only I\_eff = B\_out = 0, ρ ≈ 0, and flow and diffusion are negligible, the formula is approximate.

Proof sketch: under the listed conditions, the main equation reduces to ∂δ/∂t = −q₀δ, with solution δ(t) = δ₀·e^(−q₀t); setting δ(t₁/₂) = δ₀/2 gives t₁/₂ = ln2/q₀. ∎

Theorem 3: Main-Resolution-Zone Maintenance Theorem (within the illustrative model)

Let δᵣ \> 0 be a fixed operational threshold, and let \[t₀, t₁\] ⊂ \[0, T\_end) be a valid interval on which the solution exists.

(a) Necessary condition: let δ(x,t₀) ≥ δᵣ, and ∂δ(x,t)/∂t ≤ −c \< 0 for all t in \[t₀, t₁\]. Let T\_r = t₀ + (δ(x,t₀) − δᵣ)/c. If T\_r ≤ t₁, then no later than T\_r, δ(x,·) reaches the threshold (δ ≤ δᵣ); for every t in (T\_r, t₁\], δ(x,t) \< δᵣ, and the path strictly exits the main resolution zone. Hence, if t₁ \> T\_r, maintaining δ ≥ δᵣ on \[t₀, t₁\] requires that the net rate of change not stay at or below −c throughout \[t₀, t₁\]. Under N₊, this negative-rate condition cannot hold indefinitely (otherwise δ would become negative), so it is stated only on a valid interval.

(b) Sufficient condition: if δ(x,t₀) ≥ δᵣ, δ(x,·) is continuously differentiable in t on \[t₀, t₁\], and ∂δ(x,t)/∂t \> 0 whenever δ(x,t) = δᵣ within \[t₀, t₁\], then δ(x,t) ≥ δᵣ for all t ∈ \[t₀, t₁\].

Proof sketch: (a) for t ∈ \[t₀, t₁\], δ(x,t) ≤ δ(x,t₀) − c(t − t₀); at t = T\_r the right side equals δᵣ, and for t \> T\_r it is less than δᵣ. (b) If δ(x,t) \< δᵣ for some t ∈ (t₀, t₁\], take s\* = sup{s ∈ \[t₀,t\] : δ(x,s) ≥ δᵣ}; then δ(x,s*) = δᵣ and δ \< δᵣ immediately after s*, so ∂δ(x,s\*)/∂t ≤ 0, contradicting the assumption. ∎

This theorem guarantees only the maintenance of the main resolution zone (δ ≥ δᵣ) and does not replace the full qualification for maintaining a Familiar Route: a Familiar Route further requires existing deep sedimentation and a frequent-travel record within the window (Field Chapter 1, Definition 13).

Theorem 4: Directionality Theorem for Resolution Flow and Neighborhood Transfer (within the illustrative model)

(a) The direction of the Resolution Flow J = −r·∇ρ + γχ·∇M is the weighted combination (weights r and γχ) of the occupancy-decreasing direction −∇ρ and the match-increasing direction ∇M. Where the two agree, J points toward neighboring Domains of low resolution occupancy and high structural match; where they conflict, the direction of J is decided by the weighted sum.

(b) The destination of neighborhood transfer is given by the direction declaration of the transfer kernel T: under declaration (iii), in comparisons with other factors equal (same source y, same R(y), same distance kernel and other weights), the transferred amount is weighted toward neighbors with larger G and higher M; after transfer it is absorbed through the neighbor’s own capacity gate. The ordering of actual inflows among neighbors with different sources, distances, or weights is not derived from this.

Proof sketch: (a) read directly from the Resolution Flow equation and χ being a positive constant. (b) read directly from the declaration of T and the definition of B\_out in Section 7. ∎

Hence, in this model, both sedimentation flow and neighborhood transfer are directed by the declared gradients or transfer kernel and are not random. This theorem does not certify Boundary Saturation Outgrowth (Section 7).

Criterion 5: Growth Condition for Domain-Formation Candidacy

This chapter takes “∂δ(y,t)/∂t \> 0 in neighboring Domain y persisting for longer than τc” as the growth condition for domain-formation candidacy.

Note: this is a condition declared by this chapter, not a theorem derived from the main equation. Persistent positive growth includes the joint contributions of Effective Input, neighborhood inflow, net migration, and diffusion, and need not mean “Effective Input exceeds degradation.” It can occur in spatially uniform structures with no new boundary, so it cannot by itself certify a new Domain or a new Closure. Whether a new Domain or a new Closure forms must be certified separately by structural conditions: the five stability conditions of a Domain in Field Chapter 1, Definition 8; the Closure condition in Equation 16; the closure of stable boundaries (boundaries where coordinative unification can no longer continue, see Field Chapter 6 Theorem 4).

Criterion 6: Sedimentation-Degree Stability Condition for an Existing Nucleus

This chapter takes “\|∂δ/∂t\| \< ε persisting for longer than τs” as the Sedimentation-Degree stability condition for an existing Nucleus.

Note: this condition means that the absolute value of the sum of the right-hand terms of the main equation is less than ε; it does not require each term to be zero. An empty field, slow decay, and spatially uniform structures can all satisfy it, so it cannot by itself prove that a Nucleus exists. The existence of a Nucleus must be certified separately by structural conditions; given that a Nucleus already exists, this condition characterizes the stability of its Sedimentation-Degree.

1\. Summary Equations

Sedimentation-Field Dynamics Equations

Local Resolution Occupancy Equation: ρ(x,t) = ∫K(x−y)δ(y,t)dy

Residual Resolution Capacity Equation: G(x,t) = Λ(x) − ρ(x,t)

Local Capacity Load Equation: C(x,t) = ∫W(x−y)δ(y,t)dy

Structural Overlap Matching Equation: M(x,t) = ∫\_Nx min(S,δ)dy / ∫\_Nx max(S,δ)dy (M = 0 when the denominator is 0)

Finite-Field Input Attenuation Equation: A = I·(1+p·M)·exp(−C(x,t)/C₀); I\_eff = A·σ(G(x,t)); R = A·(1 − σ(G(x,t))), where σ(G) = max(G,0)/(max(G,0)+η)

Neighborhood transfer term: B\_out(x,t) = σ(G(x,t))·∫T(x,y,t)R(y,t)dy

Resolution Flow Equation: J(x,t) = −r·∇ρ(x,t) + γχ·∇M(x,t)

Main Sedimentation-Field Dynamics Equation: ∂δ/∂t = I\_eff + B\_out − q₀δ − β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ − ∇·J + f·∇²δ

Local Source Surplus: O(x,t) = max(I\_eff + B\_out − q₀δ − β·φ(ρ/Λ)·δ, 0)

Boundary and initial conditions: (J − f·∇δ)·n = 0 on ∂Ω\_s; δ(x,0) = δ₀(x) ≥ 0

(The equation system above is an illustrative model; N₊ and existence of solutions are declared assumptions.)

Final Compression

In a field of finite resolution, structure

is retained through competition,

migrates through flow,

spreads diffusely through diffusion,

releases capacity through degradation,

and, when capacity is full, distributes unabsorbed input according to the declared kernel; how much the neighbors actually absorb is given by B\_out.
