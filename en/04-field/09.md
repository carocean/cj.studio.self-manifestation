# Separability Dynamics Equations

1\. Purpose

The preceding chapter established: reality obtains not because the field is naturally distinct, but because the field can maintain stable fracturing under finite conditions.

Therefore: whether a difference is established depends not only on whether the Pulses differ, but more on whether the field still has the capacity to sustain them as two.

Yet the preceding chapter did not explain: why the field’s separability capacity continuously changes.

Why:

\- sometimes reality becomes increasingly clear;  
\- sometimes reality begins to blur;  
\- sometimes fine categories continuously form;  
\- sometimes fine categories collapse back into shared classes;  
\- sometimes new reality can form;  
\- sometimes old structures absorb large quantities of new differences.

The preceding chapter addressed only the existence conditions, not the dynamics.

This chapter therefore discusses: how separability capacity evolves. And: why, in this chapter’s illustrative model, the field cannot simultaneously maintain all continuously generated fracturing, but compresses and reconstructs. The status of each conclusion is given in the “Status Table of Conclusions” at the end of Section 12.

1\. Core Proposition

Reality cannot maintain infinite fracturing over the long term. Because: fracturing itself continuously raises the field’s maintenance cost.

The more boundaries, the more:

\- Tuning Load rises;  
\- inter-Domain spacing decreases;  
\- noise mutual interference strengthens;  
\- structural maintenance becomes more difficult.

Thus: the field finds it increasingly hard to sustain new fine boundaries. Fine categories that could previously be stably established begin to merge back into shared classes.

The above is this chapter’s starting view. Within it, “more boundaries → the Separability Threshold tends to rise” is expressed in this chapter as a model assumption (Assumption A1, Section 3), not a derived theorem; what is provable in this chapter’s model is: under persistent input, the density of simultaneously maintained boundaries is bounded, and the great majority of continuously generated fracturing cannot be maintained simultaneously (Theorem 6).

Therefore: reality does not infinitely subdivide, but continuously: differentiates, converges, compresses back, and reconstructs.

1\. Threshold Drift

Let:

θ\_sep(t) denote the Separability Threshold of field f at time t (directly referencing Field Chapter 8’s symbol; Field Chapter 8’s foundational assumption is θ\_sep \> 0).

Symbol note: θ\_sep is Field Chapter 8’s Separability Threshold, which determines whether two Pulses can be separated in the field (separable if d(s₁,s₂) \> θ\_sep); it is not the path outflow threshold θ(x) of Field Chapter 1, Equation 3a. The latter, “the deeper, the smaller the threshold,” means the deeper flows out more easily; the rise of θ\_sep with Deep Nucleus Density discussed here means new differences are harder to separate. The two have different objects and do not conflict.

B\_d(t) denote the Boundary Density: the density of active boundaries in the field. Its counting must be declared (for example, the number of active boundaries per unit field measure).

N(t) denote the Noise Intensity.

L(t) denote the Tuning Load of field f at time t—the total resource quantity currently consumed to sustain tuning stability for all active boundaries. The higher L, the lower the field’s remaining capacity to sustain new boundaries. L increases positively with the number of active boundaries and decreases relatively as the capacity limit Cap increases.

Cap denote the field’s tuning capacity limit; Cap − L is the Residual Capacity (Note: v1.1 denoted capacity by C, but C is already defined in Field Chapter 7 as the Local Capacity Load C(x,t); this chapter therefore uses Cap).

Dn(t) denote the Deep Nucleus Density: the density of Deep Nuclei in the field. Its counting must be declared (for example, the measure share in the field of stable local Domains whose Sedimentation-Degree is not below a declared threshold δ\_D); this chapter does not fix a unique counting.

Then: θ\_sep(t) is not constant; it evolves continuously with field state.

Threshold Drift Assumption (Assumption A1, model assumption):

Conditions under which θ\_sep(t) tends to rise:

1\. Boundary Density B\_d rises: inter-Domain spacing decreases, marginal mutual interference strengthens, Tuning Load L rises, θ\_sep ↑.  
1\. Noise Intensity N rises: fine subdivision boundaries become more unstable, θ\_sep ↑.  
1\. Residual Capacity (Cap − L) falls: the field’s available resources to sustain new boundaries decrease, θ\_sep ↑.

These three conditions expressed jointly:

B\_d ↑ or N ↑ or (Cap − L) ↓ ⟹ θ\_sep(t) tends to rise

This is a direction assumption declared by this chapter, not a theorem derived from Field Chapter 8. Its reading is unified as: with other factors equal, the above change of the corresponding quantity increases the rate of change of the Separability Threshold; whether the threshold actually rises is still decided by the sign of the total driving term. Its form in the illustrative dynamics equation is given in Section 9 (Theorem 1).

Note: history does not directly raise the threshold. History acts by forming Deep Nuclei; Deep Nuclei act through flow occupation and Familiar Route locking (Familiar Route locking per Form Chapter 20 v1.2), further raising the difficulty of sustaining new differences (see Section 5 and Assumption A2).

1\. Boundary Mutual Interference

Boundaries are not independent of each other. The denser the boundaries, the more:

\- inter-Domain spacing decreases;  
\- marginal mutual interference strengthens;  
\- tuning becomes harder to stabilize;  
\- noise penetrates more easily.

Thus: boundaries that could previously exist stably begin to exhibit diffuse spreading, overlap, compression back, and merging.

Therefore: increasing boundaries does not raise reality’s precision without limit. In this chapter’s model, with other factors equal, an increase of Boundary Density increases the rate of change of the threshold (Assumption A1), so after boundaries increase to a certain stage they may begin to weaken the overall resolution capacity.

1\. Deep Nucleus Flow Occupation

Let x\_i denote a local Domain position in the field (directly referencing Field Chapter 7’s x: a local Domain capable of forming overlap, competition, Familiar Routes, Boundary Saturation Outgrowth, and nuclear stabilization). δ(x\_i, t) is the Sedimentation-Degree of x\_i (Field Chapter 1, Definition 9; Field Chapter 7’s δ(x,t)).

Deep Nucleus flow occupation has two layers:

First, precedence: the deeper receives the flow first (Outer Chapter 3; Field Chapter 1, Law 5: under the comparison conditions C₌, the deeper satisfies the outflow condition no later than the shallower; under C₌ and P₁₇, strictly first, Theorem 17).

Second, the amount of Effective Input after entry: per Field Chapter 7 v1.2, I\_eff = A·σ(G), where the pre-gate input A is enhanced by overlap M (M does not determine precedence and need not be monotone in δ), and σ(G) = max(G,0)/(max(G,0)+η) is the residual-capacity gate. Field Chapter 7, Theorem 1: I\_eff → 0 as G → 0⁺, and I\_eff = 0 when G ≤ 0.

Flow(x\_i) denotes the subsequent continuation flow directed toward local Domain x\_i. This chapter adopts the following illustrative form:

Illustrative Assumption H\_F: Flow(x\_i, t) = k\_F·δ(x\_i, t)·σ(G(x\_i, t)), where k\_F \> 0 is a declared constant coefficient.

(Category: an illustrative assumption of this chapter. It is not an equation of Field Chapter 7, and Field Chapter 7 contains no such expression; its “increasing in δ” part is a quantitative model of receiving the flow first, stronger than the precedence conclusion of Law 5, and is used only within this chapter.)

This relation indicates: in comparisons with the same k\_F and G, Flow is nondecreasing in δ; with a common G \> 0, the one with larger δ has strictly larger Flow; with G ≤ 0, Flow is 0 for both. G changes with the sedimentation state, and G \> 0 alone does not suffice to infer that the deeper has larger flow: for example, take k\_F = Λ = η = 1 and ρ = δ (so G = 1 − δ); then for δ = 0.8, G = 0.2 and Flow ≈ 0.13333; for δ = 0.9, G = 0.1 and Flow ≈ 0.08182; both are unsaturated, yet the deeper has smaller flow. Cutoff: if δ ≤ δ\_max (δ bounded) is further declared, then as G → 0, Flow ≤ k\_F·δ\_max·σ(G) → 0; without this boundedness premise, Flow → 0 cannot be inferred from σ(G) → 0 alone. The gating conclusion of Field Chapter 7, Theorem 1, concerns the bounded pre-gate input A and does not transfer automatically to k\_F·δ.

Therefore: precedence and amount of flow are kept separate. Precedence: under the comparison conditions C₌, the deeper satisfies the outflow condition no later than the shallower (Law 5); only when P₁₇ also holds does it satisfy it strictly first (Theorem 17). Precedence does not imply occupying more flow. Amount: under H\_F, with the same k\_F and G, flow is nondecreasing in δ, and strictly increasing in δ with a common G \> 0.

Hence: reality does not maintain all inputs equally; in the sense of precedence, under C₌ Deep Nuclei receive continuation no later than the shallower, and strictly first when P₁₇ also holds (Law 5, Theorem 17).

1\. Commonality Expansion

If large quantities of new Pulses are persistently compressed into the same Deep Nucleus, then: more and more fine boundaries begin to find it hard to sustain stability. Because (per Assumptions A1 and A2, with other factors equal):

\- the rate of change of the threshold increases (whether the threshold actually rises is decided by the sign of the total driving term);  
\- tuning resources are continuously consumed;  
\- boundary mutual interference continuously strengthens.

Thus: if the Separability Threshold rises along the actual history (an additional premise), fine Domains that were separable are no longer separable and are classed as the same (Theorem 3). Whether they actually merge, whether fine boundaries disappear, and whether they merge into a Co-Nucleus (Field Chapter 1, Definition 15) must be certified separately by structural conditions.

Hence: when the structural conditions of the Co-Nucleus separately hold, the Co-Nucleus’s coverage range expands.

Therefore (interpretive): the “categories” that stably appear in reality are the stable result of long-term field compression.

1\. Why New Differences Are Difficult

If a new Pulse falls near an old Nucleus, the old Nucleus receives the flow, in the sense of precedence, no later than the place of the new Pulse (under C₌, Law 5; strictly first when P₁₇ also holds, Theorem 17; Section 5). Thus new differences find it hard to sustain their own boundaries.

A new boundary requires at least:

d(s\_new, s\_old) \> θ\_sep(t)

(Field Chapter 8’s separability condition). Near an old Nucleus, because the old Nucleus preferentially occupies flow, the difference required for a new boundary to stabilize may exceed θ\_sep(t); how large the margin is remains undetermined in this chapter and is registered as an open problem. Otherwise, the difference is compressed back into the old class.

Therefore: the more Deep Nuclei (the higher Dn), under Assumption A2 and with other factors equal, the larger the rate of change of the threshold; if the threshold actually rises (an additional premise), the harder it is to form new reality, because old structures continuously expand their range of interpretation.

1\. Resolution Collapse

If θ\_sep(t) persistently rises along the actual history (an additional premise), more and more fine categories fall to or below the Separability Threshold.

Thus: fine categories become inseparable and are classed as the same. Whether small Domains actually merge and whether fine boundaries disappear must be certified separately by structural conditions.

This is called: Resolution Collapse. This chapter’s operational definition is: the set of separable pairs shrinks as θ\_sep rises (Theorem 4).

After Resolution Collapse (interpretive), reality increasingly tends toward: Familiar Routes, Co-Nuclei, broad-class compression, and low-resolution structures.

Under Assumption A2, and when the threshold actually rises, the more reality depends on Deep Nuclei, the harder it is to continue sustaining new fracturing.

Therefore: reality does not always increase in complexity; reality also continuously compresses back on itself.

1\. Reality Oscillation

If the field continues fracturing, boundaries continuously increase. Tuning Load rises and the rate of change of the threshold increases; if the threshold thereby actually rises, the field begins to compress back, reality recompresses.

But after compression, new Pulses still continuously enter the field, and new boundaries form again.

In this chapter’s minimal model (Theorem 5; constant input, constant c): if c ≥ 0, the Separability Threshold rises in one direction, entering unidirectional collapse; if c \< 0 and an interior equilibrium exists, trajectories near the equilibrium may show damped back-and-forth (a local conclusion); the system may also rest at a lower-bound equilibrium. These are results under stated conditions, not an exhaustive classification. Sustained periodic oscillation is not given by the minimal model (the absence of closed orbits is proved only for constant input and trajectories lying wholly in the interior region). Hence “reality is always in dynamic oscillation among differentiation, convergence, compression back, and re-fracturing” is not a conclusion of this chapter’s model; this chapter gives only the conditions for back-and-forth and equilibrium.

Separability Dynamics Equations (illustrative model, minimal computable approximation):

∂θ\_sep/∂t = α\_B·B\_d + α\_N·N − α\_C·(Cap−L) + α\_Dn·Dn

where α\_B, α\_N, α\_Dn \> 0 (positively drive θ\_sep upward), α\_C \> 0 (Residual Capacity positively suppresses θ\_sep). The term α\_Dn·Dn is Assumption A2: the effect of Deep Nucleus Density on the rate of change of the threshold is positive.

Lower-bound constraint: θ\_sep(t) ≥ θ\_min \> 0. θ\_min is a uniform lower bound declared by this chapter (Field Chapter 8 gives only θ\_sep \> 0, not a uniform lower bound). When θ\_sep = θ\_min and the right-side sum is negative, take ∂θ\_sep/∂t = 0, i.e., θ\_sep stays at θ\_min rather than falling to zero or a negative value.

∂B\_d/∂t = μ\_B·I\_in − ν\_B·ψ(θ\_sep)·B\_d

where I\_in ≥ 0 is the external Pulse input rate, μ\_B \> 0 (input drives boundary formation), ν\_B \> 0; ψ is continuously differentiable and nondecreasing, with ψ(θ\_min) = ψ\_min \> 0 (rising θ\_sep accelerates boundary degradation/merging) (Note: v1.1 used μ and φ, but μ is already used in Field Chapter 4 and φ is already defined in Field Chapter 7 as the Local Resolution Competition Function; this chapter therefore uses μ\_B and ψ).

Let c = α\_N·N − α\_C·(Cap−L) + α\_Dn·Dn. In this chapter’s minimal model, N, L, Dn, and Cap are taken as given (their coupling equations are left to subsequent chapters), and c is a constant.

The two equations coupled form the minimal dynamical closure: fracturing (B\_d ↑) → θ\_sep ↑ → B\_d accelerates degradation → when B\_d \< −c/α\_B (possible only if c \< 0), θ\_sep tends downward → new boundaries regenerate. Under stated conditions, one obtains unidirectional rise, local damped back-and-forth near an interior equilibrium, or a lower-bound equilibrium (Theorem 5); this is not an exhaustive classification. The precise coupled form (including equations for L and Dn) is left to subsequent chapters.

Note: these equations are only a minimal-order approximation and do not assert that all fields necessarily exhibit periodic oscillation. Under constant input and constant c, the minimal model has no closed orbit lying wholly in the interior region (Theorem 5(c)); oscillation driven by time-varying input (for example, periodic input) and trajectories touching the lower bound are not covered by this conclusion. Under conditions of strong external perturbation, strong saturation, or Deep Nucleus lock-in, the system may also enter unidirectional collapse or a long-term low-resolution steady state.

1\. Sustainable Reality

Reality does not tend toward complete maintenance. In this chapter’s model: under persistent input, the cumulatively generated boundaries grow without bound, while the density of simultaneously maintained boundaries is bounded (Theorem 6); hence of continuously generated fracturing, only a share tending to zero can be maintained simultaneously.

Therefore: what reality truly tends toward is not maximum fracturing, but maximum sustainable fracturing (interpretive. This chapter’s model neither defines nor solves a maximization problem, so this claim is not established).

Thus: reality is not the complete unfolding of all continuously generated differences in the field. Reality is only: the stable fracturing that the field can maintain over the long term under finite resources.

1\. Summary Expression

General Separability Dynamics Expression:

θ\_sep(t) = Θ\[B\_d, N, L, Dn, Cap\](t)

meaning that θ\_sep depends on the history of these quantities up to time t (dependence notation, not a state function). The direction marks below are read, per the illustrative equation of Section 9, as the direction of effect on ∂θ\_sep/∂t:

where:

B\_d: Boundary Density. B\_d ↑ → θ\_sep tends to rise.  
N: Noise Intensity. N ↑ → θ\_sep tends to rise.  
L: Tuning Load. L ↑ → θ\_sep tends to rise.  
Dn: Deep Nucleus Density. Dn ↑ → θ\_sep tends to rise (Assumption A2: Deep Nucleus flow occupation strengthens, new differences harder to sustain). Note: the original v1.0 used D, but D is already defined in Field Chapter 6 as Stable Differentiation Degree D(s,t); this chapter therefore uses Dn.  
Cap: Capacity limit. Cap ↑ → θ\_sep tends to fall (the greater the capacity, the more boundaries it can support).

And:

Illustrative Assumption H\_F: Flow(x\_i, t) = k\_F·δ(x\_i, t)·σ(G(x\_i, t)); with the same k\_F and G, Flow is nondecreasing in δ, strictly increasing with a common G \> 0, and 0 for both when G ≤ 0; with δ bounded, G → 0 makes Flow approach cutoff. Precedence follows Field Chapter 1, Law 5.

Therefore: in the sense of precedence, under C₌ subsequent flows continue along deep paths no later than along shallow ones, and strictly first when P₁₇ also holds (Law 5, Theorem 17); old structures preferentially receive subsequent input in this sense.

Thus: reality is not passively presented. Reality is: a dynamic stable structure in the process of continuous compression, reconstruction, and continuation under finite separability capacity.

1\. Theorems

The following theorems hold under the illustrative model of Section 9 and the premises listed here; for B\_d and θ\_sep, solutions are assumed to exist over the period considered.

Theorem 1: Threshold Drift Rate Theorem (within the illustrative model)

Under the Separability Dynamics Equations, when θ\_sep \> θ\_min, ∂θ\_sep/∂t is strictly increasing in B\_d, N, L, and Dn, and strictly decreasing in Cap. That is, when B\_d rises, N rises, Residual Capacity (Cap−L) falls, or Dn rises, the rate of change of θ\_sep increases.

Proof sketch: by the Separability Dynamics Equations, ∂(∂θ\_sep/∂t)/∂B\_d = α\_B \> 0; with respect to N it is α\_N \> 0, to L it is α\_C \> 0, to Dn it is α\_Dn \> 0, and to Cap it is −α\_C \< 0. ∎

This theorem reads only the direction of the rate of change; whether θ\_sep itself rises depends on the sign of the right-side sum. A1 specifies the direction of effect on the rate of change of the threshold with other factors equal; neither it nor this theorem guarantees that the threshold rises along the actual history.

Assumption 2: Deep Nucleus Density Assumption (formerly Theorem 2; this chapter’s model Assumption A2)

The effect of Deep Nucleus Density Dn on the rate of change of the Separability Threshold is positive: in the illustrative equation, α\_Dn \> 0. It does not guarantee that the threshold rises along the actual history.

Explanation (interpretive chain, not a proof): Dn ↑ (more Deep Nuclei in the field) → Deep Nuclei receive the flow no later than the shallower (Field Chapter 1, Law 5, under C₌; strictly first when P₁₇ also holds, Theorem 17), and precedence does not imply more flow; the amount of flow follows H\_F: with the same k\_F and G it is nondecreasing in δ, and strictly increasing with a common G \> 0 → new Pulses’ Trace-Effects are more easily absorbed by old Nuclei → new difference d(s\_new, s\_old) finds it harder to exceed θ\_sep(t) and be established. The link between “absorption by old Nuclei” and “a rising Separability Threshold” in this chain is not derived, so it is declared as an assumption.

Theorem 3: Separable-Pair Shrinkage Theorem (within the illustrative model; formerly the Commonality Expansion Theorem)

Let the difference d(s₁,s₂) of each pair in a set of Pulse pairs P be fixed on \[t₀, t₁\], and let θ\_sep be nondecreasing on \[t₀, t₁\]. Let the set of separable pairs be S(t) = {(s₁,s₂) ∈ P : d(s₁,s₂) \> θ\_sep(t)}. Then S(t₁) ⊆ S(t₀); pairs satisfying θ\_sep(t₀) \< d(s₁,s₂) ≤ θ\_sep(t₁) are separable at t₀ and, at t₁, inseparable and classed as the same.

Proof sketch: if d \> θ\_sep(t₁), then d \> θ\_sep(t₁) ≥ θ\_sep(t₀); the rest is read directly from Field Chapter 8’s separability condition (d \> θ\_sep ⟺ separable). ∎

This theorem gives only that fine boundaries become inseparable and are classed as the same; “large quantities of new Pulses compressed into the same Deep Nucleus → θ\_sep rises” requires, besides Assumptions A1 and A2, the additional condition that the total driving term is positive and the threshold rises along the actual history; whether fine Domains merge into a Co-Nucleus and whether Co-Nucleus coverage expands must be certified separately by the structural conditions of Field Chapter 1, Definition 15.

Theorem 4: Resolution Collapse Theorem (within the illustrative model)

Under the premises of Theorem 3, let the solution exist on \[t₀, ∞) and θ\_sep(t) be nondecreasing on \[t₀, ∞). Let the long-term separable set be S\_∞ = ∩\_{t ≥ t₀} S(t). (a) If θ\_sep(t) → θ\_∞ \< ∞: pairs with d \> θ\_∞ are always separable and belong to S\_∞; pairs with d \< θ\_∞ are eventually inseparable; for pairs with d = θ\_∞, if θ\_sep(t) \< θ\_∞ for all t (the threshold approaches from below), they are separable at every finite time and belong to S\_∞, while if θ\_sep(T) = θ\_∞ for some T, they are inseparable from T on. Hence S\_∞ ⊇ {d \> θ\_∞}, and the two may differ only on pairs with d = θ\_∞: the long-term separable set need not equal the set obtained by substituting the limiting threshold into the criterion. (b) If θ\_sep(t) → ∞, then for any finite P there exists T such that S(t) is empty for t ≥ T, i.e., all fine categories in P are inseparable.

Proof sketch: (a) for d \> θ\_∞, d \> θ\_∞ ≥ θ\_sep(t) for all t; for d \< θ\_∞, by convergence there exists T with θ\_sep(T) \> d, and thereafter by monotonicity θ\_sep(t) ≥ θ\_sep(T) \> d; the two cases of d = θ\_∞ are read directly from Field Chapter 8’s separability condition. Example: θ\_sep(t) = 2 − e^(−t), d = 2; this pair is separable at every finite time yet does not belong to {d \> 2}. (b) P is finite; take d\_max = max d; as θ\_sep → ∞ there exists T with θ\_sep(T) ≥ d\_max. ∎

This theorem defines Resolution Collapse by the shrinkage of the long-term separable set; it gives only inseparability and classing as the same, while actual merging of small Domains and physical disappearance of fine boundaries are certified separately by structural conditions. The claim in v1.1’s proof that “Field Chapter 7’s degradation terms dominate and δ for fine boundaries persistently declines” is not derived by this chapter’s model and has been removed.

Theorem 5: Equilibrium and Back-and-Forth Theorem for the Minimal Model (within the illustrative model; formerly the Reality Oscillation Theorem)

In the minimal model of Section 9, let c be a constant and I\_in \> 0 a constant; for conclusions involving t → ∞, let the solution exist on \[0, ∞).

(a) If c ≥ 0: for B\_d \> 0, ∂θ\_sep/∂t \> 0, θ\_sep rises strictly monotonically, and there is no interior equilibrium; if c \> 0, θ\_sep(t) ≥ θ\_sep(0) + c·t → ∞ (unidirectional collapse, combined with Theorem 4).

(b) If c \< 0: let B\* = −c/α\_B \> 0. If there exists θ\* \> θ\_min with ψ(θ*) = μ\_B·I\_in/(ν\_B·B*) and ψ′(θ*) \> 0, then (θ*, B*) is an interior equilibrium and is locally asymptotically stable (a local conclusion, not global convergence from arbitrary initial states); when (ν\_B·ψ(θ*))² \< 4·α\_B·ν\_B·ψ′(θ*)·B*, the equilibrium is a stable focus and nearby trajectories show damped back-and-forth (fracturing and compression back alternate with decaying amplitude). c \< 0 by itself guarantees neither the existence of an interior equilibrium nor damped back-and-forth for all trajectories.

(c) In the region {θ\_sep \> θ\_min, B\_d \> 0}, the minimal model (constant input, constant c) has no closed orbits, i.e., no sustained periodic oscillation lying wholly in that region. This conclusion covers neither oscillation driven by time-varying input (for example, periodic input) nor trajectories touching the lower bound.

(d) Lower-bound equilibrium: let B\_m = μ\_B·I\_in/(ν\_B·ψ\_min). If α\_B·B\_m + c ≤ 0, then (θ\_min, B\_m) is an equilibrium on the lower bound: θ\_sep stays at θ\_min (by the lower-bound rule) and B\_d = B\_m. Example: θ\_min = 0.1, ψ(θ) = θ, α\_B = μ\_B = ν\_B = I\_in = 1, c = −20; then B\_m = 10, α\_B·B\_m + c = −10 ≤ 0, and (0.1, 10) is a lower-bound equilibrium, neither damped back-and-forth nor unidirectional rise.

Proof sketch: (a) ∂θ\_sep/∂t = α\_B·B\_d + c \> 0; for c \> 0, integrate. (b) The equilibrium conditions are α\_B·B\* + c = 0 and μ\_B·I\_in = ν\_B·ψ(θ*)·B*. The Jacobian is \[\[0, α\_B\], \[−ν\_B·ψ′(θ*)·B*, −ν\_B·ψ(θ*)\]\], with trace −ν\_B·ψ(θ*) \< 0 and determinant α\_B·ν\_B·ψ′(θ*)·B* \> 0, hence locally asymptotically stable; the eigenvalues are complex exactly when trace² \< 4·determinant. (c) The divergence of the vector field is ∂/∂θ\_sep(α\_B·B\_d + c) + ∂/∂B\_d(μ\_B·I\_in − ν\_B·ψ(θ\_sep)·B\_d) = −ν\_B·ψ(θ\_sep) \< 0; the region is simply connected, so by Bendixson’s criterion there is no closed orbit in it. ∎

Hence “the field does not remain at absolute unity or infinite fracturing, but is always in dynamic oscillation” is not derived by the minimal model. Under the stated conditions, the minimal model gives: unidirectional rise (a); local damped back-and-forth near an interior equilibrium (b); a lower-bound equilibrium (d). This is not an exhaustive classification of all trajectories: for c \< 0 an interior equilibrium may not exist, global convergence is not proved, and behavior under time-varying input is not addressed. Sustained oscillation requires further coupling (equations for L, Dn, etc.) and is registered as an open problem.

Theorem 6: Boundary Density Boundedness Theorem (within the illustrative model; formerly the Sustainable Reality Theorem)

Let 0 ≤ I\_in(t) ≤ Ī\_in, B\_d(0) ≥ 0, θ\_sep ≥ θ\_min, and ψ nondecreasing with ψ(θ\_min) = ψ\_min \> 0. Then:

(a) Within the range where the solution exists, for all t ≥ 0, 0 ≤ B\_d(t) ≤ max( B\_d(0), μ\_B·Ī\_in/(ν\_B·ψ\_min) ).

(b) Let the cumulative generation be Γ(t) = ∫₀ᵗ μ\_B·I\_in(s)ds. If the solution exists on \[0, ∞) and moreover I\_in(t) ≥ I\_low \> 0, then Γ(t) → ∞ while B\_d(t)/(B\_d(0) + Γ(t)) → 0: of the cumulatively generated boundaries, the share maintained simultaneously tends to zero.

Proof sketch: (a) when B\_d = 0, ∂B\_d/∂t = μ\_B·I\_in ≥ 0, so B\_d stays nonnegative; from ψ(θ\_sep) ≥ ψ\_min, ∂B\_d/∂t ≤ μ\_B·Ī\_in − ν\_B·ψ\_min·B\_d, and the comparison principle gives the result. (b) Γ(t) ≥ μ\_B·I\_low·t → ∞, and the numerator is bounded by (a). ∎

This theorem gives, within the model, “the field cannot simultaneously maintain all continuously generated fracturing”; “reality tends toward maximum sustainable fracturing” is an interpretive view, and since this chapter’s model neither defines nor solves a maximization problem, it is not established. The chain in v1.1’s proof “B\_d → ∞ → L → C → θ\_sep persistently rises” does not fit this model (in the model B\_d is bounded) and has been removed.
