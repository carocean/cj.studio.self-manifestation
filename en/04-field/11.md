# Familiar Route Measure Equations

1\. Purpose

The preceding chapters have established:

\- how Deep Nuclei occupy flow;  
\- how Closure forms;  
\- how re-entry stabilizes;  
\- why reality can persist over the long term.

Yet the preceding chapters have not truly explained: why some paths are far more easily traversed.

Why:

\- certain structures are extremely hard to break through;  
\- certain realities are extremely stable;  
\- certain memories surface at a single touch;  
\- certain paths persistently dominate flow;  
\- certain structures continuously occupy large quantities of subsequent flow.

Because: not all paths have the same continuation advantage.

Depth is what familiarity rests on: the deeper may be called familiar (Outer Chapter 3). But which path prevails in continuation competition involves, besides depth, re-entry, continuation cost, and flow share.

This chapter therefore discusses: how Familiar Routes are determined and compared (per Field Chapter 1, Definition 13). And: how continuation competition is measured (this chapter sets up a separate Continuation Competition Index, which is not the definition of familiarity). The status of each conclusion is given in the “Status Table of Conclusions” at the end of Section 12.

1\. Core Proposition

Familiar is a name for the deeper (Outer Chapter 3: “compared with old knowledge it is called more familiar, so the deeper may be called familiar”; Inner Chapter 9: “its deep accumulation sits at the top, and is called familiar; hence the familiar comes out first”).

A Familiar Route is a commonly familiar route: one with existing deep sedimentation along which signals commonly travel (Outer Chapter 3: “signals travel easily and it is commonly familiar; this is what is called the commonly familiar route”; Field Chapter 1, Definition 13).

Coming out first is a manifestation of familiarity, not its definition (Field Chapter 1, Definition 13 and Law 5): under the comparison conditions C₌, the deeper satisfies the outflow condition no later than the shallower; when P₁₇ also holds, strictly first (Theorem 17). Outer Chapter 3: “the familiar coming out first is also what is meant by the deeper coming out first.”

Thus: comparison of “more familiar” follows Sedimentation-Degree δ; determination of a “Familiar Route” follows δ \> 0 and a frequent-travel record within the declared window. The other factors of continuation competition (re-entry, continuation cost, flow share) are measured separately in this chapter by the Continuation Competition Index and are not merged into the definition of familiarity.

1\. Familiar Route Determination and the Continuation Competition Index

Let q\_i denote a path in the field (consistent with Field Chapter 4’s path symbol q).

Hierarchical relation between q\_i and x\_i: q\_i is a path sequence connecting multiple local Domain positions x\_i; x\_i is a single local Domain position (Field Chapters 7, 9, 10). Field Chapter 4’s path q is the carrier of Coordination Compression Ω\_c(q,t).

Path Sedimentation-Degree statistic: δ̄(q\_i, t) is a path statistic newly set up in this chapter, obtained from the Sedimentation-Degrees of the Structural Positions along the path (Field Chapter 1, Definition 9) by a declared aggregation protocol (for example, the minimum along the path or a weighted mean). Within one comparison set, a fixed common aggregation protocol is used; weights and path extent are declared in advance and may not change with the result of the comparison. Different aggregations can give different orders: for example, with Sedimentation-Degrees along the path of (10, 0) for q₁ and (4, 4) for q₂, the mean judges q₁ deeper, and the minimum judges q₂ deeper. Hence the order of δ̄ is meaningful only under the declared protocol.

Familiar Route determination (comparison per Field Chapter 1, Definition 13 and Operation 11; qualification is this chapter’s path-level operationalization):

\- Comparison of “more familiar”: within a declared comparison set K (a set of paths comparable in kind) and under the declared aggregation protocol, this chapter uses δ̄ as the operational comparison of “more familiar” for paths: q₁ is more familiar than q₂ if and only if δ̄(q₁, t) \> δ̄(q₂, t). Its correspondence to Field Chapter 1’s outflow comparison rests on Bridge Assumption H\_δ (Section 11).  
\- Determination of a “Familiar Route” (this chapter’s path-level operational qualification): for the declared paths (the range of paths examined being declared in advance), q\_i is a Familiar Route if and only if the Sedimentation-Degree at every Structural Position along the path is greater than 0, and the frequent-travel record of the whole path within the declared window W meets the criterion (Pass\_W reaches the declared frequent-travel criterion). The requirement of positivity at every position is an operational condition newly added in this chapter, used to exclude cases where a mean masks positions with zero sedimentation (it does not depend on the aggregation protocol of δ̄); it is not a general requirement proved by the Inner and Outer Chapters or by Field Chapter 1: Field Chapter 1, Definition 13 requires only “existing deep sedimentation” and a path along which signals commonly travel.  
\- A path in K with no deeper path is called “the most familiar in K” (MaxFam\_K), used only for comparison, not for Familiar Route determination.

Relation to Field Chapter 1’s Bias: Bias (Field Chapter 1, Equation 11) requires (4a) a skewed depth distribution and (4b) actual stable biased continuation over a long window to hold jointly. Familiar Route determination and Bias are different determinations: a Familiar Route need not be accompanied by Bias, and Bias is not derived from a Familiar Route alone. This chapter sets up no relation between the two.

Relation to Field Chapter 4’s Ω\_c: Ω\_c(q,t) is the effect quantity of tuning sedimentation under the same reference conditions (Field Chapter 4 v1.2); it is not δ, not a graded form of Ω, and high Ω\_c does not mean low current Continuation Cost 𝒵. Comparison of familiarity follows δ̄ (which carries over δ under H\_δ), not Ω\_c.

Continuation Competition Index (newly set up in this chapter, not the definition of familiarity):

If:

\- its Sedimentation-Degree δ̄(q\_i,t) persistently deepens;  
\- its re-entry ratio Reentry(q\_i,t) rises;  
\- its Continuation Cost 𝒵(q\_i,t) persistently decreases;  
\- its Flow Share FlowShare(q\_i,t) persistently increases;

then: the factors by which this path prevails in continuation competition increase. This chapter summarizes these factors by the Continuation Competition Index family CI(q\_i, t) (Section 11). It measures overall advantage in continuation competition and is not a degree of familiarity: comparison of familiarity follows δ̄ only.

1\. A Familiar Route Is Not Closure

Closure denotes: the formation of a stable re-entry ring among multiple Domains (Field Chapter 1, Equation 16).

A Familiar Route denotes: a path with existing deep sedimentation along which signals commonly travel.

Therefore: Closure does not equal a Familiar Route. Closure can increase re-entry and thereby affect the re-entry term of the Continuation Competition Index; but Closure does not constitute the definition of familiarity.

Hence: the Continuation Competition Index depends on: Sedimentation-Degree; re-entry; continuation cost; flow share. Comparison of familiarity depends only on Sedimentation-Degree.

Therefore: familiar is a name for the deeper; overall advantage in continuation competition is measured separately by CI.

1\. Familiar Routes Come Out First

If q₁ is more familiar than q₂ (δ̄(q₁) \> δ̄(q₂)) and Bridge Assumption H\_δ holds, then: under the path-level comparison conditions, q₁ satisfies the outflow condition no later than q₂; with a strict threshold order and the path-level P₁₇ in addition, q₁ satisfies it strictly first (Theorem 1, following the argument of Field Chapter 1, Theorem 17).

Coming out first does not imply occupying more flow. How much instantaneous flow is occupied is treated separately in this chapter under the newly added path-level Illustrative Assumption H\_F^q (Section 11) (Theorem 4); flow occupation in future windows is not proved in this chapter.

Positive-Feedback Assumption (Assumption H\_fb, model assumption): compare two paths q₁ and q₂ in the declared set of paths; take successive windows W₁ = \[t − W, t\], W₂ = (t, t + h\], W₃ = (t + h, t + 2h\]. H\_fb consists of the following three links, each a separate assumption:

(L1) Flow → net deepening: if within a window the cumulative flow of q₁ exceeds that of q₂, then within that window the net increment of δ̄ for q₁ exceeds that for q₂. (This link requires that differences in degradation and migration do not offset the flow difference; it is not proved in this chapter and is taken as an assumption.)

(L2) More familiar → flow in the next window: if at the end of a window δ̄(q₁) \> δ̄(q₂), then within the next window the cumulative flow of q₁ exceeds that of q₂. (This link is not a conclusion of the coming-out-first theorem: coming out first concerns only precedence, not flow; nor does it follow from the instantaneous Theorem 4, since δ̄ and the gates may change in the next window.)

(L3) Flow → re-entry: if within a window the cumulative flow of q₁ exceeds that of q₂, then within that window the mean re-entry ratio of q₁ exceeds that of q₂.

When L1 and L2 hold, one obtains the proposition (under H\_fb): if δ̄(q₁, t) \> δ̄(q₂, t), then δ̄(q₁, t + h) − δ̄(q₂, t + h) \> δ̄(q₁, t) − δ̄(q₂, t) \> 0, i.e., the order of being more familiar is maintained at t + h and the gap widens. Proof: by L2, q₁ has more cumulative flow within W₂; by L1, q₁ has the larger net increment within W₂; subtracting at both ends gives the result. ∎

H\_fb contains no condition of decreasing continuation cost, so it does not imply “low-cost traversal”; what it implies is that the relative gap of q₂ widens, not that the Sedimentation-Degree of q₂ actually shallows.

1\. Familiar Routes and Reality Stability (interpretive)

The reason large numbers of stable structures in reality can persist over the long term is not merely that they have been repeatedly impacted, but that they have formed Familiar Routes.

Therefore (interpretive): in the sense of precedence, subsequent flows long continue along these paths no later than along other paths (under H\_δ and the comparison conditions).

Thus (interpretive): reality acquires: stable continuation; rapid re-entry; long-term flow occupation. These are interpretive views, not structural or observer-level conclusions derived from H\_fb or Field Chapter 9’s Assumption A2; “low-cost traversal” would require a further condition of decreasing continuation cost, which this chapter does not set up.

Therefore (interpretive): reality’s stability is the result of certain paths having long prevailed in precedence and in flow occupation.

1\. Familiar Routes and Reality Conservatism (interpretive)

If a Familiar Route persistently occupies large quantities of subsequent flow, then: under a common gate, the instantaneous flow share of a shallow new path is limited (Theorem 5). Thus (interpretive): new fracturing is hard to sustain; new boundaries are hard to stabilize; new paths are hard to grow. These three are interpretive views, not derived from Theorem 5, nor conclusions derived from H\_fb or Field Chapter 9’s Assumption A2.

Therefore (interpretive): Familiar Routes not only sustain reality, they may also suppress the stabilization of new fracturing.

Hence (interpretive): reality has: historical inertia; Familiar Route bias (Familiar Route locking per Form Chapter 20 v1.2); path dependence; resistance to sudden change.

1\. Familiar Route Degradation

If: re-entry weakens; flow occupation falls; tuning destabilizes; sedimentation begins to degrade; then: a path that previously had Familiar Route qualification may lack the qualification at some later time (the Sedimentation-Degree at some Structural Position along the path falls to 0, or the frequent-travel record within the window no longer meets the criterion), or may no longer be more familiar in comparison (the order of δ̄ changes).

This is called: Familiar Route Degradation (Theorem 2).

1\. Migration of the Familiar Route Order

If a new path persistently receives subsequent flow and forms stable re-entry, then (per Assumption H\_fb): its Sedimentation-Degree may continuously deepen. Meanwhile, old paths may degrade as their flow occupation decreases. Thus: the order of “more familiar” in the comparison set may change (Theorem 3). A change of order does not mean the new path has acquired Familiar Route qualification or dominance.

Therefore: reality is not eternally fixed. The dominant paths in reality change as historical continuation proceeds (interpretive).

1\. Depth Differential and Competition Index Differential

What compares “more familiar” is the Sedimentation-Degree differential between paths:

Δδ̄(q₁, q₂) = δ̄(q₁) − δ̄(q₂)

Δδ̄ \> 0 means q₁ is more familiar than q₂ (under the declared aggregation protocol).

The overall differential in continuation competition is expressed by the competition index differential (with the same Φ and input protocol fixed):

ΔCI(q₁, q₂) = CI(q₁) − CI(q₂)

If Δδ̄(q₁, q₂) \> 0, then under H\_δ and C₌ q₁ comes out no later than q₂ (Theorem 1); under H\_F^q, if k\_F is the same and the common gate value σ̄ \> 0, the instantaneous flow difference Flow(q₁) − Flow(q₂) = k\_F·σ̄·Δδ̄ is proportional to the depth differential (Theorem 4).

How large the time difference in coming out first is, and how easily the order migrates, are not proved in this chapter; the relation between ΔCI and subsequent flow allocation is not set up in this chapter; all are registered as open problems.

1\. Summary Expression

Continuation Competition Index family (an index family newly set up in this chapter, not the definition of familiarity):

CI(q\_i, t) = Φ(δ̄(q\_i,t), Reentry(q\_i,t), 𝒵(q\_i,t), FlowShare(q\_i,t))

where Φ is not yet fixed as a unique function; every Φ meeting the following construction requirements belongs to the family: with other components fixed, Φ is nondecreasing in δ̄, Reentry, and FlowShare, and nonincreasing in 𝒵. When CI is actually computed or compared, the same Φ, the same input reading protocol, and the same scale protocol must be fixed. These directions are construction requirements of the index, not empirical claims about continuation; separating time windows avoids reusing the same result but does not prove that CI has predictive power.

Inputs:

δ̄(q\_i, t): path Sedimentation-Degree statistic (declared aggregation protocol, see Section 3).

Reentry(q\_i, t): re-entry ratio. This chapter adopts Field Chapter 10 v1.1’s definition: for a local Domain x, Reentry(x, t) = the total subsequent flow re-entering x after being channeled through other Domains in the field ÷ the direct new input; it is defined only when the direct new input is greater than 0. The path Reentry(q\_i, t) is the weighted sum, with declared weights, of the Reentry of the x along the path, and is defined only when it is defined at every x along the path. If Field Chapter 10 changes this definition on upgrade, this chapter will be checked accordingly.

𝒵(q\_i, t): the Continuation Cost of path q\_i (referencing Field Chapter 4 v1.2’s 𝒵(q,t), formerly named “Continuation Impedance”).

FlowShare(q\_i, t): flow share. Let Q be a declared finite set of paths (its counting declared in advance), with each path flow Flow(q, t) ≥ 0 and total flow F\_Q(t) = Σ\_{q∈Q} Flow(q, t). FlowShare(q\_i, t) = Flow(q\_i, t)/F\_Q(t) is defined only when 0 \< F\_Q(t) \< ∞; then FlowShare ∈ \[0,1\] and Σ\_{q∈Q} FlowShare(q) = 1. When F\_Q(t) = 0, FlowShare is marked “undefined” and the normalization identity is not used. Flow(q, t) is the subsequent flow on the path, whose aggregation must be declared (for example, a declared weighted sum along the path of Flow(x) under Field Chapter 9’s H\_F; H\_F already contains the gate, which is not multiplied again). When paths overlap, FlowShare is a share normalized over the declared path flows, not a non-duplicated allocation of the actual total input, and is not interpreted as resource occupation.

CI is defined only when all its inputs are defined and are finite real numbers.

Time protocol (declared): CI(q\_i, t) uses only readings at or before t, each term taken as follows: δ̄ and 𝒵 take their values at time t (end values); Reentry takes its mean over the window \[t − W, t\]; FlowShare takes the ratio of cumulative flows over the window \[t − W, t\]: ∫Flow(q\_i)ds / ∫F\_Q ds. Subsequent flow allocation used to test CI is taken in a later disjoint window (t, t + h\], likewise as a ratio of cumulative flows. The ratio of cumulative flows generally differs from the average of instantaneous shares; this chapter uses only the former. The same reading may not serve both as a basis of CI and as the outcome of the test. Validity conditions for window readings (applying equally to past windows and future test windows): (1) the path flows within the window are measurable and integrable, with 0 \< ∫\_window F\_Q(s)ds \< ∞; (2) Reentry satisfies its defining condition within the window (direct new input greater than 0), and its window mean is finite; (3) the path weighted sums, δ̄, 𝒵, and every reading input to Φ are finite real numbers. When these conditions fail, the corresponding window reading, CI, or FlowSkew is marked “undefined.”

Path-level Illustrative Assumption H\_F^q (newly added in this chapter): Flow(q\_i, t) = k\_F·δ̄(q\_i, t)·σ̄(q\_i, t), where k\_F \> 0 is a declared constant coefficient and σ̄(q\_i, t) ∈ \[0,1\] is the declared gate value of the path.

(Category: a path-level illustrative assumption newly added in this chapter, modeled on the form of Field Chapter 9’s H\_F; it is not a consequence of Field Chapter 9’s H\_F: a weighted sum of positional flows generally does not equal “aggregated Sedimentation-Degree × aggregated gate.”)

Path-level Bridge Assumption H\_δ (newly added in this chapter): within the declared comparison set K and aggregation protocol, the following two items hold: (i) cross-path threshold order, by one of the following conditions. (i-1) Within the same comparison set and with other conditions the same, all paths use a common function θ(q) = g(δ̄(q)), with g nonincreasing in δ̄; (i-2) or the paths compared are directly verified to satisfy the required cross-path threshold order: weak order θ(q₁) ≤ θ(q₂), strict order θ(q₁) \< θ(q₂). Each path using its own nonincreasing function does not suffice to give a cross-path threshold order: for example, with δ̄₁ = 2, δ̄₂ = 1, g₁(d) = 10 − d, g₂(d) = 2 − d, both strictly decreasing, yet θ₁ = 8 \> θ₂ = 1. (ii) Path-level comparison conditions: q₁ and q₂ are two routes under the same entrance, sharing an entrance pressure p that is nondecreasing step by step before either route opens; both routes are unblocked, a route opens if and only if p reaches its threshold, there is no difference in selective bias or tuning between them, and they are paths comparable in kind (the path-level counterpart of Field Chapter 1’s C₌). “Nonincreasing” is a weakened form chosen by this chapter, not a same-strength carrying over of the original “the deeper the Sedimentation-Degree, the smaller its threshold”: a weak threshold order supports only “no later than”; strictly first further requires a strict threshold order (for example, g strictly decreasing on \[δ̄(q₂), δ̄(q₁)\], or θ(q₁) \< θ(q₂) directly verified) and the path-level P₁₇. H\_δ is a bridge assumption of this chapter, not a conclusion derived from Field Chapter 1; the declaration “compare by δ̄” cannot by itself replace it.

And:

Δδ̄(q₁, q₂) = δ̄(q₁) − δ̄(q₂)

Flow Skew FlowSkew(q₁, q₂; t, h) = FlowShare\_win(q₁) − FlowShare\_win(q₂), where FlowShare\_win is the ratio of cumulative flows within the window (t, t + h\] (defined under the window validity conditions listed in the time protocol).

(v1.1’s “ContinuationBias ∝ ΔFamiliarity” is withdrawn: “continuation bias” has been defined by Form Chapter 20 v1.2 as the dependence B on sedimentation differences, and this chapter no longer uses that name; the proportionality has no basis. The relation of FlowSkew to the current Δδ̄ and ΔCI is not proved in this chapter.)

Therefore: under H\_δ and the comparison conditions, the more familiar path comes out no later than other paths; under H\_F^q with a common positive gate, the more familiar path occupies more instantaneous flow.

Thus (interpretive): continuation in reality does not unfold randomly. Reality is: the advantage structure of Familiar Routes formed by continuous competition among different paths in the field through historical continuation.

1\. Theorems

The following theorems hold under this chapter’s declarations (comparison set K, set of paths Q, aggregation protocol, window W, and frequent-travel criterion).

Theorem 1: Familiar Routes Come Out First Theorem (under Bridge Assumption H\_δ)

Within a declared comparison set K and aggregation protocol, let Bridge Assumption H\_δ hold and δ̄(q₁) \> δ̄(q₂). Let τ(q) be the step at which the entrance pressure p first reaches θ(q), +∞ if never. (a) Weak order: τ(q₁) ≤ τ(q₂), i.e., q₁ satisfies the outflow condition no later than q₂. (b) Strict precedence: if moreover the strict threshold order θ(q₁) \< θ(q₂) holds and p reaches θ(q₁) within the declared time window, with t₁ = τ(q₁), then the first opened set is {q₁} if and only if p(t₁) \< θ(q₂) (the path-level P₁₇).

Proof sketch: Step one, threshold order: by H\_δ (i-1), g is nonincreasing and δ̄(q₁) \> δ̄(q₂), so θ(q₁) = g(δ̄(q₁)) ≤ g(δ̄(q₂)) = θ(q₂); under (i-2) this threshold order is directly verified. Step two, order of first satisfaction (following the argument of Field Chapter 1, Theorem 17, step by step under H\_δ (ii)): (a) if τ(q₂) = +∞ the result is immediate; otherwise p(τ(q₂)) ≥ θ(q₂) ≥ θ(q₁), so p has reached θ(q₁) at or before τ(q₂), and τ(q₁) ≤ τ(q₂). (b) Before t₁, p \< θ(q₁) \< θ(q₂), and neither route is open; at t₁, q₁ opens; q₂ opens at the same time if and only if p(t₁) ≥ θ(q₂). ∎

This is the formal reading at the path level of Outer Chapter 3, “the familiar coming out first is also what is meant by the deeper coming out first,” its carrying over resting on H\_δ. This theorem concerns only precedence and does not imply occupying more flow (for instantaneous flow occupation see Theorem 4). v1.1’s proof (“higher sedimentation gives higher M”) disagrees with Field Chapter 7 v1.2 (M need not be monotone in δ, and M does not determine precedence) and has been withdrawn; the positive feedback has been made Assumption H\_fb (Section 5).

Theorem 2: Familiar Route Qualification Criterion

(a) q\_i lacks this chapter’s operational Familiar Route qualification at time t if and only if, within the declared range of paths, the Sedimentation-Degree at some Structural Position along the path is 0 (Sedimentation-Degree being nonnegative), or the frequent-travel record of q\_i within the window \[t − W, t\] does not meet the declared criterion.

(b) Loss of qualification: if q\_i has Familiar Route qualification at some t′ \< t and satisfies (a) at t, then q\_i is said to lose Familiar Route qualification within (t′, t\].

(c) If δ̄(q₁, ·) and δ̄(q₂, ·) are continuous, with δ̄(q₁) \> δ̄(q₂) at t₀ and δ̄(q₁) \< δ̄(q₂) at t₁, then at t₁ q₁ is no longer more familiar than q₂.

Proof sketch: (a) by negation of this chapter’s operational qualification (Section 3); (b) is a definition; (c) by the definition of “more familiar.” ∎

(a) also holds for paths that never had the qualification, so (a) is only a criterion for “lacking qualification”; “losing qualification” requires the premise of (b). How weakened re-entry, falling flow occupation, etc., lead to a decline in Sedimentation-Degree follows the model conditions of Field Chapter 7 and is not separately proved here.

Theorem 3: Familiar Route Order Migration Theorem

Let δ̄(q\_new, ·) and δ̄(q\_old, ·) be continuous on \[t₀, t₁\], with δ̄(q\_new, t₀) \< δ̄(q\_old, t₀) and δ̄(q\_new, t₁) \> δ̄(q\_old, t₁). Then there exists t\* ∈ (t₀, t₁) with δ̄(q\_new, t*) = δ̄(q\_old, t*), and at t₁ q\_new is more familiar than q\_old: the order of “more familiar” in the comparison set reverses.

Proof sketch: apply the intermediate value theorem to Δδ̄(t) = δ̄(q\_new, t) − δ̄(q\_old, t); at t₁, Δδ̄ \> 0, and the result follows from the definition of “more familiar.” ∎

This theorem proves only the reversal of order; it proves neither that q\_new acquires Familiar Route qualification (which further requires that the condition of Theorem 2(a) fail) nor that it acquires dominance. “q\_new persistently receives subsequent flow → deepens” rests on Assumption H\_fb and the model conditions of Field Chapter 7.

Theorem 4: Common-Gate Instantaneous Flow Allocation Theorem (under H\_F^q)

Under the path-level Illustrative Assumption H\_F^q, let q₁ and q₂ at time t have the same k\_F and a common gate value σ̄ \> 0. Then Flow(q₁, t) − Flow(q₂, t) = k\_F·σ̄·(δ̄(q₁, t) − δ̄(q₂, t)); q₁ is more familiar than q₂ if and only if Flow(q₁, t) \> Flow(q₂, t); when δ̄ is equal, the instantaneous flows are equal. When δ̄(q₂, t) \> 0, Flow(q₁, t)/Flow(q₂, t) = δ̄(q₁, t)/δ̄(q₂, t). When the common gate value is 0, Flow is 0 for both.

Proof sketch: compute directly from Flow = k\_F·δ̄·σ̄. ∎

This theorem concerns only instantaneous quantities at the same time; it does not imply FlowSkew in the future window (t, t + h\], since future δ̄ and gates may change. v1.1’s Theorem 4 (“ΔFamiliarity determines biased flow”) has been withdrawn; the relations of ΔCI and Δδ̄ to future FlowSkew are registered as open problems.

Theorem 5: Upper Bound on the Instantaneous Flow Share of a New Path (under H\_F^q)

Under H\_F^q, let the paths in a declared finite set Q have at time t the same k\_F and a common gate value σ̄ \> 0, with Σ\_{q∈Q} δ̄(q, t) \> 0. Then F\_Q(t) \> 0, FlowShare(q, t) is defined, and for q\_new ∈ Q, FlowShare(q\_new, t) = δ̄(q\_new, t) / Σ\_{q∈Q} δ̄(q, t). In particular, if ε ≥ 0 and δ̄(q\_new, t) ≤ ε·Σ\_{q∈Q, q≠q\_new} δ̄(q, t), then FlowShare(q\_new, t) ≤ ε/(1 + ε).

Proof sketch: F\_Q = k\_F·σ̄·Σδ̄ \> 0; substitute and cancel k\_F·σ̄; for the second part, let a = δ̄(q\_new) and b = the sum of the others; a/(a + b) is nondecreasing in a, and when a ≤ ε·b, a/(a + b) ≤ ε·b/(ε·b + b) = ε/(1 + ε) (Σδ̄ \> 0 together with a ≤ ε·b implies b \> 0). ∎

This theorem concerns only instantaneous shares, and shares are normalized over the declared path flows (not interpreted as resource occupation when paths overlap). “New paths cannot form stable sedimentation” and “new fracturing is suppressed” are not derived by this theorem. v1.1’s proof cited “Field Chapter 9 (Theorem 5),” which has been changed in Field Chapter 9 v1.2 to an equilibrium and back-and-forth theorem; the citation is withdrawn.

Note 6: On Bias and Ω\_c (formerly Theorem 6, not a theorem)

v1.1’s Theorem 6 claimed that “Familiarity and Ω\_c are directionally consistent,” with a proof relying on “high Ω\_c corresponds to deep δ and low 𝒵.” Per Field Chapter 4 v1.2: Ω\_c is the effect quantity of tuning sedimentation under the same reference conditions, sets up no correspondence with δ, and high Ω\_c does not mean low 𝒵. That theorem is therefore withdrawn. Comparison of familiarity follows δ̄, and comparison of current continuation cost compares 𝒵 directly. The relations of Familiar Routes to Bias (Field Chapter 1, Equation 11) and to Ω\_c are not set up in this chapter and are registered as open problems.
