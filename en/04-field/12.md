# Field Resolution Equations

1\. Purpose

The preceding chapters have established:

\- how Familiar Routes form;  
\- how Familiar Routes compete;  
\- how biased flow stabilizes;  
\- how history forms path dependence.

Yet the preceding chapters have not truly explained: why the field cannot differentiate without limit.

Why:

\- some differences can continue to form fine subdivisions;  
\- some differences can ultimately only merge into old Domains;  
\- some Domains can continuously generate new structures;  
\- some Domains ultimately cannot continue to differentiate.

Because: the existence of a difference does not mean the field can still stably sustain that difference.

Therefore this chapter discusses: how the field forms resolution. And: why a Domain reaches its resolution limit.

The Numericology says: “The striking does not cease, yet the field possesses physical limits. Domains must possess interstitial gaps for misalignment. When no further gaps for misalignment remain, the upper limit of differentiation is reached. Therefore this upper limit is called the capacity of the field.” This is the field-level upper limit of differentiation (Field Chapter 1, Definition 30, Cap(F)); this chapter discusses how it shows itself at the Domain level.

1\. Core Proposition

The Resolution Margin discussed in this chapter does not denote quantity of information, nor fineness of observation.

Resolution Res(F) in Field Chapter 1, Definition 29, denotes the number of differentiable Domains per unit volume, following the Numericology: “The number of domains per unit volume that may be differentially distinguished is called Resolution.” This chapter does not change that definition; it sets up, at the Domain level, a separate Resolution Margin.

The Resolution Margin means: how many additional differentials a Domain can still stably sustain given its existing sedimentation, overlap, and tuning conditions.

Therefore (the reading proposed in this chapter): if after a new difference enters the field it can still stably sustain its independent differential, that difference can continue to form a new subdivision. Conversely, if the field can no longer stably sustain a new independent differential, the new difference cannot be sustained as an additional subdivision, and its main outcome is to be compressed and merged into the existing Domain (other outcomes: see Theorem 1).

Therefore: the Resolution Margin discussed in this chapter is a Domain’s stable maintenance margin for new differentials; it does not replace Res(F), and its decline is not the same as Resolution Loss (LossRes) in Field Chapter 1, Definition 34.

1\. Resolution Margin

Let:

SDC\_f(t) denote the Stable Differentiation Capacity of Domain f at time t. Note: the original v1.0 used C\_f(t), but C is already defined in Field Chapter 7 as Local Capacity Load C(x,t) = ∫W(x−y)δ(y,t)dy; this chapter therefore uses SDC\_f(t). SDC\_f is measured in a declared unit (for example, the number of stable differential relations) and is read as: the upper limit of differentials Domain f can stably sustain while the Domain’s existing stable differentials are kept unchanged.

S\_f(t) denote the Compression Saturation of Domain f at time t—the amount of differential maintenance occupied in the Domain by Familiar Route compression, Co-Nucleus consolidation, and Closure locking. S\_f and SDC\_f use the same declared unit (if expressed as a proportion, SDC\_f must be normalized in the same way); each additional stable differential increases S\_f by a positive amount u. v1.1 called S\_f a “proportion of the Domain’s resolution” and subtracted it from SDC\_f without unifying their units; this version adds that declaration.

Relation to Field Chapter 7’s ρ(x,t): ρ(x,t) is the local Resolution Occupancy Pressure defined by Field Chapter 7’s Local Resolution Occupancy Equation ρ(x,t) = ∫K(x−y)δ(y,t)dy. S\_f is not defined by ρ; this chapter takes only a directional reading (a hypothesis): under a declared aggregation, the higher ρ within the Domain (the stronger the neighboring sedimentation pressure), the higher S\_f tends to be. The two are at different levels: ρ is at the spatial-point level, S\_f is at the Domain level; their quantitative relation is not established in this chapter.

Then:

R\_f(t) = SDC\_f(t) − S\_f(t)

R\_f(t) is called the Resolution Margin of the Domain.

Hierarchical relation to Field Chapter 7’s G(x,t): Field Chapter 7’s Residual Resolution Capacity G(x,t) = Λ(x) − ρ(x,t) is a spatial-level residual capacity (i.e., how much new difference local Domain position x can still absorb—a spatial measure); G ≤ 0 is called capacity fullness in Field Chapter 7 and is not Domain Fullness in Field Chapter 1, Definition 20. This chapter’s R\_f(t) = SDC\_f(t) − S\_f(t) is a differential-level Resolution Margin (i.e., how many new differential relations Domain f can still stably sustain—a structural measure). The two have the same directional reading (ample G and R\_f \> 0 both indicate remaining margin), but are at different levels: G is at the spatial-point level, R\_f is at the Domain level. v1.1 stated “G is the spatial-level prerequisite for R\_f—when G → 0, R\_f also tends toward ≤ 0”; this chapter has not established a quantitative relation between G and R\_f, so the sentence is withdrawn; if needed, an aggregation relation must first be set up and then proved.

Connection to Field Chapter 8’s θ\_sep: Field Chapter 8’s Separability Threshold θ\_sep is the field’s current separability threshold (d(s₁,s₂) \> θ\_sep means separable). This chapter proposes an effective within-Domain separability threshold θ\_sep,f as the local condition for differences to obtain within Domain f; it is not a quantity defined in Field Chapter 8, is not equivalent to the field-level θ\_sep, and single-Domain SDC\_f does not directly determine the field-level θ\_sep. This chapter’s directional hypothesis H\_θ: other conditions being equal, the higher SDC\_f, the lower θ\_sep,f tends to be (differences more easily obtain within the Domain); when SDC\_f falls, θ\_sep,f tends to rise. This is a hypothesis, not derived from Field Chapter 8; a decline in separability is also not the same as a rise in the threshold (Field Chapter 8 v1.2, Theorem 5).

Therefore (reading; see Theorem 1):

If R\_f(t) \> 0, Domain f still has differential maintenance margin; whether a new differential can form a new stable subdivision further requires the separability condition and the maintenance condition.

If R\_f(t) ≤ 0, then under the fixed-budget comparison (see Theorem 1) the Domain cannot continue to stably sustain new differentials while all its existing differentials are kept unchanged—this chapter calls this: Margin Exhaustion.

Relation to Domain Fullness in Field Chapter 1, Definition 20: the Numericology says: “If overlapping domains can deepen no further, then those domains can divide no further. Their inability to re-enter differentiation is called Domain Saturation.” (“Domain Saturation” in this translation is Domain Fullness in Field Chapter 1.) Definition 20 requires all three—no further deepening, no further division, no further admission of resolution—to hold together. Margin Exhaustion means only: under the budget and comparison conditions declared in this chapter, no new stable differential can be added while all existing differentials are kept unchanged; it still admits other outcomes such as displacement and reorganization. It does not replace the structural judgment of “no further division, no further admission of resolution” in Definition 20; these two and “no further deepening” must all be checked separately (for no further deepening, compare Sedimentary Saturation in Field Chapter 1, Definition 19, which itself does not equal Domain Fullness). Even if no further deepening is separately established, Domain Fullness cannot be certified from R\_f ≤ 0 alone. v1.1 directly called R\_f ≤ 0 Domain Saturation; this version renames it Margin Exhaustion.

1\. Differential Formation

Let Domain f already contain fa₁, fa₂, fa₃. New difference fa₄ now enters.

If R\_f(t) \> 0 and fa₄ satisfies Field Chapter 8’s separability condition d(fa₄, fa\_i) \> θ\_sep with each existing differential, fa₄ may form a new subdivision; whether it can be sustained long-term is judged separately by the maintenance condition (Theorem 1).

If R\_f(t) ≤ 0, then under the fixed-budget comparison the field can no longer stably sustain new differential relations while the existing differentials are kept unchanged; the main case discussed in this chapter is that fa₄ is gradually compressed into existing differential structure (other outcomes: see Theorem 1).

Therefore: differentials do not exist by nature. Differentials are: the persistent differential structure formed after the field has stably maintained them over the long term.

1\. Resolution Saturation

If R\_f(t) ≤ 0, then under the fixed-budget comparison new differentials can no longer form new stable subdivisions within the Domain while the existing differentials are kept unchanged. This chapter proposes: new differences are then continuously compressed, and the unabsorbed part gradually accumulates near the boundary (accumulation requires a separately declared retention and release mechanism). In Field Chapter 7’s illustrative model, what corresponds to this is the unabsorbed input R blocked by the capacity gate (R approaches or equals A when G → 0 or G ≤ 0), not the Local Source Surplus O.

Therefore: Resolution Saturation (Margin Exhaustion) is not too much information, nor too much structure—it is that the field can no longer stably sustain new differentials.

1\. Boundary Saturation Outgrowth Formation

R\_f(t) ≤ 0 together with new differentials continuing to enter does not by itself entail that P\_Δ increases over time. How the pressure changes is judged from its definition (below) and from changes in input, gating, and boundary weights: for example, if A, G, and ω on the boundary stay unchanged, P\_Δ can stay constant. Boundary accumulation requires a separately declared retention and release mechanism.

Let:

P\_Δ(f,t) denote the Boundary Differential Pressure of Domain f at time t—the intensity near the boundary of new differentials that cannot be absorbed by the Domain. This chapter defines it as the weighted boundary integral over Domain f of Field Chapter 7’s unabsorbed input R(x,t) = A·(1 − σ(G(x,t))):

P\_Δ(f,t) = ∫\_{∂f} ω(x)·R(x,t) dS

where ω(x) ≥ 0 is a declared weighting function at boundary position x (reflecting local boundary tuning stability and channeling direction), and dS is the boundary area element; ω and the boundary reading range must be declared in advance. P\_Δ(f,t) ≥ 0; its unit is fixed by the declared units of R and ω, and B\_f must use the same unit. To express accumulation, a window integral ∫\_{t−W}^{t} P\_Δ(f,s) ds may be declared separately, with the same reading rule in any comparison. v1.1 defined P\_Δ through Field Chapter 7’s O(x,t) (called “Structural Surplus” in v1.1, named Local Source Surplus in Field Chapter 7 v1.2) and handled dimensions with ∝; per Field Chapter 7 v1.2, at capacity fullness I\_eff = 0, and if B\_out is also 0 then O = 0, so O is not used as a transfer criterion—what is left unabsorbed is R. This version defines P\_Δ through R, with equality replacing proportionality.

B\_f(t) denote the Boundary Carrying Limit of Domain f at time t—the maximum Boundary Differential Pressure the boundary structure can stably compress and absorb under current tuning conditions (in the same unit as P\_Δ). B\_f(t) \> 0, with dependency written as:

B\_f(t) = Ψ(L\_f(t), B\_d(f,t), R\_f(t))

where L\_f(t) is the Tuning Load of Domain f and B\_d(f,t) is the boundary density of Domain f: L(t) and B\_d(t) in Field Chapter 9 v1.2 are field-level quantities, and this chapter’s Domain-level readings must have their aggregation or counting declared separately. R\_f(t) is the Resolution Margin. Directional hypothesis H\_Ψ: higher L\_f → lower B\_f; higher B\_d → lower B\_f (stronger inter-boundary mutual interference); lower R\_f → lower B\_f. This is a hypothesis of this chapter, not derived from Field Chapter 9. The precise functional form Ψ is left to subsequent chapters.

Then (Criterion 2, candidate condition for Boundary Saturation Outgrowth):

R\_f(t) ≤ 0 ∧ P\_Δ(f,t) \> B\_f(t) ⇒ Boundary Saturation Outgrowth candidate

The Numericology says: “Therefore if Edge-Seepage does not cross the threshold of the Line-Bundle at the boundary of a certain Domain, Edge-Seepage of that Domain does not arise.” (“Edge-Seepage” in this translation is Boundary Saturation Outgrowth in Field Chapter 1.) This chapter reads P\_Δ \> B\_f operationally as crossing the threshold of the boundary Line-Bundle: when P\_Δ ≤ B\_f no candidate is set; P\_Δ \> B\_f yields only a candidate. Certification of Boundary Saturation Outgrowth (Field Chapter 1, Definition 21, B(𝒟)=1) further requires: Domain Fullness already established (Definition 20, not merely Margin Exhaustion); new Trace-Effects continuing to enter and unable to continue internal sinking; overflow along the Domain boundary; generation of new Structural Positions.

Therefore: Boundary Saturation Outgrowth is not random spillover (Field Chapter 1, Definition 21: Boundary Saturation Outgrowth is not random diffusion). This chapter proposes: Boundary Saturation Outgrowth is the result of differential pressure exceeding the Boundary Carrying Limit and fracturing outward through the boundary after the Domain’s margin is exhausted; its certification follows Definition 21.

1\. Boundary Saturation Outgrowth and New Structures

This chapter proposes: after Boundary Saturation Outgrowth occurs, new differences that could not be stably sustained inside the old Domain begin to form new stable differentials outside the boundary. The Numericology says: “Thus when Edge-Seepage arises, fine classes are born.”

Thus (proposed): new fine categories begin to form; new Domains, new Familiar Routes, and new structures may appear in succession. New Domains must be certified by the five stability conditions of Field Chapter 1, Definition 8; new Structural Positions by Definition 21; new Familiar Routes by Definition 13; Field Chapter 7’s Criterion 5 (persistent growth of neighboring Sedimentation-Degree) is only a domain-formation candidacy condition.

Therefore (interpretation, following Field Chapter 1: “Successive Boundary Saturation Outgrowths generate new Structural Positions”): new structures are not generated from nothing. New structures are: the result of differentials fracturing outward after the old Domain can no longer stably sustain new differentials.

1\. Resolution and Familiar Routes

If a Familiar Route persistently occupies large quantities of subsequent flow (this is a premise; Theorem 1 of Field Chapter 11 v1.2, Familiar Routes Come Out First, gives only the order of outflow under comparison conditions, not long-term flow shares), this chapter proposes: existing differential structure becomes increasingly stable, while new differentials become increasingly hard to sustain stably.

This chapter expresses this by hypothesis H\_S: other drivers unchanged, deepening of a Familiar Route (a rise in the path Sedimentation-Degree statistic δ̄, Field Chapter 11 v1.2) increases S\_f(t). Under H\_S and the comparison with SDC\_f unchanged, R\_f(t) falls when δ̄ rises (Proposition 3). v1.1’s “the stronger the Familiar Route, the stronger the compression stability; thus S\_f(t) rises faster and R\_f(t) falls faster” is changed into this conditional statement; the rate comparison “faster” is not proved in this chapter.

Thus (interpretation): the field increasingly tends to sustain old compressed structures while finding it hard to continue forming new subdivisions.

1\. Resolution-Margin Collapse

If: Familiar Routes are long-term stable; new differences are long-term compressed; boundaries long-term cannot continue to fracture outward; tuning continuously coarsens; then this chapter proposes: the new differentials the field can stably sustain gradually decrease, while old compressed structures continuously strengthen.

Thus (proposed): SDC\_f(t) falls and S\_f(t) rises; if both hold in a declared window, R\_f(t) persistently falls in that window (Theorem 4).

A persistent fall of R\_f(t) in a declared window is called: Resolution-Margin Collapse (this chapter’s operational delimitation).

Note: Resolution-Margin Collapse differs from Resolution Collapse in Field Chapter 9 v1.2 (delimited by shrinkage of the long-term separable set S\_∞), and is not Resolution Loss in Field Chapter 1, Definition 34. A persistent fall entails neither Margin Exhaustion nor Resolution Collapse in the sense of Field Chapter 9 nor Resolution Loss.

1\. Resolution-Margin Recovery

If: old Familiar Routes begin to fracture; old compressed relations begin to loosen; boundaries reactivate; tuning refines again; then this chapter proposes: the field re-acquires the capacity to stably sustain new differentials.

Thus (proposed): differences that would previously have been compressed and merged into old Domains begin to be able to stably form new subdivisions when R\_f recovers and the separability and maintenance conditions are met.

Therefore (proposed): SDC\_f(t) rises, S\_f(t) falls, R\_f(t) recovers (Theorem 5).

A recovery of R\_f(t) in a declared window is called: Resolution-Margin Recovery (this chapter’s operational delimitation). Per Field Chapter 1, Definition 34, the field’s release of load does not necessarily restore stable differentiation; a recovery of R\_f is not the same as stable differentiation having been restored.

General Field Resolution Expression:

R\_f(t) = SDC\_f(t) − S\_f(t)

where R\_f is the Resolution Margin, SDC\_f is the Stable Differentiation Capacity, and S\_f is the Compression Saturation.

And (readings and conditional statements):

1\. Theorems

Theorem 1: Necessary Condition of the Resolution Margin (under the fixed-budget comparison)

Let SDC\_f and S\_f use the same declared unit, and let SDC\_f be read as the upper limit of differentials Domain f can stably sustain while its existing stable differentials are kept unchanged. This theorem uses the fixed-budget comparison: the Domain f, the unit of measurement, and other conditions are fixed; SDC\_f is the same before and after the addition, existing occupancy is neither released nor re-evaluated, and the new differential changes occupancy from S\_f to S\_f + u (u \> 0); the declared legitimate maintenance condition is S\_f + u ≤ SDC\_f. (a) If, under this comparison, a new difference fa₄ obtains as an additional stable differential within Domain f while all existing differentials are kept unchanged, then before the addition R\_f(t) ≥ u \> 0; hence when R\_f(t) ≤ 0 before the addition, it cannot so obtain under this comparison. (b) R\_f(t) ≥ u is not sufficient: for fa₄ to form a new subdivision, it must further satisfy Field Chapter 8’s separability condition d(fa₄, fa\_i) \> θ\_sep with each existing differential, and its maintenance must be checked separately.

Proof sketch: under the fixed-budget comparison, if fa₄ obtains as an additional stable differential while the existing differentials are kept unchanged, occupancy changes from S\_f to S\_f + u while SDC\_f is unchanged; by the declared legitimate maintenance condition S\_f + u ≤ SDC\_f, before the addition R\_f = SDC\_f − S\_f ≥ u \> 0; its contrapositive is the second half of (a). Insufficiency: if R\_f ≥ u but d(fa₄, fa\_i) ≤ θ\_sep, the two fail the pairwise separability condition of that criterion, and fa₄ does not become a new subdivision; conversely, if d \> θ\_sep but R\_f ≤ 0, the two are pairwise separable, yet under this comparison fa₄ cannot be sustained as an additional differential while the existing ones are kept. Hence R\_f and the separability condition do not entail each other. ∎

Note: (a) excludes only an addition under “all existing differentials kept unchanged.” When R\_f ≤ 0, the outcome of fa₄—merging into existing structure, displacing an existing differential, overflowing as a Boundary Saturation Outgrowth candidate, or degrading and dissipating—must be judged separately; the main case discussed in this chapter is merging. Processes in which capacity changes during the addition, or existing occupancy is reorganized, lie outside this comparison and are judged separately: for example, before the addition SDC\_f = S\_f = 2; during the addition capacity rises to 3, the new occupancy is 1, and all old differentials are kept—the addition obtains while R\_f = 0 beforehand, which does not conflict with this theorem, since the fixed-budget comparison is not met. The pairwise inseparability relation of Field Chapter 8 is not taken as a complete same-class relation without an additional classification rule. v1.1 Theorem 1’s “when R\_f(t) ≤ 0, new differentials are compressed and merged into existing Domains, and Domain Saturation occurs” and “corresponding to Field Chapter 8’s d(fa₄, fa\_i) \> θ\_sep obtaining” are withdrawn: the former writes one outcome as the only result and takes Margin Exhaustion for Domain Fullness; the latter writes two independent conditions as corresponding.

Theorem 4: Margin Decline Theorem (condition for Resolution-Margin Collapse)

Let SDC\_f and S\_f be differentiable on \[t₀, t₁\]. (a) If dS\_f/dt \> dSDC\_f/dt on \[t₀, t₁\], then R\_f strictly decreases on \[t₀, t₁\], i.e., Resolution-Margin Collapse in this chapter’s sense occurs in that window. (b) If further dS\_f/dt − dSDC\_f/dt ≥ c \> 0 on \[t₀, t₁\], then R\_f(t) ≤ R\_f(t₀) − c(t − t₀); when R\_f(t₀) \> 0 and t₁ ≥ t₀ + R\_f(t₀)/c, R\_f reaches Margin Exhaustion (R\_f ≤ 0) no later than t₀ + R\_f(t₀)/c.

Proof sketch: dR\_f/dt = dSDC\_f/dt − dS\_f/dt. (a) dR\_f/dt \< 0 on \[t₀, t₁\], so R\_f strictly decreases by the mean value theorem. (b) Let g(t) = R\_f(t) + ct; then on \[t₀, t₁\], g′(t) = dSDC\_f/dt − dS\_f/dt + c ≤ 0; by the mean value theorem g is nonincreasing, so R\_f(t) ≤ R\_f(t₀) − c(t − t₀); setting t = t₀ + R\_f(t₀)/c yields the result. ∎

Note: under the pointwise condition dS\_f/dt \> dSDC\_f/dt alone, R\_f strictly decreases but need not reach Margin Exhaustion: for example R\_f(t) = 1 + e^{−t} (t ≥ 0) strictly decreases yet stays above 1. A strict decrease does not entail tending to zero, and being bounded below does not guarantee reaching the bound. This theorem entails neither Resolution Collapse in Field Chapter 9 (shrinkage of the long-term separable set) nor Resolution Loss in Field Chapter 1; “the field tends toward low-resolution structures” is an interpretation.

Theorem 5: Margin Recovery Theorem (condition for Resolution-Margin Recovery)

Let SDC\_f and S\_f be differentiable on \[t₀, t₁\]. (a) If dSDC\_f/dt \> dS\_f/dt on \[t₀, t₁\], then R\_f strictly increases on \[t₀, t₁\], i.e., Resolution-Margin Recovery in this chapter’s sense occurs in that window. (b) If further dSDC\_f/dt − dS\_f/dt ≥ c \> 0 on \[t₀, t₁\] and R\_f(t₀) \< u, then when t₁ ≥ t₀ + (u − R\_f(t₀))/c, R\_f reaches u (the necessary condition of Theorem 1) no later than t₀ + (u − R\_f(t₀))/c.

Proof sketch: as for Theorem 4, with the sign reversed; for (b), apply the mean value theorem to h(t) = R\_f(t) − ct, with h′ ≥ 0, so h is nondecreasing. ∎

Note: R\_f rising to u or above satisfies only the necessary condition of Theorem 1; whether previously compressed differences can again become new subdivisions depends further on the separability and maintenance conditions. The pointwise condition likewise does not guarantee reaching u (counterexample as for Theorem 4, with the sign reversed). In the proof of v1.1, “old Familiar Route fracture (Field Chapter 10 Theorem 6) → LoopCoupling↓ → S\_f↓” is withdrawn: Field Chapter 10 v1.2 has changed the former Theorem 6 into Proposition 6 (Closure Fracture, proposed inducing factors), and LoopCoupling↓ making S\_f↓ is a directional hypothesis of Section 11 of this chapter.
