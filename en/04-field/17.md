# Inter-Field Coupling

1\. Purpose

The preceding sixteen chapters established the internal dynamics of a single field (Domain f):

\- how sedimentation forms;  
\- how Familiar Routes deepen;  
\- under what conditions a compression candidate obtains (Field Chapter 13 v1.2);  
\- how the Capacity Ratio falls (Field Chapter 15 v1.2);  
\- how Continuation-Dynamics arises.

But the preceding sixteen chapters carried an implicit assumption: all dynamics occur within a single field’s interior.

In reality, fields are never isolated.

Between the neural sedimentation field and the image field there is continuous continuation-flow transmission; between the intention field and the language field there is structural mapping; between the individual field and the social field there is long-term Co-Nucleus alignment and tension.

The starting viewpoint of this chapter (interpretation): the following phenomena all involve dynamic connections between fields:

\- individuals forming consensus;  
\- language carrying meaning;  
\- skills migrating across fields;  
\- culture sedimenting and being transmitted.

This chapter studies such connections and does not assert that the above phenomena necessarily cannot occur without coupling.

Therefore this chapter discusses: how coupling forms between fields, what the conditions for coupling are, why the direction of coupling is asymmetric, and what coupling failure and strong coupling each lead to.

1\. Core Proposition

Inter-Field Coupling is not simply a signal flowing from one field into another.

This chapter proposes: for coupling to obtain, the receiving field must be able to recognize, accommodate, and stably maintain the continuation-flow structure arriving from the sending field; if the receiving field’s resolution, tuning state, and Familiar Route structure are incompatible with the input signal, the continuation-flow finds it hard to stably sediment in the receiving field after entry, and coupling is hard to obtain.

Therefore (delimitation of this chapter):

Coupling is not transmission, but the stable continuation-flow transfer that follows structural compatibility.

And:

Inter-Field Coupling is defined separately for each direction: Cpl(f\_A → f\_B) and Cpl(f\_B → f\_A) are two quantities.

f\_A → f\_B obtaining does not mean f\_B → f\_A obtains.

This chapter proposes: the direction and intensity of coupling between two fields depend on each field’s θ\_sep, K\_f, φ\_f, and Familiar Route structure—not on symmetric physical connection. The compatibility in the illustrative model of Section 6 contains only φ, MatchTheta, and Familiar-Route overlap; the role of K\_f is proposed.

1\. Coupling Operator

Let f\_A and f\_B be two distinct Domains.

Define:

Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t)

as the Field Coupling Intensity from Domain f\_A to Domain f\_B at time t.

Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t) ≥ 0.

Cpl = 0 means f\_A has no effective coupling to f\_B; the higher Cpl, the more stably f\_A’s continuation-flow structure can sediment in f\_B and form sustained influence (reading). The unit and reading of Cpl must be declared.

Note: Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t) and Cpl(f\_B → f\_A, t) are generally unequal (Directional Asymmetry; see Section 5).

1\. Coupling Conditions

This chapter characterizes the conditions for coupling by the following four items; in the illustrative model of Section 6, the exact condition for coupling to obtain from zero is given in Theorem 1:

Condition 1: Continuation-Flow Output Condition

f\_A has outward continuation-flow output. Illustrative decomposition of OutFlow(f\_A, t) (a model choice of this chapter):

OutFlow(f\_A, t) = α·P\_Δ(f\_A, t) + β·Press(f\_A→f\_B, t) + γ·Rc(f\_A, t)

where:  
α·P\_Δ(f\_A, t): the passive output corresponding to f\_A’s Boundary Differential Pressure (Field Chapter 12 v1.2: P\_Δ is the weighted boundary integral of the unabsorbed input R); it is not Boundary Saturation Outgrowth itself, whose candidacy follows Criterion 2 of Field Chapter 12 and whose certification follows Field Chapter 1, Definition 21 and Equation 10; α ≥ 0.  
β·Press(f\_A→f\_B, t): f\_A’s active continuation-flow pressing into f\_B; Press(f,p,t) in Field Chapter 16 v1.2 is the binary judgment that continuation-flow f presses into Continuation-Position p, and this chapter’s Press(f\_A→f\_B, t) is its aggregate inter-field reading, whose counting must be declared; β ≥ 0.  
γ·Rc(f\_A, t): the sustained output after f\_A’s Re-Continuation stabilizes; Rc in Field Chapter 16 v1.2 is the Re-Continuation structure, and this chapter’s Rc(f\_A, t) is a reading of its output intensity, whose counting must be declared; γ ≥ 0.

α, β, γ are declared conversion coefficients converting the three terms into the same output unit. The sum OutFlow ≥ 0. If all three terms of f\_A are zero (no Boundary Differential Pressure output, no active pressing-in, no Re-Continuation output), then OutFlow = 0.

Note: this decomposition provides a precise definition of the input sources for the Transfer(f^n-1→f^n) operator in Field Chapter 19’s field layer-hierarchy structure.

Condition 2: Resolution Compatibility Condition

This chapter represents the receiving field’s remaining open continuation domain by f\_B’s Residual Capacity Ratio φ\_{f\_B}(t) \> 0 (Field Chapter 15 v1.2), and requires f\_A’s output continuation-flow structure to fall within f\_B’s differentiable range. The Resolution Margin R\_{f\_B} of Field Chapter 12 v1.2 does not enter the compatibility of Section 6; the link between R\_{f\_B} and φ\_{f\_B} follows hypothesis H\_φR of Field Chapter 15 v1.2, and neither implies the other.

This chapter proposes: if f\_B is in Margin Exhaustion (R\_{f\_B} ≤ 0, Field Chapter 12 v1.2; Margin Exhaustion is not Domain Fullness in Field Chapter 1, Definition 20), under ordinary input f\_B finds it hard to accommodate new structures, input is mostly absorbed by old Familiar Routes, and Cpl tends to fall; this reading is not in the illustrative model, where only a fall of φ\_{f\_B} lowers compatibility. Exception (proposed): when OutFlow(f\_A, t) \> T\_replace (a declared old-Familiar-Route fracture threshold), strong input may trigger fracture of f\_B’s old Familiar Routes (Field Chapter 14’s Interpenetration Fracture mechanism, per its current version), R\_{f\_B} locally recovers, and coupling can re-obtain; Boundary Saturation Outgrowth candidacy and certification follow Criterion 2 of Field Chapter 12 and Field Chapter 1, Definition 21 and Equation 10. Examples (interpretation): intense educational shock, cognitive paradigm revolution, and structural restructuring after psychological trauma.

Condition 3: Tuning Compatibility Condition

The structural characteristics of f\_A’s output signal are compatible with f\_B’s Separability Threshold θ\_sep(f\_B, t) (Field Chapter 8 v1.2: d \> θ\_sep means separable).

Separability must be judged for declared comparison objects by the difference d and θ\_sep of Field Chapter 8 v1.2 (d \> θ\_sep means separable), and cannot be replaced directly by a signal amplitude whose comparison objects are unstated. The reading rule of MatchTheta must be declared separately. If this model further stipulates MatchTheta = 0 when the separability condition is not met, then Compat = 0 in the corresponding period and no new coupling drive arises; existing Cpl still evolves by the dynamic equation and does not drop to zero at once. Only if the drive is zero over the whole history and the initial value is zero can it be judged by Theorem 1 that no coupling has been established.

This chapter proposes: if the differential structure of f\_A’s output signal has no overlap with f\_B’s tuning focus, the signal may quickly recede after entering f\_B, and new coupling drive is hard to arise.

Condition 4: Familiar Route Overlap Condition

f\_A’s output continuation-flow structure has partial matching with f\_B’s existing Familiar Routes. Familiar Routes follow Field Chapter 1, Definition 13; comparison of familiarity follows the path Sedimentation-Degree statistic δ̄ of Field Chapter 11 v1.2, and overall advantage in continuation competition is measured separately by CI, which is not a degree of familiarity. FamOverlap(f\_A, f\_B, t) ∈ \[0,1\] is a declared Familiar-Route overlap, whose counting must be declared. v1.0 wrote “existing Familiar Routes Familiarity(q\_i, t)”; this is re-expressed per Field Chapter 11 v1.2.

This chapter proposes: input with no Familiar Route overlap finds it hard to locate existing sedimentation anchors in f\_B; however, when φ\_{f\_B} is relatively high and MatchTheta is compatible, f\_B can spontaneously form new anchor points (new sedimentation nuclei) to receive unfamiliar input, and coupling obtains at lower intensity.

Therefore this condition defines Effective Familiar-Route Overlap:

FamOverlap\_eff(f\_A, f\_B, t) = max(FamOverlap(f\_A, f\_B, t), ε · φ\_{f\_B}(t) · MatchTheta(f\_A, f\_B, t))

where ε ∈ (0,1) is the New-Anchor Formation Coefficient (a declared illustrative coefficient), representing f\_B’s capacity to spontaneously form new anchors for unfamiliar input by means of its Residual Capacity Ratio and tuning compatibility. Small ε means new anchor formation is difficult; large ε means f\_B is highly open to unfamiliar input. The ε term is an illustrative model choice by which unfamiliar input obtains a positive compatibility reading; its positive value does not certify that actual new anchors, sedimentation nuclei, or Familiar Routes have formed, and whether such structures are generated and stably maintained must be checked separately by the corresponding structural conditions (Familiar Routes by Field Chapter 1, Definition 13; Domains by Definition 8).

Compat is accordingly updated: Compat(f\_A, f\_B, t) = φ\_{f\_B}(t) · MatchTheta(f\_A, f\_B, t) · FamOverlap\_eff(f\_A, f\_B, t)

Note (interpretation): this condition explains why learning new knowledge requires anchors in old knowledge (FamOverlap dominant term), and why completely unfamiliar domains can still slowly enter (ε term)—the latter corresponds to the minimal coupling path in Field Chapter 18 during the early stages of a field’s formation when no Familiar Routes yet exist.

Relation between the four conditions (within the illustrative model, see Theorem 1): starting from zero, coupling obtains only if OutFlow \> 0, φ\_{f\_B} \> 0, and MatchTheta \> 0 hold together on a time set of positive measure; FamOverlap\_eff ≥ ε·φ\_{f\_B}·MatchTheta \> 0 then holds automatically. Existing coupling does not become zero at once after input stops but decays at Decay\_C (Theorem 4; no decay when Decay\_C = 0). v1.0’s “Condition 1 is the necessary prerequisite; Conditions 2–4 jointly determine coupling intensity. The absence of any one condition drives Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t) toward zero” is replaced by this statement.

1\. Directional Asymmetry

Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t) and Cpl(f\_B → f\_A, t) are generally unequal (Proposition 2)

Reason: the compatibilities of the two directions use different receiving-field parameters (φ\_{f\_B} versus φ\_{f\_A}, and so on), and the outputs of the two directions also differ; the two fields’ θ\_sep, R\_f, K\_f, φ\_f, and Familiar Route structures are generally asymmetric.

Detailed analysis:

This chapter proposes (the compatibility of the illustrative model does not contain K\_f): if K\_{f\_A} is high (highly compressed, abstract structure) while K\_{f\_B} is low (concrete structure), coupling in the f\_A → f\_B direction may be weak (abstract structures find it hard to locate Familiar Route anchors in concrete fields), while coupling in the f\_B → f\_A direction may be stronger (concrete details entering a highly compressed field may be rapidly subsumed into existing Co-Nuclei).

This chapter proposes: when K\_{f\_A} ≫ K\_{f\_B}, the f\_A → f\_B direction may exhibit significant Inter-Level Transfer Loss (TransferLoss(f\_A→f\_B) ↑)—the output of a highly compressed field finds it hard to unfold into independently differentiable differentials in the receiving field. This regularity is one source of the inter-level loss operator Λ(f^n-1, f^n) in Field Chapter 19’s layer-hierarchy structure.

Read from the compatibility: if φ\_{f\_B} is low (capacity nearly exhausted), f\_A → f\_B compatibility is low; while if φ\_{f\_A} is high, f\_B → f\_A compatibility may still be relatively high.

This chapter proposes: if f\_A’s Familiar Route structure has no overlap with f\_B’s tuning focus, f\_A → f\_B coupling is extremely weak; but if f\_B has a dedicated threshold-gate focus (Continuation-Position per Definition 4 of Field Chapter 16 v1.2) for receiving f\_A-type inputs, the directional difference becomes even more pronounced.

Therefore (proposed): cases of strong coupling in one direction and weak coupling in the reverse are common; this chapter does not prove this to be the normal state of Inter-Field Coupling.

Examples (interpretation): the coupling of language field → intention field (language influencing intention) and intention field → language field (intention driving language) are unequal in both direction and intensity. The influence of the emotional field → cognitive field is generally stronger than that of the cognitive field → emotional field, possibly because of the asymmetry in θ\_sep and K\_f between the two fields.

1\. Coupling Dynamic Equation

Illustrative dynamic equation (a model choice of this chapter):

dCpl(f\_A → f\_B, t)/dt = a·OutFlow(f\_A, t)·Compat(f\_A, f\_B, t) − Decay\_C·Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t)

where a \> 0 is a declared constant; OutFlow and Compat are nonnegative, measurable, and locally integrable; a solution is an absolutely continuous function satisfying the equation almost everywhere. The “∝” of v1.0 is replaced by the constant a.

where:

OutFlow(f\_A, t) (Outflow Intensity): the continuation-flow output intensity from f\_A toward f\_B, corresponding to Condition 1. OutFlow ≥ 0.

Compat(f\_A, f\_B, t) (Structural Compatibility): the overall structural compatibility between f\_A’s output structure and f\_B’s receiving structure, the aggregate of Conditions 2–4.

Compat(f\_A, f\_B, t) = φ\_{f\_B}(t) · MatchTheta(f\_A, f\_B, t) · FamOverlap\_eff(f\_A, f\_B, t)

where:  
φ\_{f\_B}(t): f\_B’s Residual Capacity Ratio (directly referencing Field Chapter 15 v1.2, φ\_f = A\_f/Cap\_f), ∈ \[0,1\]. Note: it is not replaced by R\_{f\_B}/Cap\_{f\_B}, since the two differ in level and unit; the link between φ\_{f\_B} and R\_{f\_B} follows hypothesis H\_φR of Field Chapter 15 v1.2, and φ\_{f\_B} is not a synthesis of their relation. v1.0’s “φ\_{f\_B} is the correct quantity that already synthesizes their relation at the Domain level” is withdrawn.  
MatchTheta(f\_A, f\_B, t): the match degree between f\_A’s output and f\_B’s θ\_sep (Field Chapter 8), ∈ \[0,1\], whose counting must be declared.  
FamOverlap\_eff(f\_A, f\_B, t): Effective Familiar-Route Overlap (Condition 4), ∈ \[0,1\]; when φ\_{f\_B} \> 0 and MatchTheta \> 0 the new-anchor formation term ε·φ\_{f\_B}·MatchTheta provides a positive floor; when φ\_{f\_B} = 0 or MatchTheta = 0 it may be 0. The positive value of the floor term is a model score, not a proof of structure generation; Theorem 1 proves that Cpl becomes positive from zero in this model and does not double as a new-anchor generation theorem. v1.0 wrote ∈ (0,1\] and “never zero”, which conflicts with Case 4 of Section 8; this version corrects it.

Decay\_C ≥ 0 is the Coupling Decay Rate (a declared constant). This chapter proposes that it corresponds to Field Quality (Field Chapter 1, Definition 28): the Numericology says: “Field-Quality determines the speed of forgetting. Without it, the field would easily saturate.” When Decay\_C \> 0, coupling intensity decays after input stops (Theorem 4); when Decay\_C = 0 it does not decay.

Therefore (reading): coupling is not a static connection; when Decay\_C \> 0 it is a dynamic relation that requires sustained continuation-flow to maintain; this model admits Decay\_C = 0, in which case existing coupling persists by itself without input.

1\. Coupling Failure

Conditions under which Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t) → 0. The following distinguishes falling compatibility, vanishing drive, and coupling tending to zero: compatibility tending to zero does not by itself guarantee that OutFlow·Compat tends to zero (OutFlow is only required to be locally integrable and may grow over time); whether existing coupling decays further requires checking the drive product and Decay\_C. For example, with φ\_{f\_B} = 1, FamOverlap = 1, MatchTheta = 1/(1 + t), OutFlow = 1 + t, Compat tends to zero while OutFlow·Compat stays at 1; with a = Decay\_C = 1 and zero initial value, Cpl = 1 − e^{−t} tends to 1.

Case 1 (Output Interruption): OutFlow(f\_A, t) = 0. f\_A stops outputting to f\_B; when Decay\_C \> 0, coupling decays exponentially to zero (Theorem 4). Examples (interpretation): long-unused cross-field skills deteriorating; language fading without use; social connections severing.

Case 2 (Margin-Exhaustion Block, proposed): R\_{f\_B} ≤ 0. This chapter proposes that f\_B finds it hard to accommodate new input and that f\_A’s output entering f\_B is mostly absorbed by old Familiar Routes, forming no new coupling structures; this case is not in the illustrative model, where it is represented by φ\_{f\_B} → 0 (see Case 4 and Theorem 1). Examples (interpretation): new information from others failing to truly enter a rigid cognitive state; an overly compressed cognitive field being impermeable to new input.

Case 3 (Tuning Incompatibility): MatchTheta(f\_A, f\_B, t) → 0. f\_A and f\_B’s tuning foci have no overlap at all, and the compatibility tends to zero (read from the compatibility); input fails, with respect to the declared comparison objects, the separability condition d \> θ\_sep, or quickly recedes after entering f\_B (proposed). If in addition OutFlow is bounded, compatibility tending to zero implies that the drive product tends to zero; further, when Decay\_C \> 0, Cpl → 0 (Theorem 4(c)); no such conclusion is drawn when Decay\_C = 0. Examples (interpretation): language being unable to express feelings (intention field and language field tuning incompatible); children being unable to understand highly abstract concepts (tuning not yet developed).

Case 4 (No Familiar Route Anchor and New Anchor Cannot Form): FamOverlap(f\_A, f\_B, t) → 0 and ε·φ\_{f\_B}·MatchTheta → 0 (for example φ\_{f\_B} → 0 or MatchTheta → 0; the product tending to zero does not require either factor itself to tend to zero, and the two may vanish alternately), making FamOverlap\_eff → 0. If in addition OutFlow is bounded, the drive product tends to zero; further, when Decay\_C \> 0, Cpl → 0 (Theorem 4(c)); no such conclusion is drawn when Decay\_C = 0. Examples (interpretation): a completely unfamiliar knowledge domain being unable to enter when f\_B’s capacity is exhausted or tuning is completely incompatible; the fundamental obstacle in cross-cultural exchange where neither Familiar Route overlap nor open capacity exists.

1\. Strong Coupling

The state Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t) \> T\_cpl (a declared threshold) is called strong coupling (this chapter’s operational delimitation).

Structural consequences of strong coupling (proposed):

f\_A’s continuation-flow structure may form deep sedimentation in f\_B, and f\_B’s Familiar Routes may align toward f\_A’s structure (FamOverlap rises; hypothesis H\_T, see Proposition 3).

This chapter proposes: the Co-Nuclei of f\_A and f\_B may begin to align, and the stable maintenance structures shared by the two fields increase; this is called Cross-Field Co-Nucleus Formation. Whether it constitutes a Co-Nucleus is judged by Field Chapter 1, Definition 15 and Equation 7 (long-term co-Trace-Effects and subsequent stable co-recurrence); whether each field’s K\_f rises follows the growth conditions of Field Chapter 13 v1.2, and compression must be certified by Field Chapter 1, Definition 35.

After Cross-Field Co-Nucleus Formation (proposed): Cpl(f\_A → f\_B) may rise further; whether coupling tends toward stability must be proved separately.

Simultaneously (proposed): f\_B’s independent structural space may contract, φ\_{f\_B} falls, and f\_B finds it increasingly difficult to maintain independent differentials distinct from f\_A.

Therefore (interpretation): strong coupling is double-edged—it enables stable transmission of complex structures between the two fields, but also reduces f\_B’s independence, making new differential generation increasingly difficult.

Rejection Dynamics of strong coupling (proposed):

This chapter proposes: not all strong coupling tends toward stability. When FamOverlap(f\_A, f\_B) continuously falls (the structural difference between the two fields grows) while Cpl continues to rise, structural tension that f\_B cannot absorb may accumulate, ultimately possibly triggering Coupling Collapse—the forced continuation-flow transmission between the two fields activates f\_B’s rejection, raising f\_B’s separability threshold to block input (the direction of change of θ\_sep follows the directional hypotheses of Field Chapter 8 v1.2; a decline in separability is not a rise in the threshold).

Condition (proposed): FamOverlap ↓ ∧ Cpl ↑ ∧ P\_Δ(f\_B) \> B\_{f\_B} ⇒ Coupling Collapse risk rises. P\_Δ(f\_B) \> B\_{f\_B} alone does not constitute a Boundary Saturation Outgrowth candidate (Criterion 2 of Field Chapter 12 v1.2 further requires R\_{f\_B} ≤ 0).

Examples (interpretation): cognitive rejection in cultural conflict; structural resistance triggered by forced assimilation; systemic fracture after sustained accumulation of cognitive dissonance.

Examples (interpretation): skill automatization (after the motor field and cognitive field form strong coupling, motor execution no longer requires explicit cognitive field participation); social consensus formation (after individual fields and the social field form long-term strong coupling, individual fields’ Familiar Routes align with the social field, making dissent hard to sustain); conditioned reflexes (strong coupling between the stimulus field and response field makes the continuation-flow transmission between them automatic).

1\. Coupling Stability and Instability

Instantaneous balance condition:

dCpl/dt = 0, i.e., a·OutFlow·Compat = Decay\_C·Cpl. That this equality holds at a single instant does not prove coupling stability. When the drive product is a constant Sc and Decay\_C \> 0, Theorem 4(b) proves that Cpl converges exponentially to a·Sc/Decay\_C; when inputs vary over time, equilibrium must be proved separately. v1.0’s “Coupling stability condition: dCpl/dt ≈ 0” is replaced by this statement.

Coupling instability conditions:

Case 1 (Excessive Strong Coupling, proposed): Cpl continuously strengthens; f\_B’s φ\_{f\_B} → 0; f\_B loses independent continuation capacity and begins completely depending on f\_A’s input to drive it. If f\_A’s output is interrupted, f\_B may rapidly destabilize due to insufficient independent continuation-flow (coupling-dependency collapse, Proposition 6).

Case 2 (Coupling Oscillation, proposed): if f\_A and f\_B’s θ\_sep vary periodically (e.g., alternating tuning states), MatchTheta and the compatibility vary periodically with them, and coupling intensity may periodically rise and fall. Examples (interpretation): the sustained tension state of two parties’ cognitive foci alternately converging and diverging.

1\. Summary Expression

General Inter-Field Coupling Dynamics Expression (with status listed):

f\_A has output ∧ φ\_{f\_B} \> 0 ∧ tuning compatible (holding together on a time set of positive measure) ⇒ Cpl(f\_A → f\_B) becomes positive from zero (Theorem 1; the positive value of the effective overlap is given by the model’s floor term and does not certify actual new-anchor formation)

Cpl continuously grows ⇒ Cross-Field Co-Nucleus Formation ⇒ f\_B’s Familiar Routes align toward f\_A (proposed positive feedback; Proposition 3 under H\_T)

Cpl too high ⇒ φ\_{f\_B} ↓ ⇒ f\_B’s independence falls (proposed; cost of strong coupling)

Cpl → 0 ⇒ cross-field structure transmission interrupted ⇒ sedimentation decays by field quality (proposed; decay of Cpl per Theorem 4 when Decay\_C \> 0)

Interpretation of this chapter: Inter-Field Coupling is the dynamic foundation for multi-field systems to exist. Single-field resolution limits, compression, and capacity limits can be transmitted, distributed, and transformed across fields through coupling. Layer-hierarchy structures, consensus, skill migration, and civilizational accumulation in multi-field systems all involve coupling; this chapter does not prove coupling to be their only or sufficient condition.

1\. Theorems

Theorem 1: Coupling Formation Theorem (within the illustrative model)

Let the illustrative model of Section 6 hold: a \> 0, Decay\_C ≥ 0, OutFlow and Compat nonnegative, measurable, and locally integrable, and Cpl an absolutely continuous solution. Then for t ≥ t₀: Cpl(t) = e^{−Decay\_C·(t−t₀)}·Cpl(t₀) + a·∫\_{t₀}^{t} e^{−Decay\_C·(t−s)}·OutFlow(s)·Compat(s) ds. Hence: (a) Cpl(t) ≥ 0 when Cpl(t₀) ≥ 0; (b) if Cpl(t₀) = 0, then Cpl(t) \> 0 if and only if OutFlow·Compat \> 0 on some subset of \[t₀, t\] of positive measure; (c) Compat \> 0 if and only if φ\_{f\_B} \> 0 and MatchTheta \> 0 (then FamOverlap\_eff ≥ ε·φ\_{f\_B}·MatchTheta \> 0). Together: starting from zero, Cpl(t) \> 0 if and only if OutFlow \> 0, φ\_{f\_B} \> 0, and MatchTheta \> 0 hold together on some subset of \[t₀, t\] of positive measure.

Proof sketch: by variation of constants, (e^{Decay\_C·t}·Cpl)′ = a·e^{Decay\_C·t}·OutFlow·Compat almost everywhere, and integration gives the representation. (a) Both terms are nonnegative. (b) e^{−Decay\_C(t−s)} \> 0 and the integrand is nonnegative, so the integral is positive if and only if the integrand is positive on some set of positive measure. (c) Compat = φ\_{f\_B}·MatchTheta·FamOverlap\_eff; when φ\_{f\_B} and MatchTheta are both positive, FamOverlap\_eff ≥ ε·φ\_{f\_B}·MatchTheta \> 0 and all three factors are positive; when either is 0, Compat = 0. ∎

Note: v1.0 Theorem 1, “Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t) \> 0 if and only if … The absence of any one condition means coupling does not obtain,” judges the sign of Cpl(t) by conditions at the same instant and does not hold: existing coupling remains positive after input stops—for example, if OutFlow is 0 on \[t₁, t\], then Cpl(t) = e^{−Decay\_C·(t−t₁)}·Cpl(t₁) \> 0; and with Decay\_C = 0 a missing condition does not drive Cpl to zero. This version replaces it with (b). R\_{f\_B} \> 0 in Condition 2 is not in the illustrative model, which represents receiving capacity by φ\_{f\_B}; FamOverlap\_eff \> 0 holds automatically when φ\_{f\_B} and MatchTheta are both positive and is not an independent condition.

Proposition 2: Directional Asymmetry (read from the model)

Cpl(f\_A → f\_B, t) and Cpl(f\_B → f\_A, t) are driven by their own outputs and compatibilities and are generally unequal; this chapter does not derive their equality from the model.

Note: Compat(f\_A, f\_B) uses φ\_{f\_B}, MatchTheta(f\_A→f\_B), FamOverlap\_eff(f\_A→f\_B), while Compat(f\_B, f\_A) uses φ\_{f\_A}, MatchTheta(f\_B→f\_A), FamOverlap\_eff(f\_B→f\_A), and the outputs are OutFlow(f\_A) and OutFlow(f\_B) respectively. For example: with both directions starting at 0, Decay\_C \> 0, the same constant OutFlow and MatchTheta in both directions, FamOverlap\_eff taking the same existing-overlap term, and φ\_{f\_B} = 0.2, φ\_{f\_A} = 0.8, the equilibrium values of the two directions are in the ratio 1 : 4 (Theorem 4). The statement in the proof of v1.0 Theorem 2, “Symmetric coupling holds only when the two fields are structurally completely symmetric—a set of measure zero”, is withdrawn: equality of the two directions does not require complete structural symmetry (different parameter combinations can make the products of output and compatibility equal), and this chapter has not declared any parameter space on which a measure could be discussed. “unidirectional coupling obtaining while bidirectional coupling does not is the normal state of Inter-Field Coupling” becomes a proposal (Section 5).

Proposition 3: Strong-Coupling Positive Feedback (under H\_T)

Let hypothesis H\_T hold: when Cpl \> T\_cpl, FamOverlap(f\_A, f\_B) does not decrease. Then, under comparison with OutFlow, φ\_{f\_B}, and MatchTheta unchanged, Compat does not decrease, and the driving term a·OutFlow·Compat does not decrease.

Proof sketch: FamOverlap\_eff = max(FamOverlap, ε·φ\_{f\_B}·MatchTheta) is nondecreasing in FamOverlap, and Compat is its product with nonnegative factors; the result follows from H\_T. ∎

Note: a nondecreasing driving term does not entail dCpl/dt \> 0 (the decay term Decay\_C·Cpl grows with Cpl); whether strong coupling is “self-reinforcing” must be proved separately. The proof chain of v1.0 is withdrawn: (i) “f\_A’s sedimentation depth in f\_B exceeds θ\_sep(f\_B)” conflates Sedimentation-Degree with the separability threshold: θ\_sep compares the difference d (Field Chapter 8), not sedimentation depth; (ii) “φ\_{f\_B} ↓ (Field Chapter 15’s Capacity Ratio decline mechanism)”: Theorem 1 of Field Chapter 15 v1.2 is a conditional theorem within the illustrative model and requires its driving conditions; (iii) “ultimately dCpl/dt → 0, coupling stabilizes at a new equilibrium point” is unproven.

Theorem 4: Coupling Decay and Equilibrium Theorem (within the illustrative model)

Under the conditions of Theorem 1: (a) if OutFlow ≡ 0 on \[t₀, ∞) and Decay\_C \> 0, then Cpl(t) = Cpl(t₀)·e^{−Decay\_C·(t−t₀)} → 0; when Decay\_C = 0, Cpl stays at Cpl(t₀). (c) If the drive product g = OutFlow·Compat satisfies g(t) → 0 (t → ∞) and Decay\_C \> 0, then Cpl(t) → 0.

Proof sketch: (a) and (b) by direct computation from the representation of Theorem 1. (c) Given η \> 0, choose T ≥ t₀ such that g(t) \< η for t ≥ T; for t ≥ T split the history integral into \[t₀, T\] and \[T, t\]: the former together with the initial term is at most e^{−Decay\_C·(t−T)}·(Cpl(t₀) + a·∫\_{t₀}^{T} g ds), which tends to 0 as t → ∞; the latter is at most a·η/Decay\_C. Hence limsup Cpl ≤ a·η/Decay\_C, and since η is arbitrary, Cpl → 0. ∎
