# Field Layer-Hierarchy Structure

1\. Purpose

The preceding eighteen chapters established single-field internal dynamics, Inter-Field Coupling, and field genesis and dissolution. Yet one fundamental question remains unanswered:

Why does abstraction exist?

Why is there such a large structural difference between concrete perception (neural signals) and abstract concepts (linguistic terms)? Why do higher-level fields become increasingly simple and stable in structure, yet increasingly difficult for lower-level fields to directly understand?

The answer to this question lies not within a single field, nor in the parallel coupling between two fields, but in: fields standing in layer-hierarchical relation to each other.

Therefore this chapter discusses: how fields stack to form layer-hierarchy structures; how inter-level transfer occurs; why inter-level loss is inevitable; and the dynamic sources of abstraction, lower-level collapse causing upper-level destabilization, and the distinction between scale and layer depth.

1\. Core Proposition

Fields in real systems are not flat.

Different fields exhibit systematic differences in sedimentation depth, Compression Intensity, and resolution scale—some fields handle details, some handle relations, some handle relations of relations.

This systematic difference is not accidental but is determined by the dynamic laws of layer-hierarchy structure:

The Overflow Sedimentation output of the lower-level field becomes the continuation-flow input of the upper-level field.

Inter-level transfer is accompanied by loss; loss makes the structure obtained by the upper-level field increasingly compressed and abstract.

Therefore: abstraction is not the result of active induction but the structural consequence of inter-level loss.

1\. Field Layer-Hierarchy Definition

Let the Field Layer-Hierarchy Sequence be:

ℒ = {f⁰, f¹, f², …, fⁿ}

where:

f⁰ is the lowest-level field (e.g., the perceptual layer, neural activation layer);  
fⁿ is the nth-level field;  
the larger n, the higher the level, the more abstract and compressed the structure.

Definition of the layer-hierarchical relation: fⁿ⁻¹ is the direct lower level of fⁿ if and only if:

fⁿ⁻¹’s continuation-flow output (OutFlow; referencing Field Chapter 17’s decomposition) is the primary continuation-flow input source of fⁿ; and in a stable layer hierarchy, K\_{fⁿ} ≥ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹} is typically satisfied; for Strong-Abstraction Levels, K\_{fⁿ} \> K\_{fⁿ⁻¹} is satisfied (upper-level Compression Intensity higher than lower-level; Field Chapter 13).

Note: the fundamental distinction between Layer Depth (Depth) and Scale (Scale). Layer Depth is a vertical stacking relation—lower-level field output drives upper-level field; Scale is a horizontal expansion relation—the size of Cap\_f (Field Capacity) of fields at the same level (Field Chapter 15). The two are independent: large-scale fields are not necessarily high-level fields; high-level fields are not necessarily large-scale fields. Scale dynamics will be formally developed in Field Chapter 21.

1\. Inter-Level Transfer Operator

Let the inter-level transfer from fⁿ⁻¹ to fⁿ be:

Transfer(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ, t)

Its input source directly references Field Chapter 17’s OutFlow decomposition:

Transfer(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ, t) = OutFlow(fⁿ⁻¹, t) · Compat(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ, t)

That is: inter-level transfer is the application of Field Chapter 17’s coupling equation in the vertical direction—fⁿ⁻¹’s output (Overflow Sedimentation / active pressing-in / Re-Continuation output) multiplied by the structural compatibility with fⁿ gives the effective transfer quantity actually entering fⁿ.

Compat(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ, t) = φ\_{fⁿ}(t) · MatchTheta(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ, t) · FamOverlap\_eff(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ, t) (referencing Field Chapter 17)

1\. Inter-Level Loss

Inter-level transfer is necessarily accompanied by structural loss.

Sources of loss:

Reason 1 (Compression Disparity): in a stable layer hierarchy, K\_{fⁿ} ≥ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹}; for Strong-Abstraction Levels, K\_{fⁿ} \> K\_{fⁿ⁻¹}. fⁿ⁻¹’s output contains many independent differentials (Ind\_{fⁿ⁻¹} high), but fⁿ’s high Compression Intensity prevents these independent differentials from unfolding into differentiable independent structures—they are subsumed into fⁿ’s existing Shared Nuclei instead. Therefore: K\_{fⁿ} ≫ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹} → TransferLoss ↑ (referencing Field Chapter 17 Section 5’s Inter-Level Transfer Loss pre-embedding).

Reason 2 (Tuning Disparity): MatchTheta(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ) \< 1. The signal structure of fⁿ⁻¹’s output is not fully compatible with fⁿ’s Separability Threshold θ\_sep; part of the signal falls below θ\_sep after entering fⁿ and is erased by the forgetting term (Field Chapter 8).

Reason 3 (Resolution Disparity): fⁿ’s R\_{fⁿ} is typically lower than fⁿ⁻¹’s (higher-level fields have lower Resolution Margin), so fine-grained differentials transmitted from fⁿ⁻¹ cannot be stably maintained in fⁿ.

Inter-Level Loss Coefficient:

Λ(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ) ∈ \[0,1\]

denotes the loss ratio of inter-level transfer (1 − Λ is the effective transfer ratio). The higher Λ, the greater the loss, the more compressed and abstract the structure obtained by the upper level.

Level-Effective Sedimentation:

δ\_eff(fⁿ, t) = Transfer(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ, t) · (1 − Λ(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ))

denotes the effective sedimentation intensity actually accumulated in level fⁿ after inter-level loss. Note: δ\_eff is the effective continuation-flow input intensity at the Domain level, corresponding to the driving input term of δ(x,t) within Domain fⁿ—not directly equivalent to Field Chapter 7’s δ(x,t) (a spatial-point sedimentation scalar field); the two are at different levels, and δ\_eff is the aggregated driving quantity at the fⁿ Domain level.

Loss does not equal ineffective transfer. Loss means: details are compressed, relations are preserved, and abstraction is thereby formed.

1\. The Structural Source of Abstraction

Abstraction is not the result of active induction.

Abstraction is the structural consequence of inter-level loss: at each level of transfer, fine-grained differentials are pressed into Shared Nuclei; relational structures are preserved; the count of independent differentials Ind\_f decreases; Shared Nucleus coverage increases.

Therefore:

higher n → more accumulated Λ → higher K\_{fⁿ} → lower Ind\_{fⁿ} → more abstract structure

Formalized:

K\_{fⁿ} ≥ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹} (layer-hierarchical compression escalation, holds in stable hierarchies); Strong-Abstraction Levels satisfy K\_{fⁿ} ≥ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹} + ΔK (layer-hierarchical compression increment, ΔK \> 0). Note: K’s layer-hierarchical escalation is a statistical regularity, allowing certain higher-level fields to temporarily have lower K during locally open phases (e.g., states of creativity); but the escalation trend holds in long-term stable hierarchies.

Ind\_{fⁿ} ≤ Ind\_{fⁿ⁻¹} · (1 − Λ(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ)) (layer-hierarchical independent-differential decline)

This means: higher-level fields do not “see more clearly”—they “see less but more stably.” Higher-level fields have broad Shared Nucleus coverage and high stability, but low capacity for fine-grained differentiation.

Concrete examples:

f⁰ Neural Activation Layer: handles raw perceptual differentials; Ind extremely high, K extremely low.  
f¹ Image Layer: integrates perceptual patterns; some differentials pressed into image nuclei.  
f² Concept Layer: further compression; stably named Shared Nuclei form.  
f³ Language Layer: highly compressed; vocabulary = Shared Nuclei; fine-grained differentials almost entirely lost.  
f⁴ Civilization Layer: stable sedimentation of language usage patterns; most abstract, most stable, hardest to change.

1\. Lower-Level Collapse and Upper-Level Destabilization

If lower-level field fⁿ⁻¹ undergoes Death\_C or Death\_F (Field Chapter 18), fⁿ’s primary continuation-flow source is interrupted.

Dynamic chain:

Death(fⁿ⁻¹) ⇒ OutFlow(fⁿ⁻¹, t) ↓ ⇒ Transfer(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ) ↓ ⇒ δ\_eff(fⁿ) ↓

⇒ Insufficient Original-Continuation maintenance in fⁿ (continuation-flow input reduced; Familiar Route maintenance pressure falls)

⇒ old Familiar Routes turn inward, compression strengthens: K\_{fⁿ} ↑ (Field Chapter 13, Compression Inevitability Theorem)

⇒ R\_{fⁿ} ↓ (Field Chapter 12, Resolution Margin falls)

⇒ φ\_{fⁿ} ↓ (Field Chapter 15, Residual Capacity Ratio falls long-term; this is the final consequence)

If δ\_eff(fⁿ) is persistently zero and no other continuation-flow source compensates, fⁿ ultimately enters Death\_F (Forgetting Death).

Therefore: lower-level field dissolution cascade-propagates to upper-level fields—but propagation speed depends on the magnitude of Λ(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ) and K\_{fⁿ}: the greater the inter-level loss, the less sedimentation the upper-level field accumulates, the more dependent it is on the lower level, and the faster it destabilizes after lower-level collapse.

The reverse also holds: the upper-level field’s strong pressing-in (OutFlow downward) can influence the tuning state of the lower-level field, but this path is typically weaker than the lower-to-upper direction (Directional Asymmetry; referencing Field Chapter 17 Theorem 2).

1\. Layer Hierarchy and Coupling

Layer-hierarchy structure is a special case of Inter-Field Coupling (Field Chapter 17) in the vertical direction.

What distinguishes the layer-hierarchical relation fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ:

Direction fixed (lower-to-upper dominant); loss systematically escalates (Λ increases with level); K systematically escalates (upper-level Compression Intensity higher than lower-level); Genesis dependency relation (fⁿ’s genesis typically depends on fⁿ⁻¹ already obtaining; Field Chapter 18).

Whereas parallel coupling (Field Chapter 17): coupling direction and intensity are determined by each field’s φ\_f, θ\_sep, K\_f; no systematic directional preference.

1\. Dynamic Equations

Inter-level transfer equation:

Transfer(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ, t) = OutFlow(fⁿ⁻¹, t) · Compat(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ, t)

Level-Effective Sedimentation equation:

δ\_eff(fⁿ, t) = Transfer(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ, t) · (1 − Λ(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ))

Layer-hierarchical compression escalation relation (stable hierarchy):

K\_{fⁿ} ≥ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹}; Strong-Abstraction Levels: K\_{fⁿ} ≥ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹} + ΔK

Layer-hierarchical independent-differential decline relation:

Ind\_{fⁿ} ≤ Ind\_{fⁿ⁻¹} · (1 − Λ(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ))

Lower-level collapse propagation chain:

Death(fⁿ⁻¹) ⇒ Transfer ↓ ⇒ δ\_eff(fⁿ) ↓ ⇒ Insufficient Original-Continuation maintenance ⇒ K\_{fⁿ} ↑ ⇒ R\_{fⁿ} ↓ ⇒ φ\_{fⁿ} ↓

1\. Summary Expression

General Field Layer-Hierarchy Dynamics Expression:

Lower-level field Overflow Sedimentation / output ⇒ Transfer(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ) \> 0 (inter-level transfer obtains)

Transfer · (1−Λ) ⇒ δ\_eff(fⁿ) (effective sedimentation accumulates in upper level)

Accumulated Λ ⇒ K\_{fⁿ} ↑, Ind\_{fⁿ} ↓ (abstraction: details lost, relations preserved)

n ↑ ⇒ structure increasingly stable, abstract, resistant to change (structural characteristics of higher-level fields)

Death(fⁿ⁻¹) ⇒ δ\_eff(fⁿ) ↓ ⇒ Insufficient Original-Continuation maintenance ⇒ K\_{fⁿ} ↑ ⇒ R\_{fⁿ} ↓ ⇒ φ\_{fⁿ} ↓ (lower-level collapse cascade)

Layer depth ≠ scale: Depth(f) is vertical stacking; Scale(f) is horizontal expansion; the two are independent

Layer-hierarchy structure is the dynamic foundation for the progression from raw perception to civilizational accumulation in the real world. Abstraction is not actively constructed by humans but is the structural result naturally formed in the sedimentation field between inter-level transfer and loss.

1\. Theorems

Theorem 1: Strong-Abstraction Inter-Level Transfer Inevitable Loss Theorem

Under the condition that the layer-hierarchical relation fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ obtains, if K\_{fⁿ} \> K\_{fⁿ⁻¹}, then the inter-level loss Λ(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ) \> 0 necessarily holds; inter-level transfer cannot be lossless.

Proof sketch: K\_{fⁿ} \> K\_{fⁿ⁻¹} → fⁿ’s Compression Intensity is higher than fⁿ⁻¹’s → the independent differentials output by fⁿ⁻¹, upon entering fⁿ, are partially absorbed by fⁿ’s Shared Nuclei (Field Chapter 13’s Shared Nucleus Formation Theorem) → the effective maintenance count of independent differentials decreases → Ind\_{fⁿ} \< Ind\_{fⁿ⁻¹} → corresponding to Λ(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ) \> 0; simultaneously MatchTheta \< 1 and R\_{fⁿ} \< R\_{fⁿ⁻¹} further contribute to loss; hence Λ \> 0 is necessary. ∎

Theorem 2: Abstraction Escalation Theorem

In the Layer-Hierarchy Sequence ℒ, as level n increases, K\_{fⁿ} escalates and Ind\_{fⁿ} declines; higher-level fields are necessarily more abstract than lower-level fields.

Proof sketch: by Theorem 1, each level of transfer has Λ(fⁿ⁻¹, fⁿ) \> 0 → Ind\_{fⁿ} ≤ Ind\_{fⁿ⁻¹} · (1−Λ) \< Ind\_{fⁿ⁻¹} (strictly declining); K’s layer-hierarchical relation: in stable hierarchies K\_{fⁿ} ≥ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹}; in Strong-Abstraction Levels K\_{fⁿ} ≥ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹} + ΔK; hence the degree of abstraction shows an escalating trend in long-term stable hierarchies, rather than strictly escalating at every instant; Ind decline corresponds to falling fine-grained differentiation capacity; K escalation trend corresponds to increasing Shared Nucleus coverage → degree of abstraction monotonically escalates with level in the statistical sense. ∎

Theorem 3: Lower-Level Collapse Cascade Theorem

If Death(fⁿ⁻¹) = 1, then δ\_eff(fⁿ, t) → 0 and fⁿ tends toward destabilization; and if fⁿ⁻¹ is fⁿ’s sole continuation-flow source, then Death\_F(fⁿ) necessarily occurs.

Proof sketch: Death(fⁿ⁻¹) = 1 → OutFlow(fⁿ⁻¹) ↓ (both Compression Death and Forgetting Death in Field Chapter 18 drive output toward zero) → Transfer(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ) ↓ → δ\_eff(fⁿ) ↓; if fⁿ⁻¹ is the sole source, then Cpl(f\_ext → fⁿ) ≈ 0 (no other input) → Forgetting Death condition of Field Chapter 18 is satisfied → Death\_F(fⁿ) = 1 (Field Chapter 18 Theorem 3); if there are inputs from other levels (e.g., same-level parallel coupling), the degree of fⁿ’s destabilization depends on the compensating capacity of those other sources. ∎

Theorem 4: Layer Depth and Stability Theorem

Higher-level fields (large n) are structurally more stable than lower-level fields (small n) and harder to perturb; but once a higher-level field destabilizes, the lower-level reconstruction cost required for recovery is higher.

Proof sketch: K\_{fⁿ} high → Ind\_{fⁿ} low → most new input to the higher-level field is absorbed by Shared Nuclei (Field Chapter 13), making it hard to form independent new differentials → perturbation input is neutralized by old nuclei, hence high stability; but the higher-level field’s structure depends on a complete lower-level transfer chain (Theorem 3) → if lower levels must be rebuilt (Field Chapter 18’s Genesis), transfer must be re-accumulated level by level from f⁰, and reconstruction cost increases linearly with level. ∎

Theorem 5: Layer-Hierarchical Independence and Coupling Asymmetry Theorem

Layer-hierarchical transfer direction is dominated by lower-to-upper; the reverse influence in the upper-to-lower direction is significantly weaker; the higher the level, the lower its coupling intensity toward lower levels.

Proof sketch: by Field Chapter 17’s Directional Asymmetry Theorem, K\_{fⁿ} ≫ K\_{fⁿ⁻¹} → fⁿ’s highly compressed output finds it hard to unfold into lower-level differentiable differentials in fⁿ⁻¹ (highly compressed structures cannot unfold into independent entities at the fine-grained resolution of lower-level fields; FamOverlap extremely low) → Cpl(fⁿ → fⁿ⁻¹) low; in the reverse direction fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ: although fⁿ⁻¹’s detailed output is fine-grained, as effective compressed input it is more easily received and subsumed by fⁿ’s Shared Nuclei (Shared Nuclei have stronger absorption force for compressed input than analytical capacity for unfolded output) → Cpl(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ) relatively high; hence lower-to-upper more easily forms effective compressed input; upper-to-lower harder to unfold into lower-level differentiable differentials; lower-to-upper coupling strong, upper-to-lower coupling weak. ∎

Theorem 6: Layer-Hierarchy Genesis Conditions Theorem

For the layer-hierarchy structure ℒ = {f⁰, …, fⁿ} to obtain: f⁰ must first obtain (Genesis(f⁰)=1; Field Chapter 18); each level’s Overflow Sedimentation output must be sufficient to satisfy the upper level’s genesis conditions; and accumulated inter-level loss must not drive δ\_eff to zero at any level.

Proof sketch: by Field Chapter 18’s three Genesis conditions, fⁿ’s obtaining requires sufficient continuation-flow input (Condition 1); fⁿ’s primary continuation-flow source is Transfer(fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ); if Λ at some level is too high such that δ\_eff(fⁿ) \< Condition 1 threshold, then Genesis(fⁿ) = 0 and the layer hierarchy fractures at that level; hence a complete layer-hierarchy structure requires each level’s Transfer · (1−Λ) ≥ minimum continuation-flow density; f⁰ is the foundation of the entire layer-hierarchy structure—f⁰’s Death cascades to destabilize the entire layer hierarchy (recursive application of Theorem 3).
