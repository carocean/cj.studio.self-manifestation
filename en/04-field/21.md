# Field Scale Law

1\. Purpose

The preceding twenty chapters established single-field dynamics, Inter-Field Coupling, field genesis and dissolution, layer-hierarchy structure, and Observation-Domain conditions.

Field Chapter 19 introduced a preliminary distinction for Scale:

Layer Depth is vertical stacking—lower-level field output drives upper-level field;  
Scale is horizontal expansion—the size of Cap\_f among fields at the same level.  
The two are independent.

But Field Chapter 19 did not formally derive how Scale affects a field’s internal dynamics.

This chapter answers: how does a field’s scale systematically alter the effective ranges and dynamic tendencies of K\_f, R\_f, P\_s, φ\_f, and other core parameters?

1\. Core Proposition

Scale is not merely “field larger or smaller.”

Scale alters the effective boundaries of dynamic parameters—the same continuation-flow input produces systematically different compression effects, Resolution Margins, and Familiar Route lock-in in large-scale versus small-scale fields.

Therefore:

Scale ↑ ⇒ K\_f tends to increase, φ\_f tends to decrease, R\_f tends to decrease, conservatism tends to strengthen (under conditions of persistent input and long-term operation)

Scale ↓ ⇒ K\_f tends to decrease, φ\_f tends to increase, R\_f tends to increase, innovation tends to strengthen

This is not accidental but is the systematic result, at different scales, of the sedimentation field’s fundamental constraint: differential maintenance pressure within a finite capacity.

1\. The Formal Definition of Field Scale

Scale(f) = Cap\_f

That is, Field Scale equals the Sustainable Field Capacity (Field Capacity) defined in Field Chapter 15.

Explanation:

Cap\_f is the effective total domain that can simultaneously and stably maintain multiple independent continuation structures within Domain f (Field Chapter 15). It measures the “size” of the field in spatial/structural terms—how many independent differential relations it can accommodate.

Scale(f) directly uses Cap\_f without introducing a new quantity. Field Chapter 15 established Cap\_f as a constant (for a given field, the capacity ceiling does not change over time). Scale is a structural attribute, not a dynamic variable.

Distinction between Scale and Layer Depth (referencing Field Chapter 19):

Depth(f): vertical; the number of stacking levels in fⁿ⁻¹ → fⁿ;  
Scale(f) = Cap\_f: horizontal; how many independent differential structures f can accommodate;  
The two are independent—a high-level field is not necessarily large-scale; a large-scale field is not necessarily high-level.

1\. Scale and Compression Intensity

Why does a large-scale field (large Cap\_f) tend toward high Compression Intensity K\_f?

Precondition: the following derivation holds under “persistent input and long-term operation”—i.e., the field has received sufficiently many historical continuation-flow inputs and S\_f has had sufficient time to accumulate. A newly established large-scale empty field (large Cap\_f but extremely low S\_f) will not automatically have high K\_f; the Scale Law describes long-term statistical trends, not instantaneous necessities.

Derivation (under the precondition of persistent input and long-term operation):

Large Cap\_f → the field’s capacity ceiling is high; S\_f can continue accumulating without the field prematurely reaching overflow sedimentation; under equal continuation-flow input intensity, large-scale fields have a far higher S\_f accumulation ceiling than small-scale fields, and S\_f is systematically higher in long-term equilibrium;

S\_f high → P\_s(f,t) (Differential Maintenance Pressure, Field Chapter 13) ∝ N\_f·O\_f tends high (more inter-differential overlap relations; O\_f grows systematically with S\_f—Field Chapter 13; with large N\_f as the base in large-scale fields, the growth effect of N\_f·O\_f is more pronounced than in small-scale fields);

P\_s high → K\_f growth rate dK\_f/dt ∝ (1−K\_f)·max(0, P\_s−SDC\_f) tends larger (Field Chapter 13 Compression Dynamics equation);

Therefore: in large-scale fields, with other conditions equal, the equilibrium value of K\_f is systematically higher than in small-scale fields.

Intuition: large societies have a higher tendency toward categorical compression than small societies; large language systems have higher Shared Nucleus pressure on vocabulary than small dialects; large institutions have stronger rule-rigidification tendencies than small teams.

Formalized:

dK\_f/dt\|*{Scale=L} \> dK\_f/dt\|*{Scale=S}, when L \> S, other conditions equal.

Note: the relation between K\_f and Scale(f) is a statistical regularity, not a strict monotone. High-scale fields may have lower K\_f during certain periods (such as founding phases or post-overflow-sedimentation reorganization phases). But in long-term stable equilibrium, large-scale fields’ K\_f is systematically higher.

1\. Scale and Resolution Margin

Why does a large-scale field (large Cap\_f) tend toward low Resolution Margin R\_f?

Derivation:

R\_f = SDC\_f − S\_f (Field Chapter 12; Resolution Margin = Stable Differential Capacity − current differential count);

Large Cap\_f → S\_f can accumulate to higher levels without the field immediately reaching overflow sedimentation;

S\_f high and continuously growing → the effective ceiling of SDC\_f is consumed in long-term compression (K\_f rises; Field Chapter 13: SDC\_f’s effective capacity falls as K\_f compression increases);

Therefore: in long-term evolution, large-scale fields tend toward lower R\_f levels—not because SDC\_f is small, but because S\_f accumulation is systematically higher in large-scale fields.

Note: the absolute value of R\_f in large-scale fields may still be higher than in small-scale fields (because Cap\_f is large, the SDC\_f base is also large); but R\_f/Cap\_f (relative Resolution Margin) is lower in large-scale fields. Field Chapter 15’s Residual Capacity Ratio φ\_f = A\_f/Cap\_f is the correct quantity reflecting relative openness—large-scale fields’ φ\_f tends lower.

1\. Scale and Residual Capacity Ratio

Why does a large-scale field’s Residual Capacity Ratio φ\_f tend lower?

Note: Field Chapter 15 defines A\_f(t) as “the effective continuation capacity still open to new continuations” (a residual quantity); φ\_f = A\_f/Cap\_f is the Residual Capacity Ratio. φ\_f tending lower means A\_f decreasing relative to Cap\_f—i.e., open margin falls and the field tends toward saturation.

Derivation path (referencing Field Chapter 15’s dynamic equation):

dφ\_f/dt ∝ −S\_f − OccItr\_f − K\_f + α\_f (Field Chapter 15, Section 6)

In large-scale fields under persistent input and long-term operation:

S\_f (Compression Saturation Degree) ↑ (from Theorem 1: Scale↑ → K\_f↑ → S\_f↑; old structures occupy more effective continuation domain);

OccItr\_f (Occupied Interpenetration) ↑ (Scale↑ → more differential overlap accumulated, Field Chapter 14; interpenetration residue increases);

K\_f (Compression Intensity) ↑ (from Theorem 1);

All three negative-driving terms are systematically higher; while the recovery term α\_f in large-scale fields is systematically lower—due to high K\_f and high T\_replace threshold (Field Chapter 17), Familiar Route fracture and post-overflow-sedimentation domain release become more difficult;

Therefore: large-scale fields’ dφ\_f/dt is persistently negative in the long run; φ\_f is systematically lower in long-term equilibrium, corresponding to reduced open margin, declining independent new differential formation capacity, and difficulty in stably forming new differentials.

1\. Scale and Conservatism

Synthesizing the preceding three sections, Scale’s effect on conservatism:

Scale ↑ ⇒ K\_f ↑ ∧ φ\_f ↓ ∧ R\_f (relative) ↓ ⇒ conservatism ↑

Mechanism definition of conservatism: a field tends to absorb new input via old Familiar Routes rather than forming independent new differentials. High conservatism = high K\_f (broad Shared Nucleus coverage) + low φ\_f (little open margin) + relatively low R\_f (limited capacity to differentiate new differentials).

Core conclusion of the Scale Law:

Large-scale fields are not “more capable”—they are “more burdened by history.” Historical sedimentation occupies more structural space, making the generation of new structures increasingly difficult.

This explains:

Why large institutions struggle to innovate (large-scale cognitive/social fields: K\_f high, φ\_f low);  
Why ancient language vocabulary ossifies (long history = large scale × long time, K\_f extremely high);  
Why the brain tends toward conservatism with advancing age (neural field scale unchanged, but historical sedimentation accumulates continuously, φ\_f systematically falls).

1\. Scale Law and Its Relation to Field Chapter 18

The Scale Law has important intersections with field genesis and dissolution (Field Chapter 18):

A large-scale field’s Death\_C (Compression Death) threshold K\_dead is the same, but large-scale fields approach K\_dead faster—because large Scale → high P\_s → K\_f growth rate high;

Once a large-scale field reaches Death\_C, the deep-nucleus residue (δ\_res^C) it leaves is also stronger—because large Cap\_f means higher deep-nucleus sedimentation depth;

The conditions for a large-scale field to undergo Death\_F (Forgetting Death) are more stringent—it requires longer continuation-flow interruption to reduce the large-capacity field’s sedimentation depth fully below δᵣ.

Therefore: large-scale fields have stronger path dependence—both hard to dissolve (Forgetting Death threshold high) and hard to regenerate (Compression Death deep-nucleus residue strong).

1\. Scale Law and Its Relation to Field Chapter 17

Scale alters the dynamic conditions for Inter-Field Coupling (Field Chapter 17):

Large-scale B-Field: AnchorCapacity in absolute terms is high (many existing Familiar Routes), but φ\_{f\_B} is low (relative openness low) → Gate(f\_B)’s AnchorCapacity condition is easily satisfied, but the φ\_{f\_B} term’s contribution to Compat is low (note: Compat here references Field Chapter 17’s coupling operator Compat = φ\_{f\_B}·MatchTheta·FamOverlap\_eff, not Field Chapter 20’s Gate/Pass two-level formulation) → when a large-scale Observer Field receives new input, although anchor points are many, most new content passing through the gate is absorbed by old Familiar Routes (Field Chapter 17’s Domain-saturation pathway), and substantively new structures are hard to form;

Small-scale B-Field: φ\_{f\_B} high; new content more easily forms independent new differentials; but AnchorCapacity in absolute terms is low, and the anchor condition for coupling to obtain requires higher thresholds.

1\. Dynamic Equations

Scale definition equation:

Scale(f) = Cap\_f (directly equal to Field Chapter 15’s Sustainable Field Capacity)

Scale’s effect on compression dynamics:

Scale(f) ↑ ⇒ S\_f↑ (on average) ⇒ P\_s↑ ⇒ dK\_f/dt↑ (K\_f growth rate systematically higher)

Scale’s effect on Residual Capacity Ratio:

Scale(f) ↑ ⇒ K\_f↑, S\_f↑, OccItr\_f↑ ⇒ dφ\_f/dt persistently negative ⇒ φ\_f equilibrium value systematically lower

Scale’s effect on Resolution Margin:

Scale(f) ↑ ⇒ R\_f/Cap\_f (relative Resolution Margin) systematically lower

Overall Scale Law relations:

Scale ↑ ⇒ K\_f↑, φ\_f↓, R\_f (relative) ↓ ⇒ conservatism↑, independent new differential formation capacity↓

Scale ↓ ⇒ K\_f↓, φ\_f↑, R\_f (relative) ↑ ⇒ conservatism↓, independent new differential formation capacity↑

1\. Summary Expression

General Field Scale Law Expression:

Scale(f) = Cap\_f (Scale = Field Capacity; horizontal expansion quantity)

Scale ≠ Depth (Scale does not equal Layer Depth; the two are independent; referencing Field Chapter 19)

Scale ↑ ⇒ K\_f equilibrium value↑ (large systems: compression strong)

Scale ↑ ⇒ φ\_f equilibrium value↓ (large systems: openness low)

Scale ↑ ⇒ conservatism↑, path dependence↑, independent new differential formation capacity↓

Scale ↓ ⇒ openness↑, new differentials easier to form, but stability low (small-scale fields: K\_f low, structure less stable)

The core significance of the Scale Law: conservatism, rigidification, and innovation difficulty in real systems are not accidental but are the structural consequence of large-scale fields’ historical sedimentation persistently accumulating within a finite capacity. This law applies at every level of cognitive fields, language fields, social fields, and civilizational fields.

1\. Theorems

Theorem 1: Scale and Compression Positive Correlation Theorem

With other conditions equal, the larger Scale(f) is, the higher the long-term equilibrium value of K\_f; large-scale fields are systematically biased toward high compression.

Proof sketch: under the precondition of persistent input and long-term operation, Scale(f) = Cap\_f ↑ → S\_f accumulation ceiling↑ (this step requires persistent input and long-term operation; an empty field with S\_f ≈ 0 does not follow this chain); S\_f systematically higher in long-term equilibrium → P\_s ∝ N\_f·O\_f↑ (more inter-differential overlap, Field Chapter 13) → dK\_f/dt ∝ (1−K\_f)·max(0, P\_s−SDC\_f) equilibrium value higher → K\_f long-term equilibrium K̄\_f systematically higher as Scale↑; note: empty fields or large-scale fields in their founding phase have extremely low S\_f and this derivation does not apply; this is a long-term statistical regularity, permitting short-term exceptions. ∎

Theorem 2: Scale and Residual Capacity Ratio Negative Correlation Theorem

With other conditions equal, the larger Scale(f) is, the lower the long-term equilibrium value of φ\_f; large-scale fields are systematically biased toward low openness.

Proof sketch: from Field Chapter 15’s dynamic equation dφ\_f/dt ∝ −S\_f − OccItr\_f − K\_f + α\_f; under persistent input and long-term operation: Scale↑ → long-term S\_f↑ (large-scale fields have higher differential accumulation ceiling; saturation degree systematically higher); S\_f↑ → OccItr\_f↑ (more differential overlap accumulates, Field Chapter 14); Scale↑ → K\_f↑ (Theorem 1); all three negative-driving terms systematically higher; simultaneously K\_f high raises the T\_replace threshold (Field Chapter 17), α\_f (recovery term) systematically lower → dφ\_f/dt persistently negative → φ\_f long-term equilibrium value lower; corresponds to: large systems burdened by history, open margin scarce, independent new differential formation capacity systematically lower than small-scale fields. ∎

Theorem 3: Scale Does Not Equal Layer Depth Theorem

Scale(f) and Depth(f) are mutually independent; a large-scale field is not necessarily a high-level field; a high-level field is not necessarily a large-scale field.

Proof sketch: Scale(f) = Cap\_f is determined by horizontal capacity; Depth(f) is determined by the number of vertical stacking levels (Field Chapter 19 layer-hierarchy relation definition); the two definitions are completely independent—a high Cap\_f low-level field can be constructed (e.g., a large low-level neural field), as can a low Cap\_f high-level field (e.g., a highly compressed small-scale concept field); hence the two have no necessary correlation. ∎

Theorem 4: Large-Scale Conservatism Theorem

Large-scale fields (large Scale(f)) are more conservative than small-scale fields—i.e., large-scale fields tend to absorb new input via old Familiar Routes, and their capacity to form independent new differentials is systematically lower than small-scale fields.

Proof sketch: by Theorem 1, Scale↑ → K\_f↑ → broad Shared Nucleus coverage (Field Chapter 13); new input absorbed by old nuclei; by Theorem 2, Scale↑ → φ\_f↓ → fewer open continuation domains (Field Chapter 15); new differentials hard to stabilize; both together cause: large-scale fields process new input primarily by subsuming into old categories; independent new differential generation becomes increasingly difficult → conservatism↑; consistent with Outer Chapter 3 · Number Theory’s “when differentiation is finite, compression into one is inevitable”: heavy historical accumulation within finite capacity makes compression necessary. ∎

Theorem 5: Large-Scale Path Dependence Theorem

Large-scale fields depend on their own historical structures more than small-scale fields; once a large-scale field’s structure forms, the cost of changing it increases systematically with Scale.

Proof sketch: from Field Chapter 18, the intensity of deep-nucleus residue δ\_res^C after Death\_C is positively correlated with sedimentation depth → Scale↑ means historical sedimentation accumulates deeper → δ\_res^C stronger → old-nucleus residue strengthens; the probability of new input being absorbed by old nuclei ↑ → new field genesis subject to stronger path dependence (Field Chapter 18 Theorem 4); simultaneously, Scale↑ → φ\_f↓ → old Familiar Routes dominant in FamOverlap\_eff (Field Chapter 17) → coupling input absorbed by old nucleus → structural change requires stronger OutFlow exceeding T\_replace (Field Chapter 17 Theorem 5) to trigger fracture → cost of change increases systematically with Scale. ∎

Theorem 6: Scale-Openness Trade-off Theorem

A structural trade-off exists between Scale(f) and φ\_f: large-scale fields have large capacity but low openness; small-scale fields have high openness but small absolute capacity; under long-term stable conditions, large scale and high openness are hard to maintain simultaneously over the long run.

Proof sketch: φ\_f = A\_f/Cap\_f, where A\_f is the residual open continuation capacity (Field Chapter 15); with persistent continuation-flow input, Occupied Interpenetration (OccItr\_f↑), Familiar Route lock-in (K\_f↑), and increasing compression, A\_f systematically declines (dφ\_f/dt persistently negative, by Theorem 2); Cap\_f is a fixed ceiling → A\_f tends to decline over the long run → φ\_f tends lower over the long run; to forcibly maintain high φ\_f, forgetting, overflow-sedimentation fracture, or Familiar Route reorganization must release A\_f; but large-scale fields have high K\_f and high T\_replace threshold (Field Chapter 17), and release cost increases systematically with Scale; hence large-scale high-openness stable equilibrium is unsustainable; the system must trade off between scale and openness.
