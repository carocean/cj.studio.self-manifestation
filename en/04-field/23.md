These chapters each described the field’s dynamic behavior at different time scales and levels.

This chapter answers: if we place a field on a time axis, from Genesis to its final state, what is its complete evolutionary trajectory? What different evolutionary paths do different initial conditions and external environments lead to? Do universal evolutionary laws exist?

1\. Core Proposition

A field’s long-term evolution is not random drift but is shaped by three basic constraints:

Constraint 1 (Finite Capacity): Cap\_f is a constant; the field’s structural space has an upper limit—all differential accumulation is ultimately bounded by Cap\_f, and φ\_f tends lower over the long run.  
Constraint 2 (Differential Maintenance Pressure): P\_s ∝ N\_f·O\_f; the more differentials, the stronger the compression—the trend of K\_f increasing under persistent input is structural, not coincidental.  
Constraint 3 (Path Dependence): old nuclei’s absorption of new input causes historical accumulation to continuously reinforce itself—the field’s evolutionary direction is systematically constrained by existing structure.

The three constraints together produce:

The terminal tendency of field evolution: high K\_f, low φ\_f, high path dependence—i.e., structural conservatism, rigidification, and resistance to innovation.  
The sole mechanism for breaking out of this path: Shock \> T\_replace (Field Chapter 22)—external perturbation fractures old nuclei, enabling evolution to jump out of its established trajectory.

Therefore: a field’s long-term evolution is a cyclical structure of “progressive accumulation → compression and rigidification → either continuing to rigidify toward Death\_C, or awaiting a sufficiently strong perturbation to trigger fracture and reorganization.”

1\. The Three Stages of Field Evolution

A field, from Genesis to its terminal state, typically passes through three stages:

Stage 1 (Open Growth Phase): after Genesis, φ\_f is high, K\_f is low, S\_f grows rapidly, and new differentials are generated in abundance. The field is in a state of high openness, high sensitivity, and rapid accumulation. Corresponds to: young languages, emergent disciplines, startup institutions, developing neural systems.

Stage 2 (Compression-Rigidification Phase): φ\_f continuously falls, K\_f continuously rises, Familiar Routes deepen, Shared Nuclei strengthen, path dependence increases. The field is in a state of high stability, low openness, and perturbation resistance. New differential formation becomes increasingly difficult; input is increasingly absorbed by old nuclei. Corresponds to: mature languages, established paradigms, large institutions, adult neural systems.

Stage 3 (Terminal State): two possible terminal states:

Terminal State A (Compression Death): K\_f → K\_dead; Field Chapter 18 Death\_C occurs. Shared Nuclei cover the entire field; the field loses the capacity to independently process new differentials; it enters Compression Death. The residual deep nuclei provide initial sedimentation conditions for new field Genesis (genesis-dissolution closed loop, Field Chapter 18 Theorem 5). Corresponds to: language death, paradigm ossification at the eve of revolution, institutional collapse.

Terminal State B (Fracture-Reorganization after Perturbation): if external Shock \> T\_replace arrives before Compression Death occurs, the field enters the fracture-reorganization path (Field Chapter 22). Old nuclei fracture, φ\_f briefly rises, and the field enters a new Open Growth Phase (path now changed). Corresponds to: language revolutions, paradigm shifts, institutional reorganization, cognitive breakthroughs.

Note: Terminal State B does not guarantee success—if new continuation-flow direction is chaotic (Itr extremely low), the post-fracture field may enter Death\_F (Forgetting Death) rather than reorganization.

1\. Determinants of Evolutionary Rate

The rate at which a field moves from Stage 1 to Stage 2 is determined by the following factors:

Factor 1 (Scale, Field Chapter 21): the larger Scale(f) = Cap\_f, the higher the S\_f accumulation ceiling, the slower P\_s grows, and the longer Stage 1 persists; but once Stage 2 is entered, large-scale fields rigidify faster (P\_s multiplicative effect stronger).

Factor 2 (Continuation-Flow Input Intensity): the stronger the external input, the faster S\_f accumulates, the sooner P\_s reaches a high level, the faster K\_f rises, and the shorter Stage 1.

Factor 3 (SDC\_f, Field Chapter 12): the higher SDC\_f, the longer the field can maintain R\_f \> 0 at higher S\_f (Field Chapter 12), and the longer Stage 1 persists.

Factor 4 (Initial Sedimentation Conditions): the initial residue from old field δ\_res^C (Field Chapter 18 Genesis conditions)—if initial deep nuclei are strong, the field begins with higher K\_f; the Open Growth Phase is compressed (intergenerational transmission of path dependence).

1\. Cross-Field Dynamics of Evolution

Single-field evolution is not isolated—fields interact with surrounding fields through coupling (Field Chapter 17), layer hierarchy (Field Chapter 19), and perturbation cascade (Field Chapter 22):

Effect of coupling on evolution (Field Chapter 17): joint sedimentation (Itr, Field Chapter 14) among highly coupled fields accelerates Shared Nucleus formation, causing mutually coupled field clusters to enter Stage 2 faster than isolated fields; coupling interruption can trigger Death\_F.

Effect of layer hierarchy on evolution (Field Chapter 19): the evolutionary speed of lower-level fields affects the continuation-flow source of upper-level fields—when a lower-level field dies before the upper-level field, the upper-level field enters the Death\_F path as Transfer → 0; lower-level field perturbations are amplified through Transfer and reach upper-level fields (cascade effect, Field Chapter 22).

Effect of Scale on evolution (Field Chapter 21): large-scale fields typically have longer Open Growth Phases, but once they enter the rigidification phase, rigidification is deeper, Shock\_threshold is higher, and paradigm shifts require larger external shocks.

1\. Two Long-Term Equilibria of Evolution

Over a sufficiently long time dimension, field systems tend toward two long-term equilibria:

Equilibrium 1 (Compression Equilibrium): a state of high K\_f, low φ\_f, and high Shock\_threshold—the field is highly stable; most input is absorbed by old nuclei; paradigm shifts require extreme perturbation. This is the “default terminal state” of most long-running fields, unless a sufficiently strong external shock arrives. Corresponds to: mature civilizations, long-established languages, ossified disciplines.

Equilibrium 2 (Dynamic Equilibrium): under the maintenance of persistent sub-threshold perturbation (high-frequency perturbations of Shock slightly below T\_replace), the field maintains relatively high φ\_f and relatively low K\_f—this is not a static equilibrium but a “persistently perturbed openness.” It requires the external environment to continuously supply moderate-intensity diverse continuation-flow input; otherwise the field naturally slides toward Equilibrium 1. Corresponds to: active academic environments, competitive multilingual environments, continuously transforming institutions.

Therefore: a field’s “vitality” is not an intrinsic property of the field but a function of the external environment—a field can maintain Dynamic Equilibrium only under persistent diverse continuation-flow input from the external environment; otherwise it will ultimately move toward Compression Equilibrium.

1\. Observation-Domain and Field Evolution (Field Chapter 20)

A field’s evolution directly affects the obtaining and instability of the Observation-Domain (Field Chapter 20):

Stage 1 (Open Growth Phase): φ\_f high → Gate(f\_B) openness high; new content easily passes through Boundary-Gates; manifestation circuits readily form; Observation-Domain is rich and diverse; Alignment cost high (difficulty aligning multiple circuits simultaneously); convergence into one slow, but objects generated are fresh.

Stage 2 (Compression-Rigidification Phase): φ\_f low → Gate(f\_B) openness low; new content is mainly absorbed by old Familiar Routes; Observation-Domain tends toward fixed patterns; Alignment cost low (Shared Nuclei established, alignment automated); convergence into one fast, but object content tends toward repetition.

Terminal State A (Compression Death): K\_f → K\_dead → Gate → 0 (Domain saturation closure) → ObsRealm → 0 (Observation-Domain instability)—corresponds to perceptual closure; reality enters the Mute state.

Terminal State B (Fracture Reorganization): post-fracture φ\_f briefly surges → Gate briefly wide open → new content floods in → Observation-Domain briefly undergoes violent reconstruction—corresponds to the phenomenal-level descriptions of “insight events,” “sudden insight,” “cognitive breakthrough.”

1\. Theorems

Theorem 1: Field Evolution Necessity Theorem

Under conditions of persistent input, finite capacity, and no fracture perturbation (Shock \< T\_replace), K\_f shows a long-term statistical tendency to rise and φ\_f shows a long-term statistical tendency to fall; compression and rigidification are the structural necessity of long-term field evolution, not coincidence.

Proof sketch: from Field Chapter 13, dK\_f/dt ∝ (1−K\_f)·max(0,P\_s−SDC\_f); persistent input → S\_f↑ → P\_s↑; as long as P\_s \> SDC\_f, dK\_f/dt \> 0, K\_f tends to rise; note: Shock \> T\_replace causes K\_f to locally decrease (Field Chapter 22 Effect 3), so this is a statistical trend under the no-fracture-perturbation precondition; from Field Chapter 15, dφ\_f/dt ∝ −S\_f − OccItr\_f − K\_f + α\_f; S\_f↑ and K\_f↑ cause the negative-driving terms to be systematically stronger, while α\_f in most cases cannot compensate; hence φ\_f tends lower over the long run; Outer Chapter 3 · Number Theory’s “when differentiation is finite, compression into one is inevitable” provides the philosophical basis. ∎

Theorem 2: Structural Irreversibility of Evolutionary Phase Theorem

Under conditions of no external fracture perturbation (Shock \< T\_replace), the evolution from Stage 1 to Stage 2 is structurally irreversible—internal dynamics cannot return the field to its original open state (a stable state of lower K\_f and higher φ\_f).

Proof sketch: K\_f’s dynamic equation dK\_f/dt ≥ 0 (when P\_s \> SDC\_f) and K\_f ∈ \[0,1\]; K\_f can only rise or stop; no internal mechanism causes K\_f to systematically decline (note: Shock \> T\_replace is the sole mechanism causing K\_f to locally decrease, Field Chapter 22 Effect 3; but this case is excluded by this theorem’s precondition); φ\_f’s decline has recovery term α\_f, but α\_f typically cannot compensate the three negative-driving terms (Field Chapter 15) and cannot systematically reverse φ\_f’s declining trend; when Shock \< Shock\_threshold, old nuclei dominate recovery (Field Chapter 22 Theorem 4), maintaining path dependence; hence phase transition is structurally irreversible without fracture perturbation—α\_f can produce brief local recoveries, but cannot restore the overall open state of Stage 1. ∎

Theorem 3: Field Lifespan-Scale Relation Theorem

Under conditions of stable and comparable external continuation-flow input intensity, large-scale fields (large Scale = Cap\_f) have systematically longer lifespan than small-scale fields along the Death\_F path; along the Death\_C path, large-scale fields have a longer Stage 1, but their Stage 2 rigidification is deeper and K\_f’s approach to K\_dead is not necessarily slower—the two effects compete, and lifespan depends on the relative proportion of external input intensity to Scale.

Proof sketch: Death\_F path: Scale↑ → longer time needed for field-wide sedimentation depth to decay below δᵣ → longer lifespan (Field Chapter 18); Death\_C path: Scale↑ → higher S\_f accumulation ceiling → longer Stage 1 (Field Chapter 21 Theorem 1); but Scale↑ simultaneously → stronger P\_s growth effect (Field Chapter 21 Section 4) → rigidification in Stage 2 also faster; if external input intensity is unchanged and Scale increases, the absolute time for K\_f to approach K\_dead may not be significantly extended; hence large-scale fields’ lifespan advantage is primarily along the Forgetting Death path, and along the Compression Death path requires the additional precondition “comparable input intensity.” Note: in reality, large-scale systems (large corporations, large empires) often collapse faster than small systems due to overly deep Stage 2 rigidification—consistent with the two competing effects described above. ∎

Theorem 4: Paradigm Shift Necessary Conditions Theorem

Structural paradigm shift (from Stage 2 back to a new Stage 1) is possible only when Shock \> T\_replace; any perturbation below this threshold can only produce local adjustments and does not change the field’s overall evolutionary trajectory.

Proof sketch: Shock \< T\_replace → Shared Nuclei do not fracture (Field Chapter 22 Theorem 4) → recovery direction follows old nuclei → evolutionary trajectory unchanged (path dependence maintained); Shock \> T\_replace → Shared Nucleus fracture → K\_f locally decreases, φ\_f briefly rises → new continuation-flow can sediment in new directions → evolutionary trajectory switches; T\_replace ∝ SDC\_f·K\_f (Field Chapter 17) → fields deeper in Stage 2 have higher T\_replace → paradigm shift requires stronger perturbation; hence paradigm shift is a high-threshold event; necessary condition is Shock \> T\_replace. ∎

Theorem 5: Evolutionary Diversity Source Theorem

The diversity of evolutionary paths in field systems arises from: differences in initial Genesis conditions (δ\_res^C intensity), differences in Scale, and differences in external perturbation timing and intensity; given identical external continuation-flow input, these three together determine whether a field moves toward Terminal State A or Terminal State B.

Proof sketch: initial condition differences (Field Chapter 18): strong δ\_res^C → high initial K\_f → short Stage 1 → earlier entry into compression-rigidification; Scale differences (Field Chapter 21): large Scale → slower evolutionary pace → different responses to external perturbation at the same moment; perturbation timing differences (Field Chapter 22): if Shock \> T\_replace arrives before Death\_C → Terminal State B; if it arrives after Death\_C → field already dissolved; fracture-release fragments form new field Genesis; the combination of all three produces diverse evolutionary paths even under identical external continuation-flow. ∎

Theorem 6: Evolutionary Closed-Loop Theorem (Concluding Field Chapter Theorem)

The sedimentation-field system forms a complete evolutionary closed loop: a field accumulates and compresses from Genesis until it reaches Death; the δ\_res^C produced by death provides initial sedimentation conditions for new field Genesis; fracture-release triggered by external perturbation further accelerates this cycle; history therefore does not disappear but continuously shapes the evolutionary direction of successor fields in the form of residual structure.

Proof sketch: Field Chapter 18 Theorem 5: δ\_res^C produced by Death\_C satisfies new field Genesis conditions → old field death triggers new field birth; Field Chapter 18 Theorem 6: the structural direction of δ\_res^C influences the new field’s initial K\_f → path dependence transmitted across generations; Field Chapter 22 fracture-reorganization path: after Shock \> T\_replace triggers fracture, the released δ\_res^C fragments can accelerate new field Genesis → perturbation participates in the genesis-dissolution closed loop; hence the sedimentation-field system is not linear evolution (birth → aging → death) but cyclical: the residue of death shapes new birth; this constitutes the ultimate structural law governing sedimentation-field evolution—History sediments into the next cycle and never ceases to shape what follows.
