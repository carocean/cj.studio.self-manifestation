# The General Form of Computation

This chapter attempts a general proposition:

When a computation really occurs — whether its host is a circuit, a nervous system or some other physical organization, whichever operator is being computed, and by whatever mechanism the host realizes it — what is the structure that it necessarily carries and cannot lose? The initial stipulation of this group form writes the mathematical state transformation of a computation, and the relation under which the computation holds, as

　　Mₙ ≔ \[ Sₙ ──Tₙ(Iₙ?, Oₙ?)──→ Sₙ₊₁ \]  
　　Compₙ ⇔ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ

where Mₙ says that the present legitimate mathematical state transformation holds, Dₙ says that a device actually drives and implements that mathematical transformation, and Compₙ is the mathematical transformation driven by the device; Mₙ and Dₙ must refer to one and the same transformation. "Device" here means, generally, the physical host that bears the computation. These are stipulations that this group form takes as premises without proving them. Within this formulation: which structures does a computation necessarily carry? What does each of them bear, and can any be deleted? What exactly must be the same for the transformation to be "one and the same"? And which of the features commonly taken as marks of computation are not necessary at all?

Object: a computation Compₙ(𝔇, e, T), that is, the computation by host 𝔇, in event e, of the mathematical transformation operator T. The antecedent is a computation that already holds; the necessity of the three argument places, and of the event's belonging to the host, is proved in Steps One and Two of the Line of Argument. The boundary of 𝔇, the interval of e, and T together with its input and output slots stay fixed throughout every question asked; to change any one of them is to change the object or the declaration to which the present computational qualification applies, and the qualification to hold of the original proposition may not be carried over directly to the new one. Fact of holding: on e, the two items of the initial stipulation are true and refer to one and the same transformation. Scope: a single transformation step; the operator T is one whose legitimate transformation relation is already given — for a relational operator, its graph. Logical strength: Compₙ ⇒ N, the necessary direction; the converse is given by the initial stipulation and is not separately proved here. Non-degeneracy conditions: computations really do occur — for example, a working adder computes 5 from 2 and 3, a calculator computes 15 from 7 and 8, and an increment circuit raises a register from 5 to 6; and there really are physical events that are not computations — for example, a memory chip stores a 5 that an outsider, having looked at the operands, worked out mentally and wrote in (in general, a memory device stores a result that someone outside its boundary obtained from the operands held in it and then wrote in; obtaining that result is not a computation by that device), a powered-off flash memory holds a 5 for an hour, and an indicator lamp that is driven to light up is read by someone as 5. None of these is a computation, by the host in question, of the corresponding operator.

Working domain and boundary of inheritance: the antecedent is that Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) already holds. The inherited item is this group form's initial stipulation of computation: the primitive definition — computation is restricted to mathematical operations driven by a device; the formal definition — Mₙ, Compₙ ⇔ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ, the requirement that Mₙ and Dₙ refer to one and the same transformation, and the glosses on each symbol; and the bearers of computation — the brain when exercising the capacity to compute, and computing devices. Their conditions of application — that the host, the event, the operator with its input and output slots, and the legitimate transformation are all given — are the object and scope stated above. The determination problem — whether a given physical event satisfies Dₙ, and by what physical organization a host implements an operator — is adjacent to the present question but distinct from it. It is a determination problem; it is to be taken up by further work on *The Determination Form of Computation* and is not yet established within the group, and it enters neither the antecedents nor the conclusions of this form.

How far this question is answered must be stated in advance. The subquestions actually generated and answered are as follows.  
First: does a single result value already amount to an operation? No. There must be three places — prior state, operator, posterior state — while input and output slots may or may not be present (Step One). Proved.  
Second: does a mathematical transformation that holds already amount to a computation? No. There must be an event of a host (Step Two). Proved.  
Third: does the coexistence of an event of the host and a mathematical transformation already amount to a computation? No. There must be an assignment taking the states of the event to mathematical states, and the transformation must hold on the readings of that event; Mₙ is therefore a proposition at the event level (Step Three). Proved.  
Fourth: does a transformation that holds on the readings of a host's event already amount to a computation? No. Agreement of readings does not bring with it the host's actual driving, and Dₙ does not follow from the event-level Mₙ (Step Four). Proved. Dₙ is inherited from the initial stipulation as a condition of holding; its physical criteria are a determination problem.  
Fifth: if Mₙ and Dₙ each hold somewhere, is there already a computation? No. The two must take the same host, the same event and the same operator, and which transformation was computed must be fixed by one and the same state correspondence (Step Five). Sameness of event: proved. Sameness of state correspondence: proved at the single-transformation level. The operator item is given by the initial stipulation. The relation between Mₙ and Dₙ is settled at the same time: the inheritance reading of Dₙ is fixed — "that mathematical transformation" refers back to the legitimate transformation of which Mₙ speaks, so that Dₙ ⇒ Mₙ — and declared as Computation General Form-H1. Under this reading Mₙ can be absorbed by Dₙ in the logical expression, but its prior state, operator, posterior state and legitimacy are the necessary content to which Dₙ refers back.  
Sixth: are a program, a user's intention, an observer's read-out, a particular encoding, a change of output, external input and output, completion of a task, all causal action lying within the host, a particular kind of host, or a particular evaluation mechanism necessary? None of them is necessary. Proved (Step Six). Single-valuedness is not in N; whether it is necessary involves the identity of the operator on a single event, and is unanswered.  
General conclusion: the structure a computation necessarily has is the event-level Mₙ and Dₙ on one and the same set of arguments ⟨𝔇, e, ρₑ, T⟩. The two bear two distinct conceptual duties — that the transformation is legitimate, and that the host actually drives and implements it — and neither can be lost. Dₙ does not follow from Mₙ; under H1, Mₙ follows from Dₙ and can be absorbed in the logical expression, so the two are not mutually non-implying independent predicates. "One and the same" requires sameness of event; which transformation was computed is fixed by the state correspondence on which Mₙ and Dₙ both hold, and an Mₙ and a Dₙ taken on different state correspondences cannot be pieced together into this computation. This structure is inherited from the initial stipulation and obtained from it by analysis, and is equivalent to Compₙ. What is obtained is therefore: definitional equivalence; a distinction of duties; and the necessity of each duty relative to the candidate deletions and weakenings tested. N is the explicit unfolding of the two conceptual duties of the initial stipulation and of the sameness relation between them. Global minimality — that no equivalent and simpler structural expression exists — does not follow from the definitional equivalence alone, and is not proved; indeed, under H1, Mₙ ∧ Dₙ is equivalent to Dₙ. Dₙ is left unfolded within this structure; whether Dₙ has a finer necessary structure within it is unanswered. Except for "Mₙ follows from Dₙ" and its corollaries, which are conditional on H1, every item is proved and unconditional.

In this chapter we shall find, through persistent questioning, where the proof must go, and derive the general form of computation.

Our objective is:

starting from the initial stipulation and from uncontested facts of computation, to unfold item by item the arguments and relations implicit in the compressed formula of the initial stipulation; to prove that each duty is required by a computation and cannot be lost; to ascertain the implication relations among the items; to prove that none can be weakened to the candidate relations tested; to rule out the non-necessary features commonly mistaken for necessary ones; and to state the relation between the compressed formula and this unfolded structure, together with the strength of what this form proves.

The result first.

What follows is the abridged formula of the results of this chapter. The proofs are in the Line of Argument and the Proof of Minimal Structure; the formal general formula, the general theorem and the read-back are in "General Formula and General Theorem," and the formal definitions in the Appendix.

　　Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) ⇒ N(𝔇, e, T)  
　　N(𝔇, e, T) :≡ e ∈ Evt(𝔇) ∧ ∃ρₑ \[ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) \]  
　　Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) :⇔ Leg\_T(x, y), x = ( ρₑ(p\_e), I\_e ), y = ( ρₑ(p′\_e), O\_e )  
　　Single-transformation level: Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) :⇔ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ), Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) :⇔ ∃ρₑ Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ)

Here e is an event of the host 𝔇, with initial and final physical states p\_e and p′\_e; ρₑ is the state correspondence that maps this pair of initial and final states, together with the input and output slots of T, to the mathematical states x and y, and this chapter represents it in the form of an assignment; Mₙ says that the passage from x to y is a legitimate state transformation of T; Dₙ says that 𝔇 actually drives and implements, in e, this transformation of T. Mₙ and Dₙ must correspond to the same event of the host, the same operator and the same pair of mathematical prior and posterior states; this chapter expresses that sameness by a shared ρₑ. Equivalent representations of the state correspondence do not affect the qualification to hold.

Relation to the compressed formula (the initial stipulation): Mₙ ≔ \[Sₙ ──Tₙ(Iₙ?, Oₙ?)──→ Sₙ₊₁\] and Compₙ ⇔ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ are the compressed notation for this structure. The Sₙ and Sₙ₊₁ of the compressed formula are the mathematical states that ρₑ assigns to the initial and final states of e; Iₙ and Oₙ are the I\_e and O\_e on the input and output slots of T; the words "device" and "that" in Dₙ supply 𝔇, e and the sameness of the arguments. The expanded formula adds no condition beyond the compressed formula; it only writes out the arguments and the sameness requirement that the compressed formula leaves implicit. Under H1, Dₙ refers back to the legitimate transformation of Mₙ, and the Mₙ ∧ Dₙ of the compressed formula can logically be written as Dₙ. Under H1, writing Mₙ out in the compressed formula sets apart the content to which Dₙ refers back; it does not add an independent condition. Under the execution reading, Mₙ is a conjunct that cannot be dropped.

In One Sentence

A number alone cannot say what was computed; a formula alone has nothing computing it; readings that happen to agree do not mean that the host drove the transformation; a transformation here and a driving there do not add up to one computation. A computation is one legitimate mathematical state transformation, on one and the same event of one and the same host, under one and the same state correspondence, actually driven and implemented by that host; programs, intentions, watching, encodings and changes of output beyond this are not things it necessarily carries.

Abstract

The initial stipulation of computation has already said that a computation is a legitimate mathematical state transformation actually driven and implemented by a host, the two referring to one and the same transformation. This stipulation gives the definition of computation; what arguments each of its two items carries, and what must be the same for the transformation to be "one and the same," the stipulation itself does not unfold. Starting from a computation that already holds, this chapter asks these questions.

Starting from a working adder and stripping it down step by step: the screen, the onlooker, pen and paper, the encoding and the external ports can all be removed, and the computation remains. Strip it down to a single result value, 5, and it can no longer be said what was computed this time: there must be three places — prior state, operator, posterior state — which is the form of Mₙ; input and output slots may or may not be present. With only this mathematical transformation and no host at all, the transformation still holds, but no computation has occurred: there must be an event of a host. When the event and the transformation have nothing to do with each other, that transformation has not been computed in the event: the initial and final states of the event must be assigned mathematical states such that the transformation holds on these readings, and Mₙ is therefore a proposition at the event level.

Agreement of readings is still not computation. A powered-off flash memory that holds a 5 for an hour agrees in its readings with the identity transformation; a memory chip that stores a 5 an outsider worked out mentally and wrote in agrees in its readings with addition; an indicator lamp driven to light up, with off read as (2, 3) and on read as 5, also agrees in its readings with addition. None of the three is a computation. By the initial stipulation, Dₙ fails under the assignment that makes Mₙ hold; hence Dₙ does not follow from the event-level Mₙ. Dₙ is inherited as a condition of holding, and its physical constitution is not unfolded.

If Mₙ and Dₙ each hold in a different place, they can be pieced together into a computation that does not exist. The 2 + 3 = 5 an outsider wrote into the chip has Mₙ, and the calculator beside it computing 7 + 8 has Dₙ, but "2 + 3 was computed here" is false. In one and the same clock cycle, an increment circuit raises R from 5 to 6 while an outsider rewrites R′ from 2 to 3; under one state correspondence there is an Mₙ of succ(2) = 3, under another there is a Dₙ, and "succ(2) = 3 was computed" is false as well. The two must therefore take the same event; and "which transformation was computed" requires that Mₙ and Dₙ hold together on one and the same state correspondence. Sameness of operator is given by the initial stipulation. The semantics of Dₙ admits competing readings. This chapter fixes the inheritance reading — in "a device actually drives and implements that mathematical transformation," "that mathematical transformation" refers back to "the present legitimate mathematical state transformation," so that Dₙ ⇒ Mₙ — and declares it as H1. Under H1, a faulty adder that gives 6 on (2, 3) is not a Dₙ of addition: either it implemented another operator, or it implemented no legitimate transformation at all. Under H1, Mₙ can be absorbed by Dₙ in the logical expression, but its prior state, operator, posterior state and legitimacy are precisely the content to which Dₙ refers back, and they are not lost by being absorbed.

A program, a user's intention, an observer's read-out, a particular encoding, a change of output, external input and output, completion of a task, all causal action lying within the host, a particular kind of host and a particular evaluation mechanism are each lacking in some uncontested computation, and are not necessary structure. Single-valuedness is not in N; whether it is necessary is unanswered.

Proved: the event-level Mₙ and Dₙ on one and the same set of arguments are the necessary structure of a computation, and neither of the two conceptual duties can be lost; Dₙ does not follow from Mₙ; under H1, Mₙ follows from Dₙ and can be absorbed in the logical expression, so the two are not mutually non-implying independent predicates; sameness of event cannot be split into separate existence, and at the single-transformation level sameness of state correspondence cannot be split into separate existence; none of the features listed above is necessary. The structure obtained is equivalent to Compₙ, and this equivalence is inherited from the initial stipulation. N is the explicit unfolding of the two conceptual duties of the initial stipulation and of the sameness relation between them; global minimality does not follow from this equivalence alone, and is not proved. Whether Dₙ has a finer necessary structure within it is unanswered. Except for "Mₙ follows from Dₙ" and its corollaries, which are conditional on H1, the conclusions are unconditional.

Preliminary Distinctions

What follows only fixes the object and the terms; it does not bear the necessity of any structural item.

Host. A host 𝔇 is a physical system, delimited by a given boundary, that actually bears a computation; it is not presupposed to be an electronic device. The neural and bodily processes of a person exercising the capacity to compute, and an analogue apparatus working with continuous quantities, can each be such a host. The initial stipulation calls it a "device," or a "host"; in this chapter the two words refer to the same thing. One and the same physical process can fall within different boundaries; every judgement in this chapter is relative to the given 𝔇.

Event. An event e is a stretch of physical history over an interval \[t\_e, t′\_e\]; it may be a transition of state or the maintenance of a state. Its initial and final physical states are written p\_e and p′\_e. If e is part of the history of the host 𝔇, we write e ∈ Evt(𝔇); why a computation requires this belonging is proved in Step Two. "A computation" means one completed step of computation; it does not mean that the host has some capacity, nor that a whole task has been completed.

Operator and its input and output slots. An operator T is given with its legitimate transformation relation Leg\_T: Leg\_T(x, y) says "the passage from x to y is a legitimate state transformation of T." For a relational operator (single-valued, multivalued or identity), Leg\_T(x, y) :⇔ (x, y) ∈ graph(T). T also specifies its input slots and output slots — the Iₙ? and Oₙ? of the initial stipulation: which quantities are inputs newly handed in at this step, and which are outputs newly handed out; the question mark indicates that these two places may be empty. For operators of other types, Leg\_T is a problem of mathematical representation and is not unfolded in this form; this form holds for every operator whose Leg\_T is given.

State assignment. ρₑ assigns mathematical states to the initial and final physical states of e, and reads the quantities handed in through the input slots of T and handed out through its output slots during e as I\_e and O\_e: x = ( ρₑ(p\_e), I\_e ), y = ( ρₑ(p′\_e), O\_e ). ρₑ is defined only on the initial and final states of e and on the input and output slots of T; it is an assignment, not someone's act of reading. It is defined even when no one is watching, and it may be recognized by someone only afterwards. Whether a computation occurs is not borne by the assignment itself; one event can have many assignments, and under most of them the transformation of T does not hold. ρₑ is the formal tool by which this chapter represents the correspondence between the states of a physical event and mathematical states. A computation necessarily involves a correspondence between the physical states of that occasion and mathematical states, but there need not actually be, inside the host, a function or code table called ρₑ; the same correspondence may equally be given by a binary relation between physical states and mathematical states, or by some other representing map. Different but equivalent forms of representation do not change the conclusions of this chapter. When we say below that "the assignment cannot be deleted," we mean that the duty of state correspondence cannot be lost, not that a particular mathematical notation cannot be replaced.

Two readings of Mₙ. The Mₙ of the initial stipulation can be read as a mathematical proposition — (x, y) is a legitimate transformation of T, independent of any event; we write this A\_T(x, y) — or as an event-level proposition — the pair of readings of some event under some assignment is a legitimate transformation of T. Which of the two readings is the meaning of Mₙ within a computation is settled in Step Three; A\_T is not used as a synonym for Mₙ.

Dₙ. Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) says: 𝔇 actually drives and implements, in e, that mathematical transformation of T, its prior and posterior states being those assigned by ρₑ. This is an item of the initial stipulation, inherited by this form as it stands; no defining formula or criterion for it is given here. The semantics of Dₙ admits two competing readings. The initial stipulation glosses Mₙ as "the present legitimate mathematical state transformation holds" and Dₙ as "a device actually drives and implements that mathematical transformation"; it further stipulates that "Mₙ and Dₙ must refer to one and the same transformation," and glosses Compₙ as "the mathematical transformation driven by the device." The inheritance reading: "that" refers back to the present legitimate mathematical state transformation, and what is "implemented" is this transformation, not a stretch of operating that can yield a wrong result; hence Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ⇒ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ). The execution reading: "that mathematical transformation" refers only to the present step from Sₙ to Sₙ₊₁, its legitimacy being asserted separately by Mₙ; under this reading a miscomputation can satisfy Dₙ without satisfying Mₙ. The wording of the initial stipulation supports the inheritance reading (Compₙ is glossed as "the mathematical transformation driven by the device," and the two "must refer to one and the same transformation"), but does not exclude the execution reading (it lists Mₙ separately as a conjunct). This chapter fixes the inheritance reading and declares it as Computation General Form-H1. What depends on H1 is only the sentence "Mₙ follows from Dₙ" and its corollaries (Compₙ ⇔ e ∈ Evt(𝔇) ∧ ∃ρₑ Dₙ; the verdict on miscomputation). Under the execution reading, the former becomes "Mₙ does not follow from Dₙ," and the corollaries fail; the standing of sameness of state correspondence at the event level also depends on the reading (the single-transformation level is unaffected); the remaining conclusions are the same under both readings. The reasoning below makes only two uses of Dₙ: inferring that it holds from the holding of Compₙ; and inferring, from the uncontested fact that some transformation is not a computation, by way of the initial stipulation, that it fails under the corresponding assignment.

Computation of a single transformation. Under the event-level reading, the initial stipulation is written Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) :⇔ ∃ρₑ \[ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) \] (see Proof of Minimal Structure, Part Two). The conjunction in brackets is written Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) and read "𝔇 computes, in e, this transformation x → y of T"; it merely names the same conjunction of the initial stipulation and adds no condition. On one and the same event one transformation may be computed while another, whose readings agree, is not; for the latter, Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) fails.

Operating conditions. Conditions on which the running of the host depends and which come from outside its boundary, such as an external power supply, an external clock, temperature and environment.

Line of Argument

We proceed by persistent questioning, one step at a time; this forms a line of argument, to be read from top to bottom.

One

What is now known is the initial stipulation of computation and the non-degeneracy facts. On these alone, is it already clear what structure a computation minimally carries?

It is not. The initial stipulation gives Compₙ ⇔ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ, hence Compₙ ⇒ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ. But these are only two names. Mₙ can be read as a mathematical proposition independent of any event, or as an event-level proposition; what objects Dₙ carries, and what must be the same for "one and the same transformation," are left unstated by the wording. Read Mₙ as a mathematical proposition and Dₙ as "somewhere a host is implementing some transformation," and the formula still reads smoothly — but it no longer speaks of "a" computation. Let us first ask what the mathematical-operation item itself is.

Start from that adder and strip it down step by step, to see where it stops being that computation. Take away the screen: the result remains in an internal register, and the computation does not vanish because no one sees it. A person computes mentally, without pen or paper, and the computation does not vanish either. With another encoding or another material, addition can still be done. An internal update of state can take place without receiving data from outside or handing a result out. Display, watching, pen and paper, a particular material, newly added input and output: none of these is common necessary structure of a computation.

Strip it down to a single result value, 5, and things change. A 5 stored in advance, a 5 written on paper and a 5 produced by addition have the same value; "there is a 5" cannot say "this time 5 was obtained from 2 and 3." What is missing is not more display but this occasion's operation itself. If the prior state has 2 and 3 and the posterior state has 5, but no relation from the former to the latter is specified in between, then this pair of end points may be one application of addition or one application of some other relation — the same pair of end points can be described by many relations, and end points cannot on their own bear "what was computed." There must therefore be a place for a transformation relation, T. Remove the prior state, and "5 obtained by addition" has no state acted upon; remove the posterior state, and one speaks only of what may be done, not of a completed transformation. The prior state can be a vector of registers, a state of mental operation or a continuous quantity; the posterior state may equal the prior state — an identity transformation still has two places, before and after.

Hence there must be three places — prior state, operator, posterior state — together with input and output slots that may be empty: that is, the form of the Mₙ of the initial stipulation. What is forced here are the logical places needed to state fully this occasion's mathematical transformation — prior state, operator, posterior state — not three separable components that the host must physically possess; these places entail no requirement on hardware or physical organization. What is forced is only that "there must be a place for an operator"; whether a single event alone suffices to tell which operator the host implemented is the question of the identity of the operator on a single event, which is unanswered; see the Declaration of Gaps.

Two

Given prior state, operator and posterior state, is there already a computation?

Imagine a world with no host at all. A\_+((2, 3), 5): ((2, 3), 5) is one legitimate application of addition, and this mathematical fact holds in such a world as well; it does not vary with the world. A 2 + 3 = 5 written on paper is only a record of this fact. But the Dₙ of the initial stipulation requires that a device actually drive the transformation, and the primitive definition of computation restricts computation to mathematical operations driven by a device; in this world nothing is computing. The mathematical transformation holds; no computation has occurred.

What is missing is an occurrence. There must be an event e of a host 𝔇, with initial and final physical states: e ∈ Evt(𝔇). This is an object, not a relation; it is supplied by the words "device" and "that" in Dₙ.

Three

Given an event of a host, and a mathematical transformation as well, is there already a computation?

Let e be a calculator's computation of 7 + 8 now in progress, or a stone's fall now in progress; and let ((2, 3), 5) be a legitimate application of addition. Both are present, but "2 + 3 was computed in e" has no ground: the Sₙ of the initial stipulation is the state of this step, and the physical states of e have no connection whatever with (2, 3) or 5. What is missing is a connection between the event and the mathematical states.

For this there must be an assignment ρₑ that reads the initial and final physical states of e, together with the input and output slots of T, as x and y; the transformation must hold on this pair of readings. Hence Mₙ can only take the event-level reading:

　　Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) :⇔ Leg\_T(x, y), x = ( ρₑ(p\_e), I\_e ), y = ( ρₑ(p′\_e), O\_e )

The A\_T of Step Two is what remains of this proposition once the event and the assignment are removed: that it holds implies no Mₙ on any event. The assignment does not analyse the host's mechanism; it says only what the initial and final states of this step of the host are as mathematical states. The initial stipulation uses Sₙ for the state already present, that is, the complete expression of structures and structural relations, and stipulates that a state is the complete expression of the structures and structural relations, at some step of a transformation, sufficient to bear the transformation that follows; Sₙ is therefore the state at this step of the transformation, and the assignment is the unfolding of this point on the host's event.

Four

Given an event of a host and an assignment, and a transformation that also holds on the readings of that event, is there already a computation?

No. Fix three cases.

First. 𝔇 is a powered-off flash memory chip; e is one hour, during which a state is maintained; T is the identity on the integers 0 to 255, with no input or output slots; ρ reads the charge state of a certain memory cell as 5 both at the start and at the end. The pair of readings (5, 5) belongs to the graph of the identity, so Mₙ holds. During this hour the flash memory merely holds a 5; this is not its computation of the identity — a non-degeneracy fact.

Second. 𝔇 is a memory chip containing registers A, B and Z and a write port; T is integer addition (a, b) ↦ a + b, with no input slots; ρ⁰ reads the contents of A and B as operands and the content of Z as the result; e is a stretch of time during which A and B hold 2 and 3, and a person outside the boundary looks at A and B, works out 5 mentally, and writes 5 into Z through the write port. The pair of readings is (2, 3) and 5, so Mₙ holds. The sum was worked out by that person — a person exercising the capacity to compute is a host of computation, and a computation did occur, only not in this chip; the chip did not compute the addition in e — a non-degeneracy fact. A different question — whether this chip implemented the operator "load into Z the value delivered by the write port" — is a question about another computation, and is not used to confirm the qualification at issue in the first question.

Third. 𝔇 is a calculator in operation; e is a stretch of time during which it drives an indicator lamp from off to on, without performing any operation; T is integer addition, with no input slots; ρ reads the lamp as the carrier of both the operands and the result, off as (2, 3) and on as 5. Mₙ holds. In e the calculator merely lit a lamp; it did not compute 2 + 3 — a non-degeneracy fact.

In all three cases Compₙ fails, while Mₙ holds under the given assignment. By the initial stipulation, Compₙ :⇔ ∃ρₑ \[ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ \]; the failure of Compₙ means that Mₙ ∧ Dₙ fails under every assignment; hence Dₙ fails under the given assignment. Therefore Dₙ does not follow from the event-level Mₙ: agreement of readings does not bring with it the host's actual driving. What is missing is exactly the second item of the initial stipulation. To prove this, a single valid counterexample suffices; the three cases bear it out from different angles and do not exhaust the ways in which Dₙ can fail.

This argument does not need to know what Dₙ physically consists in: it uses only the uncontested fact that "these three cases are not computations" and the initial stipulation itself. The three cases fail in different ways — no mechanism is acting at all; the result is obtained outside the boundary and written in; the host is acting, but not operating — which shows that a host's driving and implementing a transformation cannot be reduced to any single physical feature. The reasoning above uses only the definitional relation between Dₙ and Compₙ.

Five

Given Mₙ on one and the same event, and knowing that Dₙ cannot be missing, is there already a computation? Not if Mₙ and Dₙ merely each hold somewhere in the system.

Fix two cases. First: 𝔇₁ is the memory chip of the second case in Step Four, e₁ is that stretch of time, the assignment is ρ⁰, and T is integer addition; 𝔇₂ is a calculator in operation, e₂ is one operation in which it computes 15 from 7 and 8, and the assignment ρ₂ reads its two operand registers and its result register. On e₁, Mₙ holds; e₂ is a computation, and Dₙ(𝔇₂, e₂, +; ρ₂) holds. To say "this system computed 2 + 3" is false: e₁ is not a computation (Step Four). Second: 𝔇 is a set of registers R and R′ in operation, where R is written by an increment circuit of 𝔇 and R′ has a separate write port; e is one clock cycle; T is the successor n ↦ n + 1, with no input slots. During e, the increment circuit raises R from 5 to 6, while at the same time a person outside the boundary looks at the 2 in R′, works out 3, and writes it back into R′ through the port. The assignment ρ\_b reads R, and ρ\_a reads R′. Under ρ\_b, this is a computation by the increment circuit, and both Mₙ and Dₙ hold; under ρ\_a, Mₙ holds, with readings 2 → 3. Pieced together, these would say "succ(2) = 3 was computed in e"; but the 3 in R′ was worked out and written in by the person outside the boundary, of the same kind as the second case in Step Four, and by the non-degeneracy facts the transformation 2 → 3 is not a computation by 𝔇: Compₙ(𝔇, e, succ; ρ\_a) fails. Here Compₙ(𝔇, e, succ) itself holds (by way of ρ\_b), so the single-transformation notation must be used: Mₙ(𝔇, e, succ; ρ\_a) holds while Compₙ(𝔇, e, succ; ρ\_a) fails, hence Dₙ(𝔇, e, succ; ρ\_a) fails.

The two failures have the same source: Mₙ and Dₙ fall on different events or on different assignments. The two must therefore take one and the same set of arguments: the same 𝔇 and e, the same T, and (at the level where a single transformation is specified) the same state correspondence ρₑ. This is not a further condition added outside Mₙ and Dₙ; it is the two sharing one set of arguments, and it is exactly the content of the initial stipulation's "Mₙ and Dₙ must refer to one and the same transformation." Sameness of state correspondence is especially indispensable: one event can have many state correspondences, and it is the state correspondence that fixes which pair x, y "this transformation" is; if what Dₙ implements is not the pair of which Mₙ speaks, it is not one and the same transformation. This requirement must be stated at the single-transformation level: "𝔇 computes, in e, this transformation x → y of T" is Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ), which requires Mₙ and Dₙ to hold on the same ρₑ; the second case shows that an Mₙ under one state correspondence on an event, combined with a Dₙ under another state correspondence on the same event, does not yield a computation under the first state correspondence. At the event level, Compₙ(𝔇, e, T), the Compₙ(𝔇, e, succ) of the second case already holds by way of ρ\_b; under H1, Dₙ brings with it the Mₙ on its own state correspondence, so "an Mₙ under some state correspondence and a Dₙ under some state correspondence" already implies ∃ρₑ (Mₙ ∧ Dₙ). At the event level, therefore, sameness of state correspondence is not a component independent of Dₙ; its content lies at the single-transformation level — which transformation was computed is fixed by the state correspondence on which Dₙ holds. The operator item: on the same event and under the same assignment, a pair of readings can belong to two operators at once (a multiplication circuit obtains 4 from (2, 2), and the pair of readings also belongs to addition); whether the host also implemented addition on this occasion depends on the identity of the operator on a single event, for which the initial stipulation gives no criterion, so sameness of operator is given by the initial stipulation; for this item there is no witness of failure that does not depend on a judgement of operator identity.

In this same step the relation between Mₙ and Dₙ is settled. Under the inheritance reading fixed in the Preliminary Distinctions (H1), "that mathematical transformation" in Dₙ refers back to the legitimate transformation of which Mₙ speaks; when Mₙ fails, there is no "that legitimate mathematical state transformation" to be implemented, and Dₙ fails; so Dₙ ⇒ Mₙ, and Mₙ ∧ Dₙ is logically equivalent to Dₙ. An adder that, because of one fixed defect, outputs 6 on the operands (2, 3) — a miscomputation — is therefore not a Dₙ of addition: the host either implemented another operator or implemented no legitimate transformation at all. The relation between the two items must accordingly be stated faithfully. Mₙ and Dₙ bear two distinct conceptual duties: the former bears "what was computed, and whether it is legitimate," the latter bears "it really was computed by this host." Under the inheritance reading Mₙ can be absorbed by Dₙ in the logical expression, but its prior state, operator, posterior state and legitimacy remain the necessary content to which Dₙ refers back. What is proved, then, is that neither of these two structural duties can be lost; under H1 the two are not independent of each other as predicates. Under the execution reading, the same miscomputing adder satisfies Dₙ without satisfying Mₙ, and Mₙ is a conjunct that cannot be dropped; the conclusion that neither duty can be lost is unchanged.

Six

We now have the event-level Mₙ and Dₙ on one and the same set of arguments. Does a computation necessarily carry any further structure that can be stated independently?

First consider what position any proposed addition X can occupy at the level of the two conceptual duties. Suppose X is a necessary condition of a computation, that is, Compₙ ⇒ X. Under the event-level reading the initial stipulation gives Compₙ ⇔ N, hence N ⇒ X: X is a consequence of N, not a condition outside N. X can come from only three sources. First, X changes Mₙ, Dₙ or their shared reference — it changes the initial stipulation and asks about a different concept of computation, not about the necessary structure of this concept. Second, X follows from Mₙ, from the event and state-correspondence arguments, and from the sameness relation — it is already in N and is not a new duty. Third, X does not follow from Mₙ and its arguments alone, but follows from N — it necessarily involves Dₙ, or the joint relation between Dₙ and Mₙ and their shared reference; to involve Dₙ is not the same as to follow from Dₙ alone. If X is merely a logical consequence of the two existing duties, it adds no independent condition of holding; if X requires a further account of why Dₙ holds, or of what joint relation can actually implement this transformation, its specific structure has not yet been unfolded in this form. Since Mₙ is a mathematical proposition about the readings of an event, the ground of every necessary condition that further involves the host's process of implementation necessarily involves Dₙ: it may be part of the internal constitution of Dₙ, or a further constraint on the relation between Dₙ and the mathematical transformation. At the level of the two conceptual duties, then, N already fully expresses the conditions under which a computation holds; any new necessary proposition is a consequence of N and does not constitute an independent condition of holding outside N. Further analysis of Dₙ, and of its relation to the mathematical transformation, belongs to another level not yet unfolded. This distinction uses only definitional equivalence, and requires no physical analysis.

The examination of proposed additions therefore falls into two classes. Those of the first class are lacking in some uncontested computation, and so are not consequences of N — they are not necessary conditions; a witness for each is given below, each using a computation that can be confirmed without any theory for determining Dₙ. To determine whether those of the second class are necessary, one must first determine whether certain contested apparatuses implemented T, that is, analyse Dₙ further; they are left to that other level.  
　A program, or rewritability: a hard-wired adder circuit has no program, and it computes addition. Not required.  
　A user's intention: an adder circuit started in advance, with no one present, computes 2 + 3 = 5 from its inputs on its own; on this occasion no user holds any intention towards it, and it computed that addition. Not required.  
　An observer's read-out: a server that no one checks during the night still computes; ρₑ is an assignment, not an act of reading, and when a researcher identifies the encoding of a register only after the operation, that computation does not thereby fail to hold. Not required.  
　A particular encoding: one and the same eight-bit adder computes addition modulo 2⁸ under both the unsigned and the two's-complement assignment. Some assignment is required; no particular assignment is.  
　An observable change of output: in the program step "R ← R + 0," the posterior state has the same value as the prior state; the Mₙ of the initial stipulation does not require Sₙ₊₁ to differ from Sₙ. Not required.  
　External input or external output: an internal increment of a register neither receives data from outside nor hands a result out; the Iₙ? and Oₙ? of the initial stipulation are places that may be empty. Not required.  
　Completion of a whole task: in long multiplication, the step that computes one partial product is one step of computation; Compₙ is a single step. Not required.  
　Single-valuedness: N requires only Leg\_T(x, y); relational operators include multivalued relations, and N contains no requirement of single-valuedness. But whether a host that yields some root of x² = 4 implements the multivalued relation {(4, 2), (4, −2)} or some choice function is a question of the identity of the operator on a single event. Single-valuedness is therefore not listed here as a proved exclusion; see the Declaration of Gaps.  
　All causal action lying within the host: an adder powered by an external supply and driven by an external clock computes addition. Not required.  
　A particular kind of host: an electronic calculator and a person doing mental arithmetic both compute addition; the initial stipulation explicitly lists the brain when exercising the capacity to compute, and computing devices, as bearers of computation. Not required.  
　A particular evaluation mechanism: an electronic summing circuit, an adder circuit that looks up a table in read-only memory, and a person doing mental arithmetic all compute addition, with different evaluation mechanisms. Not required.  
To the second class belong: designed function, giving correct results on alternative operands as well, and all intermediate evaluation lying within the host. To determine whether they are necessary, one must first determine whether certain contested apparatuses implemented T in some event, that is, analyse Dₙ further; whether they are necessary is unanswered; see the Declaration of Gaps.

At this point the structure that can be unfolded from the initial stipulation and the uncontested facts has been unfolded. What the steps above have obtained now passes to the test of minimal structure.

Proof of Minimal Structure

This part keeps three kinds of conclusion apart: (a) necessity — Compₙ implies each item; (b) indispensability of duty — no item's duty can be borne by the remaining items, together with a faithful statement of the implication relations among the items; (c) the level of closure — in what sense the structure obtained omits nothing, and in what sense this is not proved.

One. Classification by load-bearing role

The structure obtained consists of two relations, Mₙ and Dₙ, on a set of arguments ⟨𝔇, e, ρₑ, T⟩, together with the sameness of the two relations' arguments.  
　The argument T with its input and output slots, and the three places of prior state, operator and posterior state: forced by Step One of the Line of Argument; the form is inherited from the Mₙ of the initial stipulation.  
　The arguments 𝔇 and e ∈ Evt(𝔇): forced by Step Two; their necessity is obtained by analysis of the words "device" and "that" in the Dₙ of the initial stipulation.  
　The argument ρₑ (the state correspondence, represented as an assignment) and the event-level reading of Mₙ: forced by Step Three; their necessity is obtained by analysis of the Sₙ (the state of this step) and of "refer to one and the same transformation" in the initial stipulation. What is necessary is the duty of state correspondence, not the notation of an assignment.  
　The relation Mₙ: its content is inherited from the Mₙ item of the initial stipulation; its event-level reading is forced by Step Three.  
　The relation Dₙ: inherited from the Dₙ item of the initial stipulation; Dₙ ⇒ Mₙ is an item conditional on H1; that Dₙ does not follow from the event-level Mₙ is proved by Step Four; its internal structure and instance criteria are not in this form.  
　Sameness of arguments: sameness of event is forced by Step Five; sameness of state correspondence is forced by Step Five, its content lying at the single-transformation level; the operator item is inherited from the initial stipulation's "refer to one and the same transformation."  
The initial stipulation Compₙ ⇔ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ is an inherited definition, and its conditions of citation — that the host, the event, the operator with its input and output slots, and the legitimate transformation are all given — hold within the working domain of this form. The content of each item is given by the initial stipulation; what the unfolding adds is: making the arguments and the sameness requirement explicit, the necessity and indispensability of each duty, the implication relations among the items, and the verdicts on the non-necessary features. This conclusion is an analytic consequence of the initial definition of computation.

Two. Necessity

Convention on quantifiers. The Sₙ and Sₙ₊₁ of the initial stipulation are the mathematical states of this step; under the event-level reading, they are the values assigned to the initial and final states of 𝔇 in e. Under the event-level reading obtained in Steps One to Three of the Line of Argument, the initial stipulation is therefore written: Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) :⇔ ∃ρₑ \[ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) \], where one and the same ρₑ gives both the Sₙ and Sₙ₊₁ of which Mₙ speaks and the prior and posterior states of "that" transformation which Dₙ implements. This biconditional is a composite of the initial stipulation and what Steps One to Three obtained, not the wording of the initial stipulation; both of its directions come solely from the initial stipulation Compₙ ⇔ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ and from the event-level reading.

Suppose Compₙ(𝔇, e, T).  
Event: Dₙ says that the host actually drives that transformation, and this transformation occurs on 𝔇; hence e ∈ Evt(𝔇).  
State correspondence: the Sₙ and Sₙ₊₁ of Mₙ are the states of this step, and what Dₙ implements is "that" transformation; the two refer to one and the same transformation, so there is a correspondence between the initial and final states of e and the mathematical states of this step, represented by ρₑ.  
Mₙ: the Mₙ item of the initial stipulation, which at the event level is Leg\_T(x, y): Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ).  
Dₙ: the Dₙ item of the initial stipulation: Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ).  
Sameness of arguments: Mₙ and Dₙ must refer to one and the same transformation, so they take one and the same ⟨𝔇, e, ρₑ, T⟩.  
Hence Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) ⇒ N(𝔇, e, T). This completes the proof.

This proof uses only the initial stipulation and the event-level reading obtained in Steps One to Three; it does not use H1.

Three. Indispensability of duty, and the implication relations

Each item has been proved necessary in Part Two. Delete any one of them, and the conjunction of the remaining items is still a necessary condition, only a weaker one; deletion does not by itself make the remaining structure "no longer necessary." What must be proved here is therefore that no item's duty can be borne by the remaining items; and at the same time the implication relations among the items must be stated faithfully. "Indispensability of duty" is not "mutual non-implication of predicates": a duty can be carried by another predicate's reference back to it, in which case it is absorbed in the logical expression yet remains content that cannot be lost. Apart from the non-degeneracy facts, the witnesses below make no judgement of computational qualification about any event. W1, W2 and W6, the e₁ in W4, the transformation under ρ\_a in W5 and W3′ are all contrast cases in which Compₙ fails; they are anticipated degenerate cases outside the task, used in the proofs of non-implication and of non-separability, and are not breaches of the general theorem. W3, the e₂ in W4 and the transformation under ρ\_b in W5 are computations.

Relations:  
　Dₙ does not follow from Mₙ: witnesses W1, W2, W6. In all three, Mₙ holds under the given assignment; by the non-degeneracy facts, none of the three is a computation; by the initial stipulation, Dₙ fails under the given assignment (Step Four).  
　Mₙ follows from Dₙ: under H1, Dₙ ⇒ Mₙ (Step Five); under the execution reading this becomes "Mₙ does not follow from Dₙ" (W3′). As a conjunct, Mₙ can be absorbed in the logical expression; the prior state, operator, posterior state and legitimacy it bears are precisely the content to which Dₙ refers back, and they are not lost by being absorbed. Hence the duty of Mₙ cannot be lost; under H1, Mₙ is not independent of Dₙ as a predicate.

Arguments: an argument is a place in a relation, not a proposition that can hold or fail on its own. To say here that a place cannot be deleted is to say that the duty it bears cannot be lost — not that a particular notation cannot be replaced, nor that the host must physically have a corresponding separable component; prior state, operator and posterior state are the logical places needed to state fully this occasion's mathematical transformation. Delete a place, and the corresponding relation can no longer be stated. Without the place of the operator, Mₙ cannot be stated — the same pair of end points belongs to many relations, and what was computed cannot be fixed (Step One; W3 shows that, for the readings of one and the same event, Mₙ for T and Mₙ for T′ can differ). Without the event, Dₙ cannot be stated, and Mₙ is reduced to A\_T (Step Two). Without the state correspondence, neither Mₙ nor Dₙ is any longer a proposition about this transformation of e (Step Three).

Sameness of arguments: sameness of event — an Mₙ and a Dₙ on different events do not yield a computation on the event in question (W4). Sameness of state correspondence — at the single-transformation level, an Mₙ and a Dₙ on different state correspondences do not yield a computation under the first state correspondence (W5); at the event level, under H1, Dₙ brings with it the Mₙ on its own state correspondence, the split formula is equivalent to N, and sameness of state correspondence is not a component independent of Dₙ. A Dₙ elsewhere does not supply the implementation for the Mₙ here.

Witnesses. Each witness fixes the host, the event, the operator with its input and output slots, and the assignment.  
　W1 Powered-off flash memory. 𝔇: a powered-off flash memory chip. e: one hour, during which a state is maintained. T: the identity on the integers 0 to 255, with no input or output slots. ρ: the charge state of a certain memory cell is read as 5 at both start and end. Mₙ: (5, 5) belongs to the graph of the identity; holds. Non-degeneracy fact: not a computation. Hence Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρ) fails.  
　W2 Memory chip written by an outsider. 𝔇: a memory chip containing registers A, B and Z and a write port. e: the stretch of time fixed in the second case of Step Four. T: integer addition, with no input slots. ρ⁰: A and B are read as operands and Z as the result; the readings are (2, 3) and 5. Mₙ: holds. Non-degeneracy fact: the chip did not compute the addition in e. Hence Dₙ(𝔇, e, +; ρ⁰) fails.  
　W3 Multiplication circuit. 𝔇: a multiplication circuit in operation. e: one operation computing 6 from 2 and 3. ρ: the two operand registers are read as operands and the result register as the result. For multiplication, Mₙ holds; for addition, ((2, 3), 6) does not belong to the graph of addition, and Mₙ fails. Used to show that the place of the operator cannot be deleted, and that Mₙ for T cannot be weakened to Mₙ for some operator.  
　W3′ Locally faulty adder. 𝔇: an adder in operation that, because of one fixed defect, outputs 6 on the operands (2, 3) and is correct on all other operands. e: one operation. T: integer addition, with no input slots. ρ: the usual assignment of registers; the readings are (2, 3) and 6. Mₙ: ((2, 3), 6) does not belong to the graph of addition; fails. Under H1, Dₙ(𝔇, e, +; ρ) also fails; under the execution reading, Dₙ holds. Under H1, W3′ serves as the illustration in Step Five: that a host carried out a stretch of operating is not the same as its having implemented a legitimate transformation of T; under the execution reading, it is the witness for "Mₙ does not follow from Dₙ."  
　W4 Piecing together. 𝔇₁, e₁, ρ⁰, T: as in W2. 𝔇₂: a calculator in operation; e₂: one operation in which it computes 15 from 7 and 8; ρ₂: reads its two operand registers and its result register; T: integer addition. Mₙ(𝔇₁, e₁, +; ρ⁰) holds; Dₙ(𝔇₂, e₂, +; ρ₂) holds (a non-degeneracy fact); Dₙ(𝔇₁, e₁, +; ρ⁰) and Compₙ(𝔇₁, e₁, +) both fail (W2).  
　W5 R and R′. 𝔇: a set of registers R and R′ in operation, R written by an increment circuit of 𝔇, R′ with a separate write port. e: one clock cycle. T: the successor, with no input slots. ρ\_b: reads R, 5 → 6, one computation by the increment circuit (a non-degeneracy fact); both Mₙ and Dₙ hold. ρ\_a: reads R′, 2 → 3, the 3 having been written in through the port by a person outside the boundary after looking at the 2 in R′; Mₙ holds; of the same kind as W2, so by the non-degeneracy facts Compₙ(𝔇, e, succ; ρ\_a) fails, hence Dₙ(𝔇, e, succ; ρ\_a) fails.  
　W6 Indicator lamp. 𝔇: a calculator in operation. e: a stretch of time during which it drives an indicator lamp from off to on, without performing any operation. T: integer addition, with no input slots. ρ: off is read as (2, 3), on as 5. Mₙ: holds. Non-degeneracy fact: not a computation. Hence Dₙ(𝔇, e, +; ρ) fails.

Four. Test of non-weakenability

For each necessary item X and each candidate weakening W, prove X ⇒ W and W ⇏ X. X has been proved necessary; the structure obtained by putting W in place of X is still necessary, but strictly weaker. This part tests only the candidates below; the conclusion obtained is "cannot be weakened to the candidate relations tested," not "no weaker expression whatever can bear the same duty."  
　X = event-level Mₙ, W = A\_T(x, y). X ⇒ W: evident. W ⇏ X: in the world without a host, W holds and there is no event at all.  
　X = Mₙ for T, W = "there is an Mₙ for some operator T′." X ⇒ W: take T′ = T. W ⇏ X: W3, where there is an Mₙ for multiplication and none for addition.  
　X = event-level Mₙ ∧ Dₙ, W = event-level Mₙ. X ⇒ W: evident. W ⇏ X: W1, W2, W6.  
　X = sameness of event, W = "there is an Mₙ on some event and a Dₙ on some event." X ⇒ W: take the same event. W ⇏ X: W4, where Mₙ(e₁) ∧ Dₙ(e₂) holds and Compₙ(𝔇₁, e₁, +) fails.  
　X = sameness of state correspondence at the single-transformation level, that is, Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρ) requires Mₙ and Dₙ to hold together on ρ; W = "Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρ), and there is a Dₙ on some state correspondence ρ′ of the same event." X ⇒ W: take ρ′ = ρ. W ⇏ X: W5, where Mₙ(ρ\_a) ∧ Dₙ(ρ\_b) holds and Compₙ(𝔇, e, succ; ρ\_a) fails. At the event level this W is not a strict weakening: under H1, ∃ρ Mₙ ∧ ∃ρ′ Dₙ implies N.

Five. Duties cannot be merged

The four arguments have different duties: the host and the event give the occurrence; the state correspondence gives the connection between the occurrence and the mathematical states, and fixes which pair of states "this transformation" is; the operator gives the mathematical object computed. The occurrence cannot be borne by the state correspondence (in a world without a host one can write down assignments at will); the state correspondence cannot be borne by the occurrence (Step Three); and neither can be borne by the operator. The duties of the two relations also differ: Mₙ says that this occasion's readings accord with T, and bears "what was computed, and whether it was computed legitimately"; Dₙ says that the host actually drives and implements this transformation, and bears "it really was computed." The former does not bear the latter (W1, W2, W6); under H1 the latter carries the former by way of reference back (Dₙ ⇒ Mₙ), but what it carries is exactly the content of the former, not a further duty of its own. Sameness of arguments bears "one and the same," and cannot be borne by the two relations separately (W4; W5 at the single-transformation level). Encoding several arguments or relations as one composite relation, or representing the state correspondence in some other equivalent form, is — so long as each duty is preserved — another way of writing the same structure, and does not change the conclusions above.

Six. The strength of the three kinds of conclusion

(a) Necessity: the event-level Mₙ and Dₙ on one and the same set of arguments; proved unconditionally. The proof is an analysis of the initial stipulation and does not use H1.  
(b) Indispensability of duty, and the implication relations: that Dₙ does not follow from Mₙ is proved; that Mₙ follows from Dₙ, that it can be absorbed in the logical expression without loss of duty, and that the two are not independent of each other — this sentence is conditional on H1; the three duties of operator, event and state correspondence cannot be lost; sameness of event cannot be split into separate existence; sameness of state correspondence at the single-transformation level cannot be split into separate existence; none of the items can be weakened to the candidate relations listed in Part Four — proved.  
(c) Level of closure: by the biconditional of the initial stipulation under the event-level reading, N(𝔇, e, T) ⇒ Compₙ(𝔇, e, T), and N is equivalent to Compₙ. This equivalence proves that the two hold over the same range; it does not prove that the items of N are simplest in every equivalent expression, nor does it exclude an equivalent and simpler structural expression — that, under H1, Mₙ ∧ Dₙ is equivalent to Dₙ is one example. What has been obtained is therefore three things: definitional equivalence; a distinction of duties (Part Three); and the necessity of each duty relative to the candidate deletions and weakenings tested (Parts Three and Four). N is the explicit unfolding of the two conceptual duties of the initial stipulation and of the sameness relation between them, and is closed in this sense: at this level, any new necessary proposition is a consequence of N and does not constitute an independent condition of holding outside N; necessary conditions that further involve Dₙ — the internal constitution of Dₙ, or further constraints on the relation between Dₙ and the mathematical transformation — belong to another level not yet unfolded (Step Six). Global minimality has not been proved by the definitional equivalence alone. This conclusion is inherited, not an independent discovery of this form. N retains Dₙ as it stands, without unfolding it; whether Dₙ has a finer necessary structure within it is unanswered, belongs to the determination problem, and is to be taken up by further work on *The Determination Form of Computation*; it is not yet established within the group.  
The structure obtained is therefore the unfolding of the necessary structure of the initial stipulation's concept of computation at the level of its two conceptual duties, and its minimality is the necessity of each duty relative to the candidates tested. It does not prescribe a unique physical realization; and since Dₙ is not unfolded within it, it cannot be used to determine whether a given physical event is a computation.

General Formula and General Theorem

Formal general formula:

　　Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) ⇒ N(𝔇, e, T)  
　　N(𝔇, e, T) :≡ e ∈ Evt(𝔇) ∧ ∃ρₑ \[ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) \]  
　　Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) :⇔ Leg\_T(x, y), x = ( ρₑ(p\_e), I\_e ), y = ( ρₑ(p′\_e), O\_e )  
　　Single-transformation level: Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) :⇔ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ), Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) :⇔ ∃ρₑ Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ)  
　　Types of the arguments: 𝔇 is a host delimited by a given boundary; e is an event; T is an operator whose Leg\_T and input and output slots (which may be empty) are given; ρₑ is the state correspondence (represented as an assignment) on the initial and final states of e and the input and output slots of T  
　　H1 ⇒ \[ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ⇒ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) \] (the inheritance reading of Dₙ)

Compressed formula (the inherited initial stipulation):

　　Mₙ ≔ \[ Sₙ ──Tₙ(Iₙ?, Oₙ?)──→ Sₙ₊₁ \]  
　　Compₙ ⇔ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ, where Mₙ and Dₙ must refer to one and the same transformation

The relation between them: the Sₙ and Sₙ₊₁ of the compressed formula are the mathematical states that ρₑ assigns to the initial and final states of e; Iₙ and Oₙ are the I\_e and O\_e on the input and output slots of T; the words "device" and "that" in the Dₙ of the compressed formula supply 𝔇 and e; "refer to one and the same transformation" means that Mₙ and Dₙ share one and the same ⟨𝔇, e, ρₑ, T⟩. The expanded formula adds no condition beyond the compressed formula.

Read-back: 𝔇 is a physical host delimited by a given boundary; e is a stretch of physical history over an interval, with initial and final physical states, and e ∈ Evt(𝔇) says that it is part of the history of 𝔇; T is a mathematical operator given with a legitimate transformation relation Leg\_T (for a relational operator, its graph) and with input and output slots, which may be empty; ρₑ represents the correspondence between the initial and final states of e, together with the input and output slots of T, and the mathematical states x and y, and fixes which pair of states "this transformation" is; it is a formal representation and does not require that a corresponding function or code table exist inside the host. Mₙ says that the passage from x to y is a legitimate state transformation of T. Dₙ says that 𝔇 actually drives and implements, in e, this transformation of T, and is inherited from the initial stipulation; under H1, this transformation is the legitimate transformation of which Mₙ speaks, and Dₙ ⇒ Mₙ; its interior is not unfolded. Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) says that 𝔇 computes, in e, this transformation x → y of T. Mₙ and Dₙ correspond to the same event of the host, the same operator and the same pair of mathematical prior and posterior states; this chapter expresses that sameness by a shared ρₑ, and equivalent representations of the state correspondence do not affect the qualification to hold. The scope of "∃ρₑ" is the bracketed expression that follows it. "⇒" is implication, not causation or temporal order; "∧" is conjunction. The general formula asserts only the necessary direction; the converse is given by the initial stipulation.

General Theorem (the necessary structure of computation)  
(i) Necessity: for any host 𝔇, event e and operator T whose Leg\_T and input and output slots are given, Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) ⇒ N(𝔇, e, T).  
(ii) Indispensability of duty: Dₙ does not follow from the event-level Mₙ on the same arguments; neither of the two structural duties can be lost; under H1, Mₙ follows from Dₙ and can be absorbed in the logical expression, while its prior state, operator, posterior state and legitimacy remain the necessary content to which Dₙ refers back, so that the two are not independent of each other as predicates (under the execution reading this becomes "Mₙ does not follow from Dₙ"); each of the three duties of operator, event and state correspondence is required by the corresponding relation; sameness of event cannot be split into separate existence; at the single-transformation level, sameness of state correspondence cannot be split into separate existence; none of the items can be weakened to the candidate relations listed in Part Four of the Proof of Minimal Structure.  
(iii) Exclusion: a program, a user's intention, an observer's read-out, a particular encoding, a change of output, external input or output, completion of a task, all causal action lying within the host, a particular kind of host, and a particular evaluation mechanism are each lacking in some uncontested computation, and are not necessary structure of computation.  
(iv) Equivalent unfolding of the initial stipulation: by the biconditional of the initial stipulation under the event-level reading, N(𝔇, e, T) ⇒ Compₙ(𝔇, e, T), hence Compₙ ⇔ N; N is the explicit unfolding of the two conceptual duties of the initial stipulation and of the sameness relation between them. Definitional equivalence and the distinction of duties are obtained; global minimality does not follow from this equivalence alone, and is not proved. This equivalence is inherited; it is not a sufficient condition proved by this form. Dₙ is not unfolded in N; whether Dₙ has a finer necessary structure within it is unanswered, and no instance criterion for Dₙ can be obtained from N.

Proof. (i) Proof of Minimal Structure, Part Two. (ii) Proof of Minimal Structure, Parts Three and Four. (iii) Line of Argument, Step Six. (iv) The biconditional of the initial stipulation under the event-level reading, Compₙ :⇔ ∃ρₑ \[ Mₙ ∧ Dₙ \] (Proof of Minimal Structure, Part Two), together with the fact that e ∈ Evt(𝔇) is contained in Dₙ. This completes the proof.

Declaration of strength: (i), (iii) and (iv) are unconditional; they rest only on the initial stipulation, the event-level reading obtained in Steps One to Three of the Line of Argument, and the non-degeneracy facts. In (ii), the sentence "Mₙ follows from Dₙ" is conditional on H1 — Dₙ ⇒ Mₙ holds by the inheritance reading on which "that mathematical transformation" in the initial stipulation refers back to the legitimate transformation, and it is not declared as a logical theorem independent of the initial stipulation; under H1, the Mₙ ∧ Dₙ of the compressed formula is logically equivalent to Dₙ. The remaining items of (ii) are unconditional. What (ii) proves is indispensability of duty, not mutual non-implication of predicates. (iv) is inherited, not an independent proof: its strength is definitional equivalence together with the distinction of duties, N being the explicit unfolding of the two conceptual duties and of the sameness relation between them; global minimality has not been proved by the definitional equivalence alone; it does not reach the interior of Dₙ. The claim of minimality is limited to the candidate deletions and weakenings tested in Parts Three and Four of the Proof of Minimal Structure. The conclusion obtained is an unfolding of the necessary structure of a concept of computation already laid down; its strength falls short of a physical law of computation or of a criterion for determining computation.

Breach

To give a computation, compatible with the initial stipulation, that lies on no event of any host, or for whose event there is no correspondence between event states and mathematical states under which this transformation holds on the event's readings and is implemented by the host: breaches (i).  
To prove that Dₙ follows from the event-level Mₙ on the same arguments — that is, that one of W1, W2, W6 is in fact a computation, or that Mₙ fails under the given assignment: breaches the corresponding item of (ii).  
To prove that the Dₙ of the initial stipulation does not refer back to the legitimate transformation of Mₙ, that is, that it should take the execution reading: negates H1; the sentence "Mₙ follows from Dₙ" in (ii) is rewritten as "Mₙ does not follow from Dₙ," and the standing of sameness of state correspondence at the event level is accordingly left undetermined; (i), (iii), (iv) and the remaining items of (ii) are unaffected.  
To prove that sameness of event can be split into separate existence without loss of content, that is, that e₁ in W4 is in fact a computation by 𝔇₁; or to prove that sameness of state correspondence at the single-transformation level can be split apart, that is, that the transformation 2 → 3 under ρ\_a in W5 is in fact a computation by 𝔇: breaches the corresponding item of (ii).  
To give an uncontested instance that lacks one of the items excluded by (iii) and is judged not to be a computation precisely because it lacks that item; or to prove that one of the instances cited in (iii) is not a computation: breaches the corresponding item of (iii).  
Not a breach: that it is hard to determine whether some physical event satisfies Dₙ; finding some physical criterion for Dₙ, or a counterexample to one; finding some finer necessary structure within Dₙ (the general theorem contains no assertion that there is none); representing the state correspondence in some other equivalent form; another physical realization of the same function.

Appendix: Formalization

One. Arguments  
𝔇: a physical host delimited by a given boundary.  
Evt(𝔇): the set of events of 𝔇; each e ∈ Evt(𝔇) carries an interval \[t\_e, t′\_e\] and initial and final physical states p\_e and p′\_e, and may be a transition or a maintenance.  
T: an operator, given with Leg\_T and with input slots In(T) and output slots Out(T), either of which may be empty; for a relational operator, Leg\_T(x, y) :⇔ (x, y) ∈ graph(T).  
ρₑ: an assignment on {p\_e, p′\_e} and on In(T) and Out(T), representing the correspondence between event states and mathematical states (a formal tool; an equivalent representation by a relation does not change the conclusions); x = ( ρₑ(p\_e), I\_e ), y = ( ρₑ(p′\_e), O\_e ).

Two. Relations  
A\_T(x, y) :⇔ Leg\_T(x, y) (a mathematical proposition, independent of any event).  
Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) :⇔ Leg\_T(x, y), with x and y given by ρₑ on e (event level).  
Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ): 𝔇 actually drives and implements, in e, that mathematical transformation of T, its prior and posterior states being those assigned by ρₑ (the initial stipulation, inherited; its interior is not unfolded).  
H1 (the inheritance reading): Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ⇒ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ("that" refers back to the present legitimate mathematical state transformation; pending verification).  
Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) :⇔ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) (the computation of a single transformation, that is, the name of the conjunction of the initial stipulation).  
Compₙ(𝔇, e, T) :⇔ ∃ρₑ Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) (the initial stipulation written under the event-level reading).  
N(𝔇, e, T) :≡ e ∈ Evt(𝔇) ∧ ∃ρₑ \[ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρₑ) \].

Three. Conclusions  
(i) Compₙ ⇒ N.  
(ii) ¬(Mₙ ⇒ Dₙ) (W1, W2, W6); H1 ⇒ (Dₙ ⇒ Mₙ), and under H1, Mₙ ∧ Dₙ ⇔ Dₙ, the duty of Mₙ being retained through Dₙ's reference back to it; ¬(A\_T(x, y) ⇒ Mₙ); ¬(∃T′ Mₙ(·, T′) ⇒ Mₙ(·, T)) (W3); ¬\[ Mₙ(𝔇₁, e₁, T; ρ₁) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇₂, e₂, T; ρ₂) ⇒ Compₙ(𝔇₁, e₁, T) \] (W4); ¬\[ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρ\_a) ∧ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρ\_b) ⇒ Compₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρ\_a) \] (W5, single-transformation level); under H1, ∃ρ Mₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρ) ∧ ∃ρ′ Dₙ(𝔇, e, T; ρ′) ⇒ N(𝔇, e, T) (at the event level there is no separate component of sameness of state correspondence).  
(iii) A program, a user's intention, an observer's read-out, a particular encoding, a change of output, external input or output, completion of a task, all causal action lying within the host, a particular kind of host, and a particular evaluation mechanism are each not necessary (Line of Argument, Step Six).  
(iv) N ⇒ Compₙ (the initial stipulation under the event-level reading; inherited), hence Compₙ ⇔ N; under H1, this is also Compₙ ⇔ e ∈ Evt(𝔇) ∧ ∃ρₑ Dₙ. N is the explicit unfolding of the two conceptual duties and of the sameness relation between them; global minimality has not been proved by the definitional equivalence alone; whether Dₙ has a finer necessary structure within it is unanswered.
