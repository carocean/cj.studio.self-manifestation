# The General Specification of Forms

Introduction

What this chapter treats is not only the internal writing specification of On Self-Manifestation, but a methodological problem that has long lacked unified treatment: what are the basic directions of theoretical proof?

Logic, mathematical proof theory, axiomatic systems, experimental method, and model-building mainly answer: whether an inference is valid; whether a theorem holds; whether a model fits the facts; whether an experiment supports a hypothesis. The question of whether different theoretical tasks inherently demand different kinds of proof has been touched at points by these traditions, but rarely made an independent object and built into a system that can be selected from and reviewed.

The answer of this chapter is: different tasks demand different proofs.

Proving what an object must minimally possess in order to stand, and proving how it is precisely represented and run, are not the same task; proving that a result necessarily occurs under conditions, and proving that an object can exist, are not the same task; proving what an object possesses, and proving what at most can be known from observed results, must never be conflated.

The basic proof directions therefore divide into five principal forms: the General Form proves necessary constitution (O ⇒ N); the Mathematical Form establishes a precise formal and operating system (S ↦ M); the Necessity Form proves necessary consequence under conditions (C ⇒ R); the Possibility Form proves that the object can exist (∃X, C(X)); the Inversion Form proves what can be identified from results (y ⇒ H⁻¹(y)). Local tasks — boundary, classification, dissolution, source, transition, phase transition, compatibility, construction, reachability, realization, determination, distinction, indistinguishability, diagnosis, stability — are assigned to the fifteen auxiliary forms; Forms may be combined in use, and a Form so combined is called a combined form.

The most central principle of the whole specification: the manner of proof is determined by the proposition to be proven, not by the object of study. The object unchanged, the proof task changed — the method must change.

Why must there be Forms? A theory is not a pile of propositions. Once a proposition is put forward, it demands proof; proof demands form; form demands organization. Only when a proposition to be proven, a proof objective, a completion condition, and a breach method are organized into one and the same formal bearer does a theory first acquire a unit that can be reviewed, overturned, and sealed. That unit is the Form. Without Forms, propositions scatter into assertions, proofs scatter into fragments, results cannot be located, errors cannot be traced, and sealing is out of the question. The existence of Forms is not a stylistic preference; it is the minimal condition for a theory to be reviewable.

Every complete formalized theoretical task in the book stands as a Form, is breached as a Form, and is sealed as a Form; local results such as formulas, theorems, equations, and criteria stand and are cited through the form-text to which they belong.

This classification is empirically complete for the book's present theoretical tasks; whether it exhausts all directions of proof is to be tested by cross-disciplinary back-testing, not settled by conceptual assertion (see Part Two, Chapter Two, and Part Ten).

The ten Parts below set out what a Form is, how many kinds there are, when to use them, how to write them, and how to review them.

I. The Nature of This Text

This chapter is not a theory of forms, not a theory of specification, not an axiomatic system, not a general treatise.

This chapter answers five things only:  
(1) What a Form is;  
(2) How Forms are classified;  
(3) When each kind of Form is to be used;  
(4) How each kind of Form is to be written;  
(5) How review is to be conducted.

Any theory that grows out of this basis is to be established in a new chapter and is not written into this chapter.

II. Scope of Application

This chapter applies to the writing, revision, and review of every formal form-text in On Self-Manifestation, and to the admission judgment of form-texts added later.

III. The Source of the Specification

The specification in this chapter is not designed a priori; it is extracted empirically from the formalized chapters that On Self-Manifestation already contains. For the extraction corpus and the exclusion rulings, see Part Ten, Chapter One.

Part One: On Forms

Chapter One: The Definition of a Form

A Form is not a formula, not an equation, not a theorem, not a criterion, not an algorithm, not the title of a paper.

A Form is defined as: a formal organization that bears one complete theoretical task.

A formal Form must bear four things at once:  
(1) one independent proposition to be proven;  
(2) one independent proof objective;  
(3) one independent completion condition;  
(4) one independent breach method.

Lacking any one of the four, it is not a formal Form.

Chapter Two: Forms and Form-Components

A formula is only a form-component; a theorem is only a form-component; an equation is only a form-component; a criterion is only a form-component.

Form-components are the constituent parts of a Form and do not bear theoretical tasks on their own. A form-component is not a Form.

For the classification, identification, and usage rules of form-components, see Part Five, Chapter Three.

Chapter Three: The Seven Admission Requirements

For a problem to stand as an independent Form, it must satisfy all seven of the following:

(1) an independent theoretical problem;  
(2) an independent proof objective;  
(3) non-trivial resistance — if the conclusion is obvious and there is nowhere it could fail, it does not make a Form;  
(4) a complete derivation space — from premises to conclusion there is a derivation that can be unfolded;  
(5) output into the system — its conclusion can be cited by other Forms;  
(6) non-demotability — it cannot be demoted to a definition, lemma, theorem, equation, or appendix; whatever can be so demoted must be so demoted;  
(7) a reviewable logical standing — the conclusion can be re-examined and can be breached.

If even one of the seven is not satisfied, it cannot be established as a formal Form.

Chapter Four: Non-Forms

The following names and genres do not in themselves constitute formal Forms:

reading guides; concept explanations; historical surveys; commentary; thought experiments; experimental records; engineering logs; program documentation; algorithms; reference material; version notes; glossaries; translation notes; comparative essays.

If content within them bears a complete theoretical task (that is, passes the seven admission requirements of Chapter Three), it should be rewritten as a formal form-text; otherwise it is filed as a non-form genre: it takes no Form Chapter number, enters no group form, and is not bound by the review rules of Part Nine, though it remains bound by the book-wide rules for terminology and numbering.

Chapter Five: The System of Formal Form-Texts

The formal system has four layers:

Layer One: the form-text — a single formal text bearing the task of one form-kind (a principal-form text or an auxiliary-form text; see Part Six, Chapter Two). The one form-kind a form-text bears is called that text's form-body. The title of a form-text must state its form-body, in the shape "object + form-kind": the General Form chapter proving the minimal structure of Objectification is titled the Objectification General Form; the Transition Form chapter characterizing the Recognition-Realm transition is titled the Recognition-Realm Transition Form. Non-form documents (specifications, glossaries, reading guides, and the like) bear no form-body, and their titles are not bound by this rule;  
Layer Two: the group form — a set of form-texts around one object or one family of propositions;  
Layer Three: the integrative forms — the Paradigm, the General Outline, and the Form-Group Master Diagram, which organize Forms already established (see Part Five, Chapter Two);  
Layer Four: the whole book.

The Paradigm is not a principal form. The General Outline is not a principal form. Integrative forms cannot substitute for any local proof.

The theoretical derivation of the four-layer system: the three bearing layers (form-text, group form, whole book) in Canon Chapter 6, General Form of the Form-Text; the organizing stratum and the component layer in Canon Chapter 5, General Form of the Strata.

The non-main-text blocks of a form-text (the attachment declaration, the version notes) are placed at the tail of the chapter without exception, never at the head or within the body.

Part Two: The Classification Theory of Form-Kinds

Chapter One: The Basis of Classification: the Proposition to Be Proven

Forms are not classified by object.

There is no such form-kind as an "Observer Form," a "Reality Form," or a "Current Form." Observer, reality, and the current are objects, not form-kinds.

The form-kind is determined jointly by three things:

object + proposition to be proven + proof objective → form-kind

The same object can correspond to entirely different principal forms. For the same "Observer": asking "what must it minimally possess to stand" is a General Form; asking "how is it precisely represented and run" is a Mathematical Form; asking "what cannot fail to happen" is a Necessity Form; asking "can it stand at all" is a Possibility Form; asking "what at most can be known from the results" is an Inversion Form.

This is one of the most important principles of the whole text. The principle has been proven as a theorem by Canon Chapter 2, Necessity Form of the Proposition to Be Proven (the Determination Theorem). The kind-assignment of every form-text in the book, and Step Four of review (reviewing the form-kind), rest on it.

Chapter Two: The Five Principal Forms and Completeness

There are five principal forms, closed as they now stand; there is no sixth principal form:

First Principal Form: the General Form  
Answers: what must the object minimally possess in order to stand?  
Proves: minimal structure.  
Shape: O ⇒ N

Second Principal Form: the Mathematical Form  
Answers: how is an established structure precisely represented, run, coupled, and solved?  
Proves: a formal system.  
Shape: S ↦ M  
Special standing: the only formal principal form.

Third Principal Form: the Necessity Form  
Answers: under the given structure, dynamics, and conditions, what cannot fail to happen?  
Proves: necessary consequence.  
Shape: C ⇒ R

Fourth Principal Form: the Possibility Form  
Answers: can the object stand?  
Proves: existence.  
Shape: ∃X, C(X)

Fifth Principal Form: the Inversion Form  
Answers: from known results, what at most can be known?  
Proves: identifiability.  
Shape: y ⇒ H⁻¹(y)

Completeness: the five principal forms cover five proof directions — necessity of constitution (General), form (Mathematical), inevitability (Necessity), possibility (Possibility), knowability (Inversion). Each of the five directions is attested by established chapters of the book: the constitutive direction in the Form Chapters, the formal direction in the Field Chapters, the inevitability direction in the Social Chapters, the possibility direction in the Engineering Chapters, and the knowability direction in the Form Chapters and the judgment chapters.

The standing of this completeness: the five principal forms constitute an empirically complete basic classification of the book's present theoretical tasks — to date, no sixth basic problem has been found that cannot be absorbed by the five principal forms and their auxiliary forms while independently determining the proof direction of a form-text (for the assignment table of common proof tasks, see Part Six, Chapter One). Empirical completeness is not strict completeness: the five kinds do not lie on a single axis of classification (General, Necessity, and Possibility classify by logical direction of the proposition; Inversion by epistemic direction; Mathematical by mode of expression and operation); one proposition can span two directions (for example the biconditional characterization O ⇔ N, see Part Six, Chapter Two). The closed classification proof within the restricted domain has been given by Canon Chapter 3, Classification Form of Proof Directions — the five principal forms are strictly complete under the restriction of the seven admissions and single-task normalization, on conditions Canon-3-H1 through H3. Strict completeness beyond the restricted domain is confirmed step by step through the reverse review of Part Ten and the continuing admission testing of candidate form-kinds.

No sixth principal form is established. Adding any principal form requires first proving that there exists a sixth basic proof direction which the existing form-kinds cannot absorb.

Chapter Three: The Subdivision of the Principal Forms

The five principal forms divide into two classes:

Proof principal forms — the General Form, the Necessity Form, the Possibility Form, the Inversion Form. Their common trait: they prove theoretical propositions directly.

The formal principal form — the Mathematical Form. Its role: to establish precise formal systems. The Mathematical Form is a principal form as a form-kind; when a form-text takes the building of a closed formal system as its principal task, that text is classed as a Mathematical Form. When mathematical expression serves only to support a General, Necessity, Possibility, or Inversion Form, it is merely a mathematical form-component or formal support within that text and does not constitute a second principal form.

Chapter Four: The Fifteen Auxiliary Forms

There are fifteen auxiliary forms in five groups, closed as they now stand; addition or deletion must pass the candidate-testing procedure at the end of this chapter:

Group One, boundary-discernment auxiliary forms: the Limit Form, the Grading Form, the Dissolution Form.  
Group Two, source-and-transformation auxiliary forms: the Source Form, the Transition Form, the Phase-Transition Form.  
Group Three, establishment-proof auxiliary forms: the Compatibility Form, the Construction Form, the Reachability Form, the Realization Form.  
Group Four, inversion-discernment auxiliary forms: the Determination Form, the Distinction Form, the Indistinguishability Form, the Diagnosis Form.  
Group Five, persistence-and-stability auxiliary forms: the Stability Form.

Auxiliary forms are formal form-kinds: they must pass the seven admission requirements of Part One, Chapter Three, and each has its six-field specification (see Part Four).

The closure of the auxiliary layer is weaker than that of the principal layer: if the system is ever to gain a new form-kind, it will more likely appear at the auxiliary layer, not the principal layer. The theoretical ground is Canon Chapter 4, General Form of the Auxiliary Forms — within the working domain ActiveDep(T), the layer's existence is necessary; its membership and grouping are empirical. The current watch list of candidates comprises three task types: invariance (what remains unchanged under a group of transformations, perturbations, or changes of realization), independence (two structural items neither of which can be derived from the other), and robustness (a conclusion insensitive to changes of model, parameters, observation, or realization). Of the three, invariance is the priority candidate for testing; independence and robustness can still be absorbed by existing form-kinds and remain under general observation.

The Stability Form has passed the candidate-testing procedure of this chapter. It proves whether an already-established qualification, structure, or object can be preserved within a declared domain of permitted variation, whether it can return after exit, and at which quantifier level preservation occurs. The stability task cannot be naturally absorbed by the Necessity Form, because beyond conditional consequence it must also handle the domain of permitted variation, object identity, substitution, recovery timing, and trajectory quantifiers; nor by the Limit Form, because a boundary answers only where failure sets in, not preservation, substitution, or return; nor by the Reachability Form, because the existence of one recovery path is not stability, still less guaranteed stability over all lawful trajectories. Its independent derivation procedure, completion conditions, and breach method are given in Part Four, Chapter Fifteen. Cross-object back-testing has yielded repeated instances in the tasks of law, phase, field, and theoretical text.

Before any candidate is formally added as an auxiliary form, six things must be proven at once (this is testing at the level of form-kinds, a different level from the seven admission requirements of Part One, Chapter Three, which govern a single text's problem): it has an independent task to be proven; it cannot be naturally absorbed by existing form-kinds; it has an independent derivation procedure; it has an independent completion condition; it has an independent breach method; it is reusable across multiple objects. The testing method is back-testing: classify a large body of actual proofs from the book's corpus and from external formalized literature one by one, and see whether the task type repeatedly overflows the existing form-kinds; the matter is not settled by conceptual judgment. Until a candidate passes, candidate tasks are handled within the nearest existing form-kind or form-component.

Chapter Five: The Completion Strengths of Establishment Proof

Compatibility, construction, reachability, realization: these are not four kinds of object but four completion strengths of one and the same establishment proof.

The order of strength:

compatibility ≺ construction ≺ reachability ≺ realization

Compatibility: the premises can coexist without contradiction;  
Construction: an explicit object satisfying the conditions is given;  
Reachability: that object can be reached from a given initial state by permitted dynamics;  
Realization: that object can be brought about under real constraints of resources and materials.

In logical bearing, a higher strength must cover the requirements of the lower: realization must at once show that the object is compatible, constructible, and formable by a permitted process. Lower-strength proofs may be completed in independent form-texts or be fully contained in a higher-strength text; the lower may never be presumed established merely because the higher is claimed.

This is a scale of proof completion, not a scale of theoretical truth. Higher strength means only that the proof has been carried deeper, not that the theory is more correct.

Chapter Six: The Places of the Equivalence Form and the Integrative Forms

The Equivalence Form is the comparative meta-form; it is not a principal form and not an auxiliary form (specification in Part Five, Chapter One).

The Paradigm, the General Outline, and the Form-Group Master Diagram are integrative forms; they are not principal forms and not proof forms (specification in Part Five, Chapter Two).

Synthesis is not a form-kind. Synthesis is a problem; according to its proposition to be proven, it is assigned to the General Form, the Compatibility Form, the Construction Form, or the Necessity Form (see also Part Six, Chapter One).

The combined form (see Part Six, Chapter Two) and the rejected synthesis form-kind are two different things: synthesis names a supposed independent proof direction, which does not stand; combination names the composite structure that principal and auxiliary forms make in use — it is not a form-kind and adds no proof direction.

Part Three: The Specification of the Principal Forms

This Part has five chapters, each with the same six fields: Purpose; Entry Conditions; Standard Composition; Completion Conditions; Breach Method; Prohibited Writing.  
The six fields are identical across the five chapters; the number of sections inside the Standard Composition field is set by each principal form's proof task and is not forced to be uniform.  
Every Standard Composition is extracted empirically from the book's established General Form, Mathematical Form, and related chapters (for the corpus, see Part Ten, Chapter One).

Chapter One: The Specification of the General Form

Purpose: to prove the minimal structure by which an object stands. It answers "what must O minimally possess in order to stand." Shape: O ⇒ N.

Entry Conditions: the object O is already clearly delimited; "O stands" can be stated as a reviewable fact; for the task this text undertakes, the minimal structure has not yet received an established unconditional conclusion, or a previous version has given the general formula while explicitly declared unclosed points remain. An upgrade may not feign "no Form has yet given O's minimal structure" as its premise; an upgrade must take over from the previous version and state which specific gap this version closes, and may not smuggle in a different task under the name of upgrading.

Standard Composition (eleven sections; the following are logical duties):

General Rule — the eleven sections below prescribe the logical duties a General Form must discharge, not a directory of mandatory headings, still less a fixed order of narration. The head of the text must complete the positioning of "general proposition / task — objective — advance notice of the general formula to be established"; version positioning, abstract, working domain, inheritance boundary, and the antecedent discrimination may be written together before or after these, but no later than the point where they first bear derivation. After the recursion of the body has forced out the constitution and relations of N, they may first be collected into the formal general formula and the general theorem then stated and proven; or the collection may follow the completion of proof. A theorem may be stated first and its proof follow in the usual "theorem — proof" shape; before its proof closes, it may not conversely serve as an already-established premise for later text. If a theorem is announced, invoked, or separated from its proof before that proof, its awaiting-proof status must be shown. Once a local lemma or local theorem is proven within its scope, corollaries may follow it at once without waiting for the general theorem; but local conclusions may not leap their station to bear whole-text conclusions still awaiting later proof. "One sentence" is a standard compression slot: one to three short sentences compressing the general conclusion, placed directly after the advance notice of results or the abstract; it bears no load and may not be stronger than the general theorem.

Problem-Driven Prompt — every General Form must include in its opening positioning the following sentence:

“In this chapter, through continuous questioning, we shall discover where the proof must go and derive the General Form of ××.”

“××” is replaced by the name of the object of that chapter. This sentence fixes the problem-driven manner of writing a General Form: the path of proof may not be laid out in advance, and each next step may arise only from the point left unclosed by the preceding round. The sentence bears no structural preview and may not pre-list any component of N.

(1) General Proposition — first write O's irreducible task as a reviewable general proposition: what the object is; what standing fact must be explained or maintained; what the domain of action, the temporal domain, the pool/domain, the quantifiers, the non-degeneracy conditions, and the logical strength claimed by this Form are. Task-side conditions may only delimit the facts and working domain by which the problem enters; they may not directly contain N, paraphrases of structures in N awaiting proof, or conclusions obtainable only from N; the degenerate cases that cannot be asked and the questions this Form does not undertake must also be carved out.

(2) Version Positioning and Declaration of the Degree of Answer — a first version must reduce the general proposition to the initial question or entry constraint that has already become exposed and can enter proof. Before argument begins, it may not rewrite the components of the structure awaiting proof as parallel sub-problems, nor may it prescribe the full order of recursion in advance. Every later sub-problem may arise only from the point left unclosed by a preceding round. After the whole text is complete, the sub-problems actually generated and the standing of their answers are then recorded as they in fact occurred.

Any upgrade that changes the strength of answer, the condition group, the range of validity, the structural items, or the chain of proof must state which point of the previous version remained unclosed and what this version advances. An upgrade limited to terminology, citation, translation, arrangement, or correction must likewise record the boundary of correction and state "the degree of answer unchanged"; it may not fabricate an unclosed gap or an advance in maturity. Every sub-problem actually generated and the overall conclusion must be marked proven, conditional, awaiting verification, or unanswered. New terminology, added length, or added formulas may not pose as an advance in the degree of answer. A sub-problem may be named as a gap and borne by an appendix under a division of labor by gaps; but naming a gap is a reduction at the problem level and may not license the pre-listing of components of N or a predetermined outline of the proof.

(3) Working Domain, Inheritance Boundary, and Objective — in the head positioning, or at the latest before any item first bears derivation, list the structures, definitions, mappings, and theorems this text may already use, with their source Forms, versions, ranges of validity, and citation preconditions; state which obtained result this text takes over, which unclosed question it continues to answer upon it, and draw the division of labor with adjacent Forms. Distinguish the structures this text is to force out, the structures merely inherited, and the conditions still awaiting H-group verification; make explicit that this text does not redo tasks the upstream has completed, nor may downstream texts' conclusions be back-fed as this text's premises. Then state the objective: within the above working domain, what minimal structure N is to be obtained, closing which task. All of this may be unfolded after the head's advance notice of N, but the notice of the general formula may not be deferred until after the argument flow on the excuse that details are not yet fully listed.

(4) Advance Notice of the Conclusion — a General Form should announce, in its opening positioning, the N this text is to establish, together with the task N answers, so that the reader knows at a glance what the text will establish. N may be a structural tuple, predicate, relation, mapping, system of equations, conjunction of gates, boundary division, or a combination of these. The notice must preserve enough content to identify the task, range, and principal relations; it may not merely list several nouns. The notice may use a compressed expression. Where complete types, quantifiers, domains, relations, or conditions must be formally unfolded in the body or an appendix, the notice must be marked as an abbreviated result awaiting proof, and the exact location of the later formal expression must be given.

The advance notice is a device of reading organization, composed after the argument of the entire form-text has been completed and then placed at the beginning on the basis of the results actually obtained, so that the reader can see the content of the whole text at a glance. It does not belong to the proof process and does not imply that the path of proof was known before proof began. In its position within the finished text, N in the notice has only the standing of a result awaiting proof. It may not serve as a basis of later recursion, determine the order in which structural items are forced out, or substitute for the constraint recursion, proof, and general theorem that follow. Results may be previewed; proof may not be previewed.

(5) Abstract, Overview of Results, and Antecedent Discrimination — the abstract states what existing chapters have already given, from which unclosed point this text begins its questioning, what the text finally obtains, and the boundary among proven, conditional, and awaiting-verification results. Like the advance notice of the conclusion, the abstract and the overview of results are composed after the entire form-text is complete and then placed at the beginning, and must report what actually occurred. Before argument begins, they may not announce later questions, structural items, or a recursive order that continuous questioning has not yet generated.

Where a key term admits competing readings, antecedent discrimination must be made before the term enters load-bearing, fixing the object domain, meaning, and exclusions used by this text. An antecedent discrimination, definition, or counterexample-type exclusion that only fixes object or scope may not pose as a structural item of N. It may enter N only where it is itself forced out by a task gap and bears a necessary relation. For ease of reading, a definition may be given before the passage that forces it out, but it may only stipulate object, condition, and exclusion; its necessity must still be independently established in the argument flow, and a conclusion awaiting proof may not be hidden in the definition. Where an upstream General Form exists, the abstract begins with the inheritance sentence — "The General Form of X has already shown …" — and explicitly states the step this text actually advances.

(6) Constraint Recursion — the first round may begin only from the frozen proposition to be proven, the task-side non-degeneracy conditions, the declared Entry Conditions, correctly inherited content, and H conditions already declared and explicitly holding the standing of premises. The first question of the first round is fixed: with these current knowns and nothing else, is this text's irreducible task already closed? If it is closed, forcing must stop; only if it is not closed does the first round proceed to test, verdict, force, fix, and re-ask. The first round may not use any component of N not yet forced out by a gap, and may not generate the proof route in reverse from the opening notice of results, a pre-listed table of contents, section topics, a result tuple, or later-obtained structure.

Thereafter every round returns to the same irreducible task and proceeds as a continuous argument in the six beats "ask — test — verdict — force — fix — re-ask":

Ask: with the content obtained so far and nothing else, is the task already closed?

Test: if it is not closed, exhibit a concrete degeneration, counter-model, alternative path, explanatory possibility, boundary case, or break in derivation that preserves the task-side conditions and the content already obtained, yet does not contain the item awaiting forcing.

Verdict: state precisely which task constraint the case leaves unsatisfied, or which premise, relation, quantification, or interface still fails to stand.

Force: only after that failure has been identified may it be concluded that, in order to exclude it, a new structural difference, necessary relation, mapping, gate, boundary, division, dependency relation, local theorem, or boundary conclusion must be added.

Fix: state the type, role, scope, and relation to existing content of the new item.

Re-ask: add the new item to the current structure and test the same task again. If the task is closed, forcing must stop and the text must turn to the general formula, minimality, or theorem proof. No item may be added merely to enrich N.

Recursion is not the item-by-item supplementation of proof; it is continuously driven by the same question. Every round must take everything obtained in all preceding rounds as its current known content and again ask whether the same irreducible task is closed. A later round may arise only from a concrete point of non-closure left by an earlier round. It may not arise from a preset directory, a pre-listed structural item, a chapter topic, or a new issue introduced by the author.

If a preceding round forces out A, the next round must ask: "With A now in place, is the original task closed?" Only if a failure can still be constructed while A and all earlier obtained content remain in place may B be forced out. Review does not ask whether the structural item could possess an independent necessity proof elsewhere. It asks whether the item's occurrence in this text depends on what has already been obtained and whether it addresses the concrete non-closure left by the earlier recursion.

If a round can stand unchanged without using prior obtained content, without preserving the structures already established, and without addressing a concrete prior point of non-closure; or if it can be moved arbitrarily forward or backward without changing the force of the argument, then that round is a parallel supplementary proof rather than recursion and is disqualified.

The order of recursion is generated by the continuous narrowing of the problem, not by a table of contents. A necessity proof may use what earlier rounds have already forced out — the chain itself participates in load-bearing. A term or symbol may be declared early for readability, but its standing as a necessary component, necessary relation, or load-bearing item of N arises only after the corresponding gap has been exhibited and the item has been forced out. If either the Test or Verdict beat is missing, the argument is itemized and triggers the Prohibited Writing clause.

The six beats are logical beats, not prose labels. The body advances naturally through the continuous movement of the problem and may not be mechanically written as six labeled fields such as "Ask:" and "Test:". Problem-driven writing also does not mean adding the fixed question "Is it enough?" to every section. Review asks whether the later round must arise logically from the earlier round's non-closure, not whether a fixed interrogative sentence appears.

A heading may only mark a place already reached by the derivation. It may not prescribe what the next item to be proved will be; a section may not become the engine of argument. Review follows the problem-chain and the six beats, not headings or labels. The six beats are separately listed here because this is a specification; this layout is not a model of the prose of a form-text.

Where the unclosed point is not a constructible counter-model but a premise, function, physical specification, external realization fact, or interface proof not yet established by the text, the exact break in derivation must be stated. Every unproven item that bears the structure or conclusion of the text must be numbered into the H-group and may not pose as a new structure. If it is only a realization proposal, further formalization, or explanatory matter that does not enter load-bearing, it must be listed as a non-load-bearing subsequent matter with an express statement that it supports neither N nor the general theorem.

One gap normally forces one structural difference or one local load-bearing result. If an inseparable group of differences must be forced at once, its inseparability under the task must first be proved; a batch of pre-listed items may not be presented as one answer.

The whole text must form a connected recursive structure governed by the same irreducible task. Recursion may proceed linearly, or may explicitly fork from the same point of non-closure and later merge. The common premise of the fork, the question separately closed by each branch, and the condition of merger must all be stated; every branch must point back to the concrete gap it inherits. Proof order makes no assertion by itself about actual operation, temporal order, causal direction, or joint generation.

(7) The Minimal-Structure Proof and Load-Bearing Classification — after the recursion, mark for every component of the candidate N, every necessary relation, and every declared inseparable group: an item forced out by this text; an inherited item, from which Form, which version, which range; or a conditional item depending on which H condition. Items forced by this text must point back to the round of the gap that forced them; inherited items need not re-prove their upstream conclusions here, but must be shown to satisfy their citation conditions within this text's working domain, and may not bear the core task this text has not yet proven.

Then prove: what definite failure the removal, replacement, or weakening of any component, relation, or inseparable group leaves behind; why the roles within items or groups cannot be merged into one another; why the dependence is linear, forked, conjunctive, disjunctive, or of whatever kind it is, rather than conjured from the order of narration. If a group of differences has decidable meaning only in joint existence, the group is the unit of the removal test, with an account of why its internal roles are not redundant; meaningless single-item counter-examples may not be fabricated.

Minimal does not mean forbidding any additional structure to coexist with N; a realization may of course carry parts that bear no load. Minimal means: N lacks no independent structural difference forced by the task, and no independent difference answering no remaining task constraint is stuffed into N. Hence it must be proven that any proposed addition, if it opens no new task constraint, is only a repetition, combination, or realization of existing items, relations, or details, and does not belong to N. The General Form thereby gives functional/structural minimal necessity; it does not automatically prescribe a unique physical realization.

(8) The General Formula, the General Theorem, and the Formal Read-Back — after the recursion has forced out the constitution and relations of N, they may first be collected into the formal general formula, with the general theorem and its proof following at once in the usual "theorem — proof" shape; or the proof may be completed first and the collection made afterward. Before its proof closes, the formula or theorem may not be used in reverse as an established result; wherever it is announced, invoked, or separated from its proof beforehand, its awaiting-proof status must be marked. The head's notice, the formal general formula after recursion, and the general theorem must correspond item by item: no component, necessary relation, quantified condition, or boundary may appear for the first time in the formal formula or the general theorem; it must already have been forced out by this text, correctly inherited, or listed as an H condition.

The complete symbol system may be distributed across the body and locatable appendices, but the formal general formula must have a complete account of symbols and a read-back in words: every symbol, type, domain/codomain, quantifier, time or pool/domain, condition, relation, and exception must be shown at a checkable location. The compressed formula at the head cannot substitute for this formal read-back. A tuple is not a causal chain; the order of proof is not a temporal order; support relations, order relations, and functional relations may not be written interchangeably. The direction and strength of the general theorem must be exact: O ⇒ N, O ⇔ N, existence, uniqueness, conditional conclusions, or boundary conclusions — only what has been proven or explicitly conditionalized is written; the unconditional part and the H-conditional part are stated separately, and no explanatory conclusion stronger than the proof may be appended outside the general theorem.

(9) Gap Declaration and the Status of Answers — every precondition, function awaiting formalization, physical specification awaiting verification, realization fact, and interface proof that participates in this text's load-bearing while remaining unproven enters the H group, each item marked with its number, content, the text responsible for verification together with that text's form-body or non-form nature, its status, and the structural items or conclusions it supports. The verifying text of an H is determined by the task that verification itself bears: if the verification has its own independent proposition to be proven, proof objective, completion condition, and breach method, a corresponding form-text must be established and the proof completed under its form-kind; only where it is measurement, material-specification checking, realization records, or other verification bearing no independent theoretical task may it be completed as a non-form verification record. Proven mathematics, conditionalized theory, and facts still awaiting verification must be kept apart. Subsequent questions, realization ideas, or non-necessary formalizations that do not enter the load-bearing of N or the general theorem may not be falsely reported as argumentative premises of H; they are to be marked expressly as subsequent matters not affecting this text's conclusions. Only after an H is cleared may the conditional conclusions it supports be raised to unconditional. An H entry may declare its proven part and its awaiting part (half-entry split); load-bearing may depend on a disjunction over the H group (for example "one of H5/H6"), in which case the disjunctive formula must be written out, and the dependence closes when any one branch stands.

Restoration check of H propositions — a load-bearing proposition still awaiting proof does not acquire proven standing by being redefined as a predicate, qualification, label, state quantity, interface quantity, or other symbol. If the definition of a symbol contains a proposition H awaiting verification, or the symbol is equivalent to H or takes H as the precondition of its standing, then every downstream conclusion bearing load on that symbol still inherits H; in review, the symbol must be restored to the original proposition awaiting proof and checked. Renaming, encapsulation, qualification, labeling, and interfacing do not constitute proof.

Condition closure — if a current conclusion stands only under H, that is, H ⇒ C, then every abstract, corollary, compressed formula, interface output, group-form propagation, general diagram, downstream inheritance, and version citation that keeps the same theoretical strength must retain H or its proven equivalent precondition. Only when H has been closed by independent proof may the corresponding conclusion be raised to unconditional C. Deleting, weakening, or concealing H while keeping the statement of C is the offense of deleting a premise to raise strength.  
(10) Appendices, Corollaries, Realization Correspondence, and Breach Testing — the complete theorems, computations, symbolic expressions, counter-examples, and proofs required by the body's load-bearing may be placed in appendices, but the body must point to their load-bearing positions. Testable, operational, or refutable consequences obtained directly from a completed local lemma, local theorem, or the general theorem should be listed as corollaries immediately after the result they depend on, or point back to it precisely; local consequences that do not depend on the general theorem may not wait until the end of the text to be dressed up as general corollaries. Realization correspondences, models, or engineering landings may explain how N falls upon a concrete carrier, but must be marked as the realization/correspondence layer; a realization example may not stand in for the necessity proof of a General Form, nor may the realization supply in reverse a structural premise this text lacks. Phenomena, intuitions, and cases can serve only as illustration, testing, or attempted breach; they cannot replace universal proof. Every counter-example must state whether it is a genuine breach preserving the entry conditions, an expected degeneration outside the task, or a case still awaiting the H group's decision.

(11) Interfaces — list this text's inputs, outputs, upstream citations, downstream preconditions, and version relations; for every inherited item state the exact position at which it enters this text, and for every output state to what strength later Forms may use it. No circular load-bearing may form: the core preconditions this text requires cannot be supplied in reverse by downstream Forms whose premises include this text's conclusions.

Completion Conditions: all eleven logical duties are discharged without being mechanically written as a fixed table of contents. The opening has positioned the general proposition/task, the objective, and N as awaiting proof, and N has throughout remained only a result awaiting proof rather than a premise of derivation. Task-side conditions do not restate N or its awaited conclusions. The working domain, Entry Conditions, inherited items, and H conditions are distinguished; the inherited problem, division of labor with neighboring Forms, source Forms, versions, ranges of validity, and citation premises have all been checked. The launch of the first round is traceable to the frozen proposition to be proven, the task-side non-degeneracy conditions, the Entry Conditions, lawfully inherited items, and declared H, and no first round has been formed by back-flow from pre-listed structural items, a result tuple, section topics, or later-obtained structure.

Every forcing round completes "ask — test — verdict — force — fix — re-ask," and the whole text forms a connected recursive structure governed by the same irreducible task: each later round takes all content obtained in preceding rounds as known and addresses only a point of non-closure explicitly left by a stated earlier round; a fork points back to its common gap; no isolated proof section lacks a generative relation to the principal problem. The degeneration, counter-model, or break in derivation preserves all content already obtained and the applicable conditions, and states precisely where the same task remains unclosed. No heading has generated a structural item in advance.

Every component, relation, local load-bearing result, or inseparable group forced by this text can be traced to its gap; definitions do not replace necessity proof. Forks, mergers, dependency types, and their distinction from proof order are explicit. The formal general formula corresponds item by item to the opening notice, and neither the formal formula nor the general theorem contains a new load-bearing item that was not forced out, correctly inherited, or declared in H. The symbol system and verbal read-back are complete and locatable in the body or appendices.

Minimality proves that N lacks no necessary difference and contains no independent difference not forced by the task, without inflating functional/structural necessity into a unique realization. No formula or theorem has been used in reverse as an established result before its proof closed; every result announced, invoked, or separated from its proof beforehand is marked as awaiting proof. Every proposition awaiting verification that is carried by labeling, qualification, or interfacing has been restored and checked; every conditional conclusion keeps condition closure in abstracts, general formulas, interfaces, corollaries, and downstream citations. All load-bearing H entries, corollaries, realization correspondences, breach paths, and interfaces are locatable and checkable, with no load-bearing omission.

Breach Method: with the declared object domain, entry conditions, task-side non-degeneracy conditions, and the relevant H conditions all preserved, exhibit a system in which O still stands while N as a whole, some component of N, a necessary relation, an inseparable group, a gate, a boundary, a division, or a quantified condition does not stand, and the task nonetheless closes; or prove that two roles listed as unmergeable can in fact be merged, or that a relation or group listed as necessary can be removed or weakened with the task still closing. If the breach targets a conditional conclusion, the counter-example must satisfy the H conditions that conclusion depends on. What falls only within the degenerate domain this Form excludes, fails the entry conditions, or exceeds the range of validity is not a breach but a boundary case. Exhibiting another physical realization of the same function N is not a breach either; the object of breach is the functional/structural difference claimed necessary, not any realization's outward shape. A breach must state precisely which general proposition, component, relation, or group it strikes, and may not switch objects, tasks, or quantifiers.

Prohibited Writing: treating the eleven duties as a fixed list of headings, or substituting a parallel table of contents for an actual argument flow; writing task-side conditions as N, as a synonymous restatement of N, or as conclusions obtainable only from N; using the advance notice of the general formula, abstract, terminological definition, title explanation, enumeration of phenomena, or intuitive judgment as a premise of derivation; launching the first round directly from pre-listed components of N, headings of a table of contents, a result tuple, or later-obtained structure; treating the opening notice as a current known of the first round; declaring "therefore the next structure is needed" without asking whether current content closes the task, without constructing a degeneration or counter-model that preserves obtained content, or without stating the break in derivation; passing a definition, antecedent discrimination, or exclusion off as a proven component of N;

replacing the continuous "ask — test — verdict — force — fix — re-ask" argument with a pre-listed tuple, a parallel list of missing items, or the empty phrase "a gap remains"; assigning pre-listed structural items to separate sections and then performing the six beats independently inside each section; beginning a later round from the topic of its section instead of re-asking the same task with the results of prior rounds in place; writing a section that can stand unchanged without using prior obtained content, preserving prior structures, or addressing a concrete prior non-closure; arranging sections so that their order can be arbitrarily exchanged without changing the force of proof; placing the difference awaiting proof into the object by definition and then treating that definition as the result of derivation;

claiming that a separable batch of items has been forced out at once, or treating proof order as actual causation, temporal order, or functional composition; passing off an inherited item as one forced by this text; feeding a downstream conclusion back as a premise; or writing H conditions, functions, realization specifications, and facts awaiting verification as unconditional structural items; using a proposition awaiting proof as an ordinary proven premise after renaming it a qualification predicate, label, state quantity, or interface quantity; compressing H ⇒ C into unconditional C in an abstract, interface, general diagram, downstream citation, or version propagation; falsely reporting a non-load-bearing realization proposal as a premise of the general theorem, or feeding one realization example back as proof of a General Form; listing a tuple without giving, in the body or appendix, its quantifiers, domains, conditions, relations, and verbal read-back; allowing a new load-bearing item to appear for the first time only in the formal general formula or general theorem; mixing explanatory, illustrative, or empirical text into formal load-bearing without marking its standing; or adding outside the general theorem a strengthened statement that has not been proved.

Chapter Two: The Specification of the Mathematical Form

Purpose: to establish a precise formal system for an already established structure. Answers "how S is precisely represented, run, coupled, and solved." Shape: S ↦ M. The only formal principal form.

Entry Conditions: the structure S is already established by a proof principal form (or its establishing Form advances in parallel with this one, each declaring the other); the components of S already have stable terminology.

Standard Composition (eight sections, extracted from Field Chapter practice):  
(1) Purpose — which established structures this formal system serves, declaring which Form and which version the structure comes from;  
(2) Core Thesis — the central content the system is to express precisely;  
(3) Foundational Objects and the Definition Sequence — numbered definitions given one by one, each definition corresponding to one component of the established structure; the definition sequence is itself the correspondence table; where the definition sequence contains a relation (an order relation, a relation of dominance, of equivalence, of dependence, and the like), the properties that relation possesses — reflexivity, symmetry, antisymmetry, transitivity, and the like — must be declared item by item, each declared as holding or not holding, and none assumed by default;  
(4) Equations — dynamic equations or static relations;  
(5) Operation and Solution — how the system evolves over time or under operations, with a statement of solvability;  
(6) Coupling Relations — coupling terms with other established formal systems and their correspondences; a Mathematical Form group may be organized in layers: a master-system chapter establishes all foundational objects, and sub-equation chapters each develop their special equations;  
(7) Read-Back Rules — how formal results are read back into structural propositions; any formal quantity with no counterpart on the structural side must be declared a purely formal auxiliary quantity;  
(8) Interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the formal system is consistent; it runs; the correspondence table and the read-back rules are complete, with no undeclared dangling symbols; the properties of every relation in the definition sequence are declared item by item.

Breach Method: exhibit a contradiction inside the formal system; or exhibit a conflict at the read-back point between a formal result and an established structural proposition; or exhibit a load-bearing symbol missing from the correspondence table; or exhibit that the actual properties of some relation in the definition sequence do not match those declared.

Prohibited Writing: coining new concepts detached from the established structure — formalization may not build a second, free-standing system of its own; it may only restate established quantities precisely (the explicit practice of the Law Chapters); altering structural definitions for the convenience of solution; substituting formal elegance for completeness of read-back; assuming a relation to be a partial or total order by default without declaring its properties.

Chapter Three: The Specification of the Necessity Form

Purpose: to prove what cannot fail to happen under the given structure, dynamics, and conditions. Shape: C ⇒ R.

Entry Conditions: every item of the condition group C comes from an established Form or is explicitly declared as an assumption; the result R can be stated as a reviewable fact; the formal system on which the operator mapping rests is already established.

Standard Composition (eight sections, extracted from Social Chapter practice):  
(1) Purpose;  
(2) Core Proposition — the central proposition the text argues, stated in one passage;  
(3) Operator Mapping — the objects of the proposition mapped one by one onto the operators and quantities of the established formal system, the mapping table given in full;  
(4) Dynamic Equations or Dynamic Laws — the evolution rules of the system under the condition group C, each with its source noted;  
(5) The Theorem Group — necessity theorems named one by one and proven one by one: C standing, R cannot fail to happen;  
(6) Boundary Cases and Structural Breakdown Conditions — the conditions under which the text's conclusions fail, given explicitly; this is the built-in declaration of breach conditions; additional conditions on which the necessity depends are numbered into the H-group;  
(7) Summary Formula — the whole text's conclusion gathered into a general formula;  
(8) Interfaces.  
Appendix (where applicable): directions for observable proxy variables, joining the operationalization rules of Part Eight.

Completion Conditions: the theorem group is proven one by one; the operator-mapping table is complete; the condition group is explicit with every item sourced; boundary cases and structural breakdown conditions are given; no undeclared hidden condition exists.

Breach Method: construct a counterexample in which the condition group holds and R does not occur — that constitutes a breach.

Prohibited Writing: writing high probability as necessity; hiding conditions in mid-derivation; substituting teleological sentences ("in order that … therefore necessarily") for dynamical derivation.

Chapter Four: The Specification of the Possibility Form

Purpose: to prove whether the object can stand. Shape: ∃X, C(X).

Entry Conditions: the condition group C is already closed and checkable; whether the object can stand is still unsettled, and the problem is non-trivial (neither obviously establishable, nor already covered by an established impossibility result).

Standard Composition (five sections; the condition-group style extracted from Engineering Chapter practice):  
(1) Condition-Group Declaration — the conditions given one by one as numbered definitions, each independently checkable;  
(2) Candidate Construction or Existence Argument — a constructive proof gives an explicit X; a non-constructive proof declares its non-constructive character;  
(3) Existence Theorem — the general formula states "the condition group complete, the object can stand";  
(4) Strength Declaration — the completion strength this Form reaches (compatibility / construction / reachability / realization, see Part Two, Chapter Five), and where the gaps toward higher strengths belong;  
(5) Interfaces.  
Appendix (where applicable): the condition modules developed one by one.

Completion Conditions: the existence proof is complete; the completion strength is explicitly declared.

Breach Method: prove that under the condition group no satisfying object exists (refutation of existence); or exhibit that the given construction fails some item of the condition group.

Prohibited Writing: passing off "no contradiction" as "existence" (compatibility is not existence); leaving the completion strength undeclared; substituting engineering prospects for proof.

Chapter Five: The Specification of the Inversion Form

Purpose: to prove what at most can be known from known results. Shape: y ⇒ H⁻¹(y).

Entry Conditions: the forward mapping H (from structure to results) is already given by an established Form; the observation set y is clearly delimited.

Standard Composition (six sections):  
(1) Declaration of the Observation Set and the Forward Mapping;  
(2) Statement of the Inversion Problem — the bounds of the preimage sought from y;  
(3) Identifiability Analysis — what can be discerned and what cannot;  
(4) Upper and Lower Bounds — the most that can be known (upper bound) and what necessarily cannot be known (lower bound), each given;  
(5) Residue Declaration — the unknowable residue listed explicitly, never left tacit;  
(6) Interfaces.

Completion Conditions: both the upper and the lower bound are proven; the residue is explicit.

Breach Method: exhibit, under the same y, discernible information beyond the stated upper bound; or exhibit an indiscernibility counterexample inside the range claimed discernible.

Prohibited Writing: giving the knowable without the unknowable; writing statistical correlation as identifiability; omitting the source of the forward mapping.

Part Four: The Specification of the Auxiliary Forms

This Part has fifteen chapters in five groups. Each chapter has the same six fields: Purpose; Entry Conditions; Standard Composition; Completion Conditions; Breach Method; Prohibited Writing.  
Mixed use of alternative field names such as "trigger," "completion," or "writing" is not permitted; all fifteen chapters use exactly these six fields.  
The Entry Conditions of every chapter are stated uniformly as prerequisite states: clause by clause, state which structures, definitions, and mappings are already established, given, or closed, and then state the question to be answered.  
For the combination and standalone-text rules of auxiliary forms, see Part Six, Chapter Two: an auxiliary form may serve as an appendix of a principal-form text, or stand in a text of its own; a standalone auxiliary-form text's conclusions still depend on the standing of the principal form it attaches to.

── Group One: Boundary-Discernment Auxiliary Forms ──

Chapter One: The Limit Form

Purpose: after a principal form has given a structure, to discern the boundary of its standing — the properties, such as compression, truncation, and reuse, that are unavoidable at the limit.

Entry Conditions: the structure is already established by a principal form; the question to be answered is "what must this structure necessarily exhibit as resources, time, or input tend to the limit."

Standard Composition: basic setup (including the limit direction and the limit conditions); inevitability argument; core definitions; structural characterization (equivalence classes and the like); main conclusion (including the properties inside and outside the boundary and the degeneration results beyond it); uniqueness conclusion (where uniqueness is claimed); minimal expression (where applicable); interpretation (in its own section, marked as not part of the formal structure); final conclusion; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the limit direction, the limit conditions, the properties inside and outside the boundary, and the degeneration results beyond the boundary are all proven; if the Form claims that the limit structure, the minimal expression, or the boundary value is unique, the uniqueness proof must also be completed.

Breach Method: exhibit a system in which the limit conditions hold and the asserted property does not appear.

Prohibited Writing: passing off finite-case conclusions as limit conclusions; claiming uniqueness without proving it; mixing interpretation into the formal structure.

Chapter Two: The Grading Form

Purpose: to give an established structure its levels or its comparison relation, and to prove that relation holds. Grading falls into two types. The single-axis stratification type divides objects into levels by a single criterion, the levels being mutually exclusive and jointly complete. The multi-dimensional partial-order type gives, on a fixed multi-dimensional grading domain, the comparison relation among objects, admitting that some objects are mutually incomparable. One type must be declared; the two may not be mixed. Where an overall grade is claimed on a multi-dimensional grading domain, the legitimacy of the aggregation must be proven separately.

Entry Conditions: the structure is already established. For the single-axis stratification type, a single grading axis must exist, and the differences between levels must be structural differences, not differences of degree. For the multi-dimensional partial-order type, the grading domain must first be declared: the set of dimensions fixed, the criterion of each dimension explicit, the direction of order on each dimension explicit, and the value range of each dimension explicit (which may be discrete levels, a continuous quantity, or a degree of membership).

Standard Composition: declaration of the type; premises; fixing of the prior facts. For the single-axis stratification type, further: the grading criterion (declared single-axis); the definition of each level (level by level); proof of mutual exclusivity; proof of completeness; proof of the non-emptiness of each level; final expression; interfaces. For the multi-dimensional partial-order type, further: declaration of the grading domain (dimension set, criterion, direction of order, and value range of each dimension); an argument for the non-redundancy of each dimension (if one dimension is a function of the others, removing it leaves the order unchanged and it bears no load; the argument may proceed by exhibiting a counterexample pair — two objects agreeing on all other dimensions and differing on this one — or by an equivalent rigorous reasoning); proof of the non-degeneracy of each dimension (at least two of its values are attainable under the entry conditions); the definition of the comparison relation and proof of its properties (reflexivity and transitivity must be proven; where antisymmetry fails, it must be declared that what is established is a preorder, and the treatment of the equivalence classes given); the conditions of incomparability (an explicit criterion for when two objects are judged mutually incomparable; "not yet compared" may not pass for "incomparable"); the rule of projection (where a subset of dimensions is used for a local comparison, it must be declared a projection and may not be raised to an overall grade); where an overall grade is claimed, an aggregation theorem (the aggregation rule given, and proven to preserve all the load-bearing order information the present task requires); final expression; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the type is declared. For the single-axis stratification type: both mutual exclusivity and completeness are proven; the criterion is single-axis; every level is non-empty under the entry conditions. For the multi-dimensional partial-order type: the grading domain is closed, with the criterion, direction of order, and value range of every dimension explicit; the non-redundancy of each dimension is argued; the non-degeneracy of each dimension is proven; the properties of the comparison relation are proven, and where it is a preorder the treatment of equivalence classes is given; the conditions of incomparability are explicit; the rule of projection is explicit; where an overall grade is claimed, the aggregation theorem is proven.

Breach Method: exhibit a system that belongs to no level, or to two levels at once; or exhibit a level that under the entry conditions can have no member; or exhibit that the criterion is in fact multi-axis while the single-axis stratification type has been declared; or exhibit two objects declared mutually incomparable that can in fact be compared, or the converse; or exhibit a dimension that is a function of the others, whose removal leaves the order unchanged; or exhibit a dimension that under the entry conditions can take only one value; or exhibit that an aggregation claimed as derived is in fact stipulated; or exhibit a local projection passed off as an overall grade.

Prohibited Writing: declaring the single-axis stratification type while grading by mixed criteria; substituting examples for levels; levels without proofs of exclusivity and completeness; padding the count of levels with an empty class — using a level that under the entry conditions can have no member, so that the grading appears finer than it is; producing a total order on a multi-dimensional grading domain by undeclared weighting; writing a stipulated aggregation as a derived one; shrinking the dimension set or changing the criteria in passing so as to manufacture comparability; using multiple dimensions without declaring the direction of order on each; calling a grading what rests on an intransitive comparison relation — an intransitive relation of dominance or of mutual checking yields no levels and can only be treated as a relation structure, its formalization belonging to the Mathematical Form and its necessity to the Necessity Form or the General Form.

Interface with the Phase-Transition Form: the Phase-Transition Form proves as far as the change of phase identity; the evolution of degree thereafter, within one and the same phase, belongs to the single-axis stratification type of this Form where it admits a single-axis stratification, to the multi-dimensional partial-order type where multi-dimensional comparison is required, and to the Limit Form where the question is one of limiting properties. The first appearance of a property and its subsequent intensification are not the same thing: that the latter admits grading is no ground for demoting the former to grading as well.

Chapter Three: The Dissolution Form

Purpose: to prove that an apparent contradiction or a misplaced concept does not stand at the structural level, and to give the source of its arising.

Entry Conditions: the apparent contradiction or misplacement is already clearly stated; the concepts involved all have established definitions.

Standard Composition: statement of the misplacement; localization of the source (by what structural process the misplacement is pressed out); dissolution conditions; dissolution theorem; residue declaration (what the dissolution does not exhaust, listed explicitly); interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the dissolution theorem is proven; the source is localized; the residue is explicit.

Breach Method: reconstruct the contradiction where the dissolution conditions hold.

Prohibited Writing: passing off reworded phrasing as dissolution; dissolving without localizing the source; concealing the residue.

── Group Two: Source-and-Transformation Auxiliary Forms ──

Chapter Four: The Source Form

Purpose: to prove the generative source of a structure or label — by what process it is pressed out, rather than by what it is given.

Entry Conditions: the structure or label is already established; its source is still presumed given or external.

Standard Composition: declaration of the object; exclusion of the presumed source; characterization of the generative process; source theorem; declaration of where the source cannot be uniquely determined; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the source theorem is proven; where the source cannot be uniquely determined is listed explicitly.

Breach Method: with all declared source conditions holding, the target structure still fails to be generated; or prove that the generative chain depends on undeclared external conditions; or show by intervention that the designated process does not bear the generative role claimed. Only where the Form claims a necessary or unique source does the target structure's still arising in the absence of that process constitute a breach.

Prohibited Writing: substituting correlation for cause; reporting only one source where several resolutions exist.

Chapter Five: The Transition Form

Purpose: to characterize the discontinuous transformation of one and the same object between two established levels or two structural configurations, giving its necessary conditions and its types.

Entry Conditions: both end configurations of the object are already established by Forms; the difference between the ends cannot be explained by continuous parameter adjustment.

Standard Composition: setup; problem; characterization of the change at both ends (end by end); necessary conditions; declaration of non-sufficiency (where the necessary conditions do not suffice to guarantee the transition, this must be declared explicitly); definition of the transition; division into types; stability constraints; main theorem; final configuration; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the necessary conditions are proven; the division into types is given; the status of sufficiency is explicitly declared.

Breach Method: exhibit the transition occurring with a necessary condition absent; or exhibit that the alleged transition can in fact be reached continuously.

Prohibited Writing: writing quantitative change as transition; suppressing the non-sufficiency declaration; types without stability constraints. Demarcation from the Phase-Transition Form: a phase transition is judged by whether the phase identity changes; a transition is judged by whether the configuration changes discontinuously. Discontinuity is neither necessary for a phase transition — a continuous phase transition is still a phase transition — nor sufficient for one, since a transition may occur within a single phase. The two Forms may both apply to one object, but each proves its own: the Transition Form proves the necessary conditions and types of a change of configuration, the Phase-Transition Form proves a change of phase identity.

Chapter Six: The Phase-Transition Form

Purpose: to prove that, under a change of control conditions, the phase identity of one and the same object changes — that it passes from a phase defined by one set of organizing criteria and maintenance rules into a phase defined by another set which is not mutually reducible to the first. The change in the maintenance standing of the prior phase may be a loss of local stability, or an inversion of relative stability whereby the prior phase falls back into metastability. Stability here is not stipulated as stability within any one landscape: it means the capacity of the organizational structure on which the prior phase rests to maintain itself under its permitted perturbations; and an organizing criterion may be local, or relational, non-local, or topological. A phase transition may be continuous or discontinuous. This Form admits two kinds of claim: to prove only that the phase structure undergoes a qualitative change at some condition is a phase-structure claim; to prove in addition that a given system or trajectory has in fact left the prior phase and entered the posterior phase is a transition-event claim.

Entry Conditions: the dependence of structural properties on the control parameter or the control-condition set is already given by a Mathematical Form or an established Form; the organizing criteria of both the prior and the posterior phase are already explicit, and their determination depends neither on the critical conditions nor on whether the transition has already occurred; where such a criterion depends on observation, coarse-graining, classification, scale, or a formation protocol, the legitimate protocol family and its range of application must be declared.

Standard Composition: declaration of the kind of claim (phase-structure claim or transition-event claim); the phase-identity criterion (its values and manner of determination, an undecided value being admitted; its invariance within the legitimate protocol family must be proven); declaration of the prior and posterior phases; control parameter or control-condition set; organizing criterion (where an order parameter is used, its relation to the organizing criterion must be stated); derivation of the conditions under which the phase structure changes; independent reading of the reorganization (it must be proven by structural facts independent of the phase label); argument for the change in the maintenance standing of the prior phase (the type taken must be declared: loss of local stability, or inversion of relative stability whereby the prior phase falls back into metastability); exclusion of the same-phase explanation (holding the phase-identity criterion and the maintenance rules fixed, prove that no within-phase continuous path realizes the target change); the conditions of actual transition (required for a transition-event claim; to be treated separately from the conditions under which the phase structure changes, with their relation declared as coincident, separated, or hysteretic); argument for the formation and maintenance of the new phase (maintenance being relative to a declared perturbation domain, and a breachable criterion must be given by which the maintenance regime of the posterior phase is distinguished from that of the prior); declaration of continuity or discontinuity; hysteresis, residue, metastability, and irreversibility (where applicable); phase-transition theorem; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the phase-identity criterion holds, is non-circular, and is invariant within the legitimate protocol family; the conditions of change of the phase structure are proven; the change in the maintenance standing of the prior phase is argued and its type declared; the same-phase explanation is excluded; the reorganization is proven by an independent reading; the organizing criteria of both phases are fully characterized; the maintenance of the posterior phase is proven and a breachable criterion given; continuity or discontinuity is declared; the kind of claim is declared — and, for a transition-event claim, the conditions of actual transition are additionally proven and their relation to the conditions of change of the phase structure declared.

Breach Method: exhibit that the alleged prior and posterior phases in fact satisfy one and the same phase-identity criterion, or that the phase-identity criterion is circular, or that the distinction between the phases is produced solely by an arbitrary choice of protocol; or exhibit that the target change can be realized entirely by within-phase continuous evolution; or exhibit that the change of phase structure bears no causal relation to the asserted conditions — that crossing them leaves the phase structure unchanged, or that the change of phase structure is unrelated to them; or exhibit that the asserted new phase cannot form an independent maintenance regime; or (for a transition-event claim only) exhibit that the asserted system did not in fact leave the prior phase, or that its leaving occurred where the phase structure had not changed.

Prohibited Writing: speaking of phase transition without phases or an organizing criterion; mere change of state without reorganization; determining a phase transition solely by a jump of the order parameter; excluding continuous phase transitions from phase transition; using a change of the phase label to prove the reorganization; producing a phase identity by an arbitrary choice of observation, coarse-graining, classification, scale, or formation protocol; treating the conditions of change of the phase structure and the event of actual transition as one and the same condition; requiring that every phase transition presuppose the destabilization of the prior phase; conflating a first appearance with the subsequent within-phase evolution as one and the same transition.

── Group Three: Establishment-Proof Auxiliary Forms ──

The four Forms of this group are the four completion strengths of one and the same establishment proof (Part Two, Chapter Five): compatibility ≺ construction ≺ reachability ≺ realization. A higher strength must cover the logical requirements of the lower; lower-strength proofs may be completed in independent form-texts or be fully contained in a higher-strength text, and may never be presumed established without proof.

Chapter Seven: The Compatibility Form

Purpose: to prove that a group of established propositions or structures can coexist in one system without contradiction.

Entry Conditions: each premise is already established by a Form or explicitly declared as an assumption; between the premises there is a questioned or non-obvious problem of coexistence.

Standard Composition: the premise list (each item with its source); discernment of candidate conflict points; a coexistence model or coexistence argument; compatibility theorem; strength declaration (this Form stops at compatibility, with the gaps toward higher strengths explicitly assigned); interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the compatibility theorem is proven; the candidate conflict points are handled one by one.

Breach Method: derive a contradiction from the premise group.

Prohibited Writing: passing off "no contradiction found" as a compatibility proof; omitting premises from the list; passing off compatibility as existence.

Chapter Eight: The Construction Form

Purpose: to give an explicit construction of an object satisfying the condition group.

Entry Conditions: compatibility is already proven, or advances in parallel with this Form with mutual declaration; the condition group is closed; the question to be answered is "what does a satisfier look like."

Standard Composition: condition-group declaration; construction procedure (explicit step by step); item-by-item verification that the constructed object satisfies the conditions; construction theorem; strength declaration; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the construction is explicit; the item-by-item verification is complete.

Breach Method: exhibit that the constructed object fails some item of the condition group; or exhibit that some step of the construction procedure cannot be executed.

Prohibited Writing: passing off an existence proof as a construction (non-constructive proofs belong to the Possibility Form); missing items in the verification; a construction resting on undeclared premises.

Chapter Nine: The Reachability Form

Purpose: to prove that the constructed object can be reached from a given initial state by permitted operations or by the system's dynamics.

Entry Conditions: the construction is already given; the initial state and the set of permitted operations are clearly delimited.

Standard Composition: declaration of the initial state and the permitted operations; path construction or reachability argument; bounds on cost and step count (given where possible, declared where not); reachability theorem; strength declaration; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the reachability theorem is proven; the path or the reachability argument is complete.

Breach Method: prove that no permitted path arrives; or exhibit that the given path contains an unpermitted operation.

Prohibited Writing: substituting "reachable in principle" for a path argument; enlarging the permitted set without declaration; leaving the initial state undeclared.

Chapter Ten: The Realization Form

Purpose: to prove that the constructed object can be brought about under real constraints of resources, materials, and engineering.

Entry Conditions: reachability is already proven; the group of real constraints (resources, materials, precision, time) is explicitly given.

Standard Composition: declaration of the real constraint group; realization scheme; item-by-item verification against the constraints; realization theorem or realization record; gap declaration for the parts not yet brought about; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the item-by-item verification against the constraints passes; what is not yet brought about is explicitly declared.

Breach Method: exhibit that some real constraint cannot be satisfied.

Prohibited Writing: passing off simulation as realization without declaring it; missing items in the constraint verification; suppressing the parts not yet brought about.

── Group Four: Inversion-Discernment Auxiliary Forms ──

Chapter Eleven: The Determination Form

Purpose: to give a procedure or criterion for determining, from the observation set, whether the property P holds.

Entry Conditions: the definition of the property P is already closed; the observation set Y is already clearly delimited; the forward relation between P and Y is already given by an established Form.

Standard Composition: declaration of the property and the observation set; construction of the criterion; proof of the criterion's validity (both directions: judging true what is true, judging false what is false); declaration of the misjudgment bound (where both cannot be had, listed explicitly); determination theorem; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: validity in both directions is proven, or the misjudgment bound is explicitly given.

Breach Method: construct an instance in which P holds and the criterion judges no, or P does not hold and the criterion judges yes, exceeding the declared misjudgment bound.

Prohibited Writing: passing off one-directional validity as determination; a criterion resting on unobservable quantities; leaving the misjudgment bound undeclared.

Chapter Twelve: The Distinction Form

Purpose: to prove that an observation exists which separates two candidate structures or hypotheses.

Entry Conditions: the definitions of the two candidates are already separately closed; the forward mappings of both candidates are already given.

Standard Composition: declaration of the candidate pair; construction of the distinguishing observation; proof of distinguishing validity (under this observation the outputs of the two candidates necessarily differ); distinction theorem; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the distinguishing observation is explicit and its validity proven.

Breach Method: exhibit a case in which the two candidates' outputs coincide under this observation.

Prohibited Writing: passing off differences of statistical tendency as distinction; an observation that cannot be executed; candidate definitions not closed.

Chapter Thirteen: The Indistinguishability Form

Purpose: to prove that within the given observation set no observation exists which separates the two candidates.

Entry Conditions: the definitions of the two candidates and of the observation set are all closed; attempts at distinction are already on record, or the distinction problem is non-obvious.

Standard Composition: declaration of the candidate pair and the observation set; proof of the identity of outputs under arbitrary observation; indistinguishability theorem; the bound of observation-set extension (under what extension distinction becomes possible, given where possible); interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the proof covers all observations in the set, not merely some.

Breach Method: exhibit one distinguishing observation inside the declared observation set.

Prohibited Writing: verifying some observations and passing that off as a universal proof; a blurred boundary of the observation set; suppressing the bound of extension.

Chapter Fourteen: The Diagnosis Form

Purpose: from failure or anomalous results, to infer the level and location of the fault source.

Entry Conditions: the failure results are already on record; the system's level structure is already given by an established Form; the forward manifestations of faults at each level are already characterized.

Standard Composition: declaration of the failure; enumeration of candidate fault sources (by level); comparison of manifestations candidate by candidate; localization argument; declaration of the unlocalizable residue; diagnostic conclusion; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: the localization argument is complete; the residue is explicit.

Breach Method: exhibit that the same failure can be produced by a candidate source that was excluded.

Prohibited Writing: pronouncing without enumeration; comparison with levels missing; residue left tacit.

── Group Five: Persistence-and-Stability Auxiliary Forms ──

Chapter Fifteen: The Stability Form

Purpose: to prove whether an already-established qualification, structure, object, or solution can be preserved within a declared domain of permitted variation; whether, if it exits, it can return under lawful dynamics; and to distinguish the quantifier levels of preservation by the same bearer, preservation by some bearer, stability of a declared trajectory family, and guaranteed stability over all lawful trajectories. The Stability Form proves the preservation of an established qualification under variation, not the first existence, first reachability, or first occurrence of the object.

Entry Conditions: the qualification Q to be stabilized has a closed definition and already stands in the initial state; the identity level of the stabilized object has been declared — a fixed individual, a fixed structure, one solution, a solution set, a property, a phase identity, or another explicit object; the domain of permitted variation Δ has been declared in advance, and the rule for recomputing Q after variation is established; where dynamic recovery is involved, the lawful successor dynamics, time domain, and observation protocol have been given by a Mathematical Form or another established Form; where guaranteed stability over all lawful futures is claimed, the full set of lawful trajectories and its decidable range must be delimitable.

Standard Composition: the stabilized object and the original qualification; the domain of permitted variation; recomputation of the qualification after variation; the level of instantaneous preservation; substitution relations among objects, solutions, and internal witnesses; dynamic trajectories, observation window, and decidable domain; recovery; the stability boundary; the quantifier-level check; the stability theorem and strength declaration; interfaces.

Recovery must prove that actual failure has occurred and that return strictly follows failure; where a recovery deadline is declared, timing begins at the first actual failure within the declared observation protocol, and a later failure may not reset the deadline; where the failure set has only an infimum whose attainment has not been proven, the infimum may not be treated as an actual failure event; where stable recovery is claimed, the return must satisfy the declared condition of sustained verification. Mere reappearance of Q without prior actual failure does not constitute recovery.

Where the stability boundary is studied along an ordered perturbation parameter, the infimum/supremum must be distinguished from the point actually attained. b = inf A or b = sup A gives only the boundary and does not automatically give the truth value of A(b); before failure responsibility, state, or phase identity is read off the boundary, attainment of the boundary must be proven separately.

Wherever "sustained," "preserved," "global," "guaranteed," or "always" is used, the order of quantifiers and the level of objects must be checked item by item. ∃x∀t P(x,t) and ∀t∃x P(x,t) are not equivalent; recovery of a fixed object and recovery of a solution set are not equivalent; stability of a declared trajectory family and guaranteed stability over all lawful trajectories are not equivalent.

Completion Conditions: the original qualification and the identity of the stabilized object are closed; the domain of permitted variation and the recomputation rule are declared; the fixed-object level and the existence level are distinguished; substitution of internal witnesses and substitution of objects are not conflated; for dynamic tasks the lawful trajectories, observation protocol, and decidable domain are explicit; a recovery claim satisfies "actual failure — posterior return — deadline (if any) — sustained verification (if stable recovery is claimed)"; the quantifier level of every "sustained, global, guaranteed" conclusion has been checked; where the stability boundary uses inf/sup, it has been separated from the point actually attained; reading an event or responsible party off the boundary has been preceded by a proof of attainment; conditional results keep condition closure; the breach method is operable.

Breach Method: with the declared original qualification, domain of permitted variation, and condition group preserved, exhibiting any one of the following constitutes the corresponding breach: fixed-object stability is claimed while that object exits Q; existence-level stability is claimed while every candidate exits Q; recovery is claimed while return occurs before actual failure, without prior actual failure, beyond the reported deadline, or without satisfying the reported sustained condition; trajectory-family stability is claimed while the family contains a lawful, decidable destabilizing trajectory; guaranteed stability over all lawful trajectories is claimed while a lawful destabilizing trajectory exists, or a lawful undecidable trajectory exists but was removed from the quantifier domain in advance; failure at the boundary point is claimed while that inf/sup is not attained; persistence of the same object is claimed while in fact different objects bear it at different moments; changing the original Δ, identity level, range of validity, or other declared conditions and then calling it recovery of the original stability instance also constitutes a breach.

Prohibited Writing: saying stable without declaring the domain of permitted variation; writing high-probability invariance as stability; writing the existence of one recovery path as guaranteed stability; writing ∀t∃x as ∃x∀t; passing off the existence of a substitute after the original object fails as preservation or recovery of the original; declaring the qualification destabilized as soon as the original witness dies without checking same-level substitute witnesses; calling it recovery without actual failure having occurred; resetting the recovery deadline by a later failure; calling an instantaneous reappearance stable recovery; manufacturing a vacuous universal from the missing part of a trajectory that does not cover the observation window; removing lawful but undecidable trajectories from "all lawful trajectories" and then claiming guaranteed stability; treating inf/sup as the actual failure point; changing the domain of permitted variation, entry conditions, object identity level, working domain, or other load-bearing declarations and still calling it the same stability instance; substituting one local result from a limit boundary, a reachability path, or a necessary consequence for the complete proof of the Stability Form.

Part Five: The Comparative Meta-Form, the Integrative Forms, and Form-Components

Chapter One: The Equivalence Form (the Comparative Meta-Form)

Position: the Equivalence Form is the comparative meta-form; it is not a principal form and not an auxiliary form.

Purpose: to compare two Forms, two models, two realizations, or two versions, and to establish their levels of equivalence.

Entry Conditions: both objects of comparison are already established with explicit versions; the purpose of the comparison is explicit (choosing one, keeping both, or checking an upgrade).

The seven levels of equivalence:  
(1) symbolic equivalence — the notation systems are mutually translatable;  
(2) propositional equivalence — the proven propositions are mutually derivable;  
(3) behavioral equivalence — the same input yields the same observable behavior;  
(4) dynamical equivalence — the evolution rules are the same;  
(5) structural equivalence — the minimal structures are the same;  
(6) historical equivalence — the sedimentation histories are the same within the precision at issue;  
(7) self-manifestation equivalence — the standing under the self-manifestation judgment is the same.

No entailment relation is presupposed among the seven levels; if a text claims an entailment chain among levels, that must be proven separately.

Standard Composition: declaration of the objects of comparison; determination kind by kind (all seven kinds determined one by one, or a declaration of which kinds are determined and why only those); equivalence conclusion (the standing of each kind of equivalence listed); the points of non-equivalence listed explicitly; interfaces.

Completion Conditions: every kind determined is argued; every kind not determined is explicitly declared.

Breach Method: exhibit a counterexample at a kind of equivalence asserted to hold.

Prohibited Writing: saying "equivalent" at large without declaring the kinds; passing off one kind of equivalence as another; claiming an entailment chain among kinds without proof.

Chapter Two: The Integrative Forms

The integrative forms comprise: the Paradigm, the General Outline, and the Form-Group Master Diagram.

Purpose: to organize multiple Forms already established.

Entry Conditions: the Forms to be integrated are all separately established with explicit versions; the need for organization is non-trivial (the relations among the Forms have grown too complex to see through cross-references alone).

Restrictions: integrative forms cannot substitute for local proofs; they are not a new principal form; they are not proof forms. Every load-bearing conclusion in them must be annotated item by item with its source Form and version (the table of terminological correspondence and theorem sources is a required component).

Standard Composition (Paradigm): general proposition; core definitions; generative structure; summary of necessary conditions (each with its source Form noted); levels; level relations; definition of the paradigm; conclusion; table of terminological correspondence and theorem sources.

Completion Conditions: every integrated Form is established; the source table is complete.

Breach Method: to breach an integrative form is to breach one of its source Forms; an integrative form has no independently breachable proposition of its own — if it has one, that proposition should be established in a form-text of its own.

Prohibited Writing: smuggling unestablished new propositions into an integrative form; omitting the source table; using the integrative position to suppress the revision of local Forms.

Chapter Three: The Five Classes of Form-Components

Form-components divide into five classes:

(1) semantic components — definitions, terms, statements of propositions;  
(2) proof components — lemmas, theorems, proofs, counterexamples;  
(3) mathematical components — formulas, equations, symbol tables, parameter tables;  
(4) operational components — criterion procedures, algorithms, verification arrangements;  
(5) editorial components — numbering, tables of contents, cross-references, version marks.

Auxiliary forms are not listed among form-components. Auxiliary forms are formal form-kinds, bound by the specification of Part Four, and cannot be demoted to form-components for filing.

Identification of form-components: whatever cannot independently answer "what is this item's proposition to be proven, its proof objective, when is it complete, how is it breached" is a form-component and cannot be called a Form.

Usage rules for form-components: form-components bear local tasks inside a form-text only. Formal citation takes the form-text as its unit; where a form-component must be cited, the form-text it belongs to and the component's number must be given together.

Part Six: Selection, Combination, and Citation of Form-Kinds

Chapter One: The Selection Procedure

Selection proceeds from the proposition to be proven, not from the object. The input of selection is a normalized proposition to be proven; the procedure that yields propositions from object-level problems is Canon Chapter 8, Construction Form of Proposition-Establishment — establishment first, selection after:

asking "what must the object minimally possess to stand" → the General Form;  
asking "how is the established structure precisely represented, run, coupled, solved" → the Mathematical Form;  
asking "what cannot fail to happen under the structure, dynamics, and conditions" → the Necessity Form;  
asking "can the object stand" → the Possibility Form;  
asking "what at most can be known from known results" → the Inversion Form.

Local tasks then call auxiliary forms: boundary problems call Group One; source-and-transformation problems call Group Two; deepening of establishment calls Group Three (in the order of strengths); discernment problems call Group Four; problems of the preservation, failure, substitution, and return of an established qualification, structure, or object under permitted variation call Group Five, the persistence-and-stability group.

The assignment of common proof tasks:

sufficiency (N ⇒ O) — the Necessity Form;  
biconditional characterization (O ⇔ N) — a combined form of the General and Necessity Forms, with the Equivalence Form confirming the two directions (see Chapter Two);  
impossibility (¬∃X, C(X)) — the negative result of the possibility direction, handled by the breach method of the Possibility Form;  
uniqueness (∃!X, C(X)) — existence plus review by the Equivalence Form or the Indistinguishability Form (proving that any two satisfiers are equivalent);  
consistency — the Compatibility Form;  
constructiveness — the Construction Form;  
computability, solvability — the Mathematical Form;  
reachability, realizability — the Reachability Form, the Realization Form;  
causal source — the Source Form;  
stability — the Stability Form; where only some necessary consequence of a condition is proven without bearing the institution of preservation, it still goes to the Necessity Form; where only a limit boundary is sought without bearing preservation, substitution, or recovery, it still goes to the Limit Form;  
invariance — assigned by task to the Necessity, Limit, or Equivalence Form, and remains a candidate under testing (see Part Two, Chapter Four);  
decidability, discernibility, undecidability bounds — the Inversion Form and Group Four;  
minimality, irreducibility — the General Form;  
optimality — proving "none better exists" goes to the Limit or Necessity Form; producing the optimal object goes to the Possibility or Construction Form.

Synthesis is given no form-kind. Synthesis is a problem; by its proposition to be proven it is assigned to the General, Compatibility, Construction, or Necessity Form.

Chapter Two: The Combination of Forms and the Combined Form

A formal form-text can have only one governing form-kind and one principal task.

A principal-form text is determined throughout by one principal form: the text's proposition to be proven, proof objective, completion condition, and breach method are the four items of that principal form.

Where a proof requires several principal and auxiliary forms, Forms may be combined in use. A Form so combined is called a combined form. The combined form is not a new form-kind; it is the composite body that principal and auxiliary forms make in use.

Combination takes two shapes:

(1) The appendix shape — an auxiliary form serves as an appendix of a principal-form text, bearing a local proof task inside it (the book's practice: Appendices 1 and 2 of Form Chapter 1 each bear one local theorem task, standing in attachment to the whole text). An auxiliary-form appendix does not determine the whole of a principal-form text.

(2) The standalone shape — an auxiliary form stands in a text of its own, and that one auxiliary form determines the whole text's proof task, reviewed under that auxiliary form's specification in Part Four. But seen from the proposition argued, the standalone text's conclusions still depend on the standing of its principal form: at establishment the text must declare the principal form or established structure it attaches to; if the principal form is breached or withdrawn, the conclusions of the attached auxiliary-form text fail with it or fall pending. A standalone auxiliary-form text does not promote the auxiliary form to a principal form, and does not cover the five basic proof directions.

The place of the combined form in the four-layer system: the appendix shape is one form-text; the standalone shape constitutes a group form centered on the principal-form text.

The typical case of the combined form is the characterization task (O ⇔ N): the General Form gives O ⇒ N, the Necessity Form gives N ⇒ O, and the Equivalence Form confirms that the two directions compose a biconditional. Characterization is a combined form, a composite proof objective, a shape of the general theorem — not a sixth principal form.

Review of a combined form: the principal-form part is reviewed under Part Three, the auxiliary-form parts under Part Four; the combination itself is reviewed on two further points — whether the dependency declaration is explicit, and whether any auxiliary conclusion is used beyond the standing range of the principal form it attaches to.

Every form-text is bound by the rule of one text, one form — one text bears the task of one form-kind and may not bear a mixture.

Chapter Three: Cross-Form Citation and Version Citation

Citation takes the Form as its unit. Citation format: chapter name · version number (the title already contains the form-kind under the form-body rule; example: the Objectification General Form v2.0).

When a cited Form is upgraded, the citing text must check the standing of the cited conclusions in the new version; where a conclusion is withdrawn or made conditional, the citing text is revised accordingly, with the change entered in its version notes.

Gap citation: whoever cites another Form's specifications awaiting verification (the H-group) must cite them under their original numbers, may not renumber them, and may not restate them as proven.

Condition closure: whoever cites H ⇒ C must inherit H, or its proven equivalent precondition, together with C; C may not be inherited alone. Only after the responsible chapter has closed H, and after the version has been checked, may the citing text raise the citation to unconditional.

Chapter Four: The Specification of the Group Form

Purpose: to prescribe the aggregating organizational unit of Forms — the group form. A group form does not prove a new theory; it organizes several already-established Forms so that their basis of aggregation, membership boundary, relational structure, propagation paths, and integral reading obtain a unified norm. A group form opens by answering one question only: why must these Forms compose one group rather than stand independent; the answer given, it proceeds at once to the basis of aggregation, membership boundary, relational organization, and the group-diagram norms. A group form must not install introductory rubrics such as "abstract," "purpose," "research aims," "research content," "research significance," "work objectives," or "contributions" — these belong to expository prose, not to group-form norms. A group form serves to organize those inter-chapter relations which no single form-text can present and execute on its own. When two or more established form-texts compose a group form under the entry conditions below, the group form uniformly registers membership boundaries and the directions of relations, executes upgrade propagation and sealing roll-up, and compiles the degrees of answer of its member chapters. A group form is first of all an organization of Forms and replaces no member chapter's local proof. Load-bearing relations within the group — equivalence-to-be-proven, attachment, combination — must be borne by the respective form-texts; relations of display only are presented by the group diagram, and the group diagram generates no new breachable proposition. When there arises within the group an independent group-level theoretical task that no single member can bear, the group form may carry a Form through a group-outline form-text; that task can be borne only by the group-outline form-text and must not be proven by proxy through the group diagram.

Entry Conditions: there exist two or more established form-texts that cite, attach to, or combine with one another under a single basis of aggregation; the basis of aggregation can be stated and reviewed.

Standard Constitution (seven pieces):

(1) The bearing/view division — group forms divide into bearing group forms and view group forms. A bearing group form aggregates along the object axis, and every form-text belongs to exactly one bearing group form; upgrade propagation, sealing roll-up, and the execution of attachment run only through the bearing group form. Group forms on the chain axis and the purpose axis are view group forms without exception: they may overlap, they bear no load, and the first line of their group diagram must be marked "view group form (non-bearing)."

(2) Declaration of the basis of aggregation — the criterion is a single axis. Three axes are currently lawful: the object axis (the principal form and its auxiliary group under one object of study), the chain axis (the General Forms along a chain of inheritance), and the purpose axis (chapters aggregated by one methodological purpose). New axes enter by the candidate testing of Part Two, Chapter Four. Mixing two axes in one aggregation is prohibited.

(3) The member table — registered member by member: chapter name · version number + form-body. The member table is the reviewable basis of the group's boundary.

(4) The routing of relations — whatever relation within the group bears load (equivalence awaiting proof, attachment, combination) must be borne by the corresponding form-text (an Equivalence Form text, a Combined Form text, an attachment declaration); whatever serves display only goes to the group diagram. The group diagram produces no new breachable proposition.

(5) The group diagram — the group-level integrative document, written under the integrative-form specification of Part Five, Chapter Two, with the group-level minimum set added: the member table, the basis of aggregation, and the propagation table (the table of directions of citation and attachment within the group, so that at an upgrade the affected range is found at a glance), and the degree-of-answer roll-up (a roll-up view of the proof status of the group's propositions; it summarizes and bears no load). Mandatory threshold: the members number four, or the depth of attachment reaches two layers, or a Combined Form text exists within the group — any one triggers the requirement of a group diagram; below the threshold, cross-references suffice.

(6) The order of generation — the writing and generation of a group form proceed: the principal form first; auxiliary forms enter in the order of the five residue groups (the boundary group, the source-and-transformation group, the establishment-deepening group, the inversion-discernment group, the persistence-and-stability group; theoretical ground in Canon Chapter 4, General Form of the Auxiliary Forms); a Combined Form merges only after both directions are separately proven; the group diagram closes the sequence and is updated as members upgrade.

(7) The two kinds of group form and the group-charter form-text — group forms divide into organizational group forms and theoretical group forms. An organizational group form bears organization only, with no group-level proposition to be proven; it is the default kind. When a group bears a group-level independent theoretical task that no single member can bear (for example, the necessity of a split, or a group-level general formula and general theorem), and satisfies the four-item definition of a Form, the group is a theoretical group form: the group-level proposition is borne by a group-charter form-text — a member chapter of the group, kind-assigned by the decision tree of Canon Chapter 3 according to its proposition, complete in the four items, carrying its own H group, completion conditions, and breach method, independently versioned, its bearing membership being this very group. A theoretical group form must have a bearing group form as its body; a view group form bears no load, carries no group-charter form-text, and its group-level observations enter only the degree-of-answer roll-up. The group diagram remains an integrative form and gains no load-bearing standing from the group's being theoretical. Identity is determined by the proposition to be proven, not by the name "group."

Completion Conditions: the basis of aggregation is single-axis and explicit; the member table matches the actual members; load-bearing relations are all borne by form-texts; a group at threshold has its group diagram with the group-level minimum set complete; bearing membership is unique book-wide. A group form carries a version number: the version number tracks revisions of the group's own items (the basis of aggregation, the member table, the propagation table, the group diagram, group-level declarations); changes in member content are borne by member version numbers and the propagation table and do not move the group version. Group sealing conditions: all members sealed (the group-charter form-text included), the group's own items updated to the current member-version set, and the group's frozen items settled; group sealing is recorded in the group diagram together with the group version. H numbers remain with the individual chapters (group-level H with the group-charter form-text), and the group diagram holds only a roll-up view.

Breach Method: exhibit a group whose basis of aggregation fails or mixes two axes; or whose group diagram's members and relations do not match the facts; or in which a load-bearing relation is not borne by a form-text but proven by the group diagram in its stead; or a form-text with two bearing memberships. Any one breaches.

Prohibited Writing: a pile of Forms without a basis of aggregation calling itself a group form; the group diagram substituting for local proofs; a view group form bearing upgrade propagation or sealing roll-up; abbreviating "group form" to "group" (group form and Combined Form must be written in full and kept distinguishable); dragging the group version number by a member's upgrade (the group version tracks only revisions of the group's own items); bearing a group-level proposition in the group diagram (a group-level proposition can be borne only by the group-charter form-text); passing a member list off as a group diagram.

Part Seven: Proposition Maturity and Degree of Completion

Chapter One: The Maturity Ladder of Propositions

The maturity of any proposition to be proven runs, in order:

(1) posed — the proposition can be stated;  
(2) definitionally closed — every concept involved has an established definition;  
(3) provable in shape — the proposition is decomposed into separately attackable sub-problems;  
(4) conditionally proven — proven under an explicit condition group, the conditions entering the H-group;  
(5) theoretically complete — unconditionally proven, with no premise awaiting verification;  
(6) sealed — the editorial finalized state, which concerns publication only and adds no theoretical content.

A version upgrade of a form-text must correspond to an actual advance in maturity, declared in the Declaration of the Degree of Answer.

Chapter Two: Degree of Completion

For establishment-type propositions, the degree of completion is the completion strength of Part Two, Chapter Five: compatibility ≺ construction ≺ reachability ≺ realization.

For all other Forms, the degree of completion is measured by the Completion Conditions field of the respective specification; no further scale is established.

Chapter Three: The Gap-Declaration System

Every unproven premise must be set up as an explicit specification, numbered into the H-group.

Declaration keeps three separations: the mathematically proven separated from the physically unverified; what this Form has proven separated from what other Forms have yet to prove; the conditional separated from the unconditional.

Every H entry must be annotated with: number; content; assignment (which chapter is responsible for verification); status (awaiting verification / verified / withdrawn).

A form-text without gap declaration cannot be sealed. Gaps cleared to zero, or all made explicit, is a precondition of sealing.

An H entry does not leave its standing as a gap by being renamed, qualified, labeled, turned into a state, or turned into an interface. If the definition of a downstream symbol still depends on the original H, the status of the original H continues to propagate.

While H is not cleared, every same-strength restatement of H ⇒ C remains at the layer of conditional proof; only after H is closed may it be raised to unconditional C at the layer of theoretical completion.

Part Eight: Operationalization and Verification

Chapter One: Operationalizing the Completion Conditions

The completion conditions of every form-text must be verifiable by a reviewer in execution: theorems have proofs on file; declarations have numbers on file; interfaces can be checked against the master table. The standard of operability: a reviewer can complete the verification without the author's explanation. Whatever "completion" cannot be operationally verified counts as not complete.

Chapter Two: Operationalizing the Breach Method

The breach method of every form-text must give the minimal shape of the counterexample: what kind of system breaches a General Form; what kind of counterexample breaches a Necessity Form; what contradiction or read-back conflict, and where, breaches a Mathematical Form. A breach method that cannot be operationalized fails the seventh admission requirement and cannot make a Form.

Chapter Three: Managing Specifications Awaiting Verification

H-group numbers take the chapter name as prefix plus a serial number, so that every entry is locatable book-wide without duplication; the H numbers already present in the book's chapters receive their prefixes at numbering freeze, with the serial numbers unchanged. The chapter responsible for verification must be assigned at declaration; when the responsible chapter is upgraded, it must respond entry by entry to the status changes of the H entries in its charge. Verified entries are published by the responsible chapter as verification records (a non-form genre), and the original form-text raises its conditional conclusions to proven in its next version.

Chapter Four: Cross-Task Operational Checks

1\. Recovery-posteriority check

Wherever a form-text uses conclusions such as "recovery," "return," or "re-attainment," the following verification must be executable: locate the first actual failure event within the declared observation protocol; locate the return event; verify that the return strictly follows the failure; where there is a recovery deadline, verify that it is timed from the first actual failure and has not been reset by a later failure; where the failure boundary is given only by an infimum or another lower bound, verify separately whether that boundary is actually attained; where stable recovery is claimed, verify the sustained condition after return. If any item cannot be verified, the recovery conclusion is incomplete.

2\. Boundary-attainment check

Wherever a form-text goes on from an inf, sup, limit, or critical boundary to read off an event truth value, failure responsibility, state, phase identity, or any other point predicate, it must first verify whether the boundary is actually attained. With only a boundary value and no proof of attainment, only the boundary may be reported, not an event at the boundary point.

3\. Cross-level quantifier-preservation check

Wherever a form-text uses conclusions such as "sustained, preserved, global, guaranteed, always, all paths, all realizations," the objects and order of the quantifiers must be written in verifiable form. Review checks at least three levels — individual/solution set, fixed object/substitute object, single trajectory/trajectory family/all lawful trajectories — and no restatement, abstract, interface, or general diagram may change the order of quantifiers while keeping the original strength term.

Chapter Five: Formal Standing and the Obligations of Instantiation

1\. Separation of task layers

Formal standing, instantiation, external back-testing, and real-world realization are different theoretical tasks. A form-text that has reached the completion conditions of its own form-kind thereby obtains the qualification of standing at its currently declared strength; that qualification may be unconditional standing or conditional standing under H preconditions. Whether it reaches the "theoretical completion" of Part Seven is judged separately by whether H has been cleared. Later-stage instantiation, external back-testing, or real-world realization not yet completed does not automatically withdraw a proof qualification already completed.

2\. Prohibition of reverse negation

A form-text that has reached its own completion conditions may not be judged not to stand merely on the grounds of "no instance yet," "no numerical demonstration yet," "no external benchmark back-test yet," or "not yet engineered." If these later-stage tasks are not among the original Form's entry conditions or completion conditions, their incompleteness shows only that the corresponding later-stage responsibility is not yet closed and cannot be inverted into failure of the original Form.

3\. Obligation triggered by explanatory claims

When a form-text goes further and claims that its formal objects, variables, relations, or criteria correspond to real objects, can explain real systems, support empirical determination, produce executable solution, or hold a real-world explanatory advantage over external theories, the corresponding obligations of instantiation and external verification arise. That obligation declares, according to the actual claim, at least one or several of the following: operationalization mapping; assignment of parameters and states; a minimal reproducible instance; positive and negative examples; rejection of pseudo-examples; back-testing against an external benchmark counterexample set; distinguishing criteria against competing theories; range of applicability. Where these are incomplete, their present status must be declared, and formal standing may not be read directly as established real-world explanatory power.

4\. The exception of breach by back-testing

If instantiation or external back-testing produces an instance that satisfies the original Form's object domain, entry conditions, and relevant H preconditions yet violates the original theorem, necessary relation, completion conditions, or boundary conclusions, that result constitutes an actual breach of the original Form and is not "a later-stage task not yet completed." Where later-stage verification refutes a load-bearing premise of the original Form, withdraws its upstream dependence, or breaches its real-world mapping or interface claim, it likewise propagates back to the corresponding level. Later-stage verification can breach or withdraw the corresponding earlier-stage qualification, but cannot negate an earlier-stage theory merely by its own incompleteness.

5\. Declaration of the four states

A form-text bearing on external applicability shall distinguish and separately declare:  
(1) the standing state of this Form — to what degree this Form is completed under its own form-kind: unconditional standing, conditional standing, awaiting verification, or unclosed;  
(2) the credibility of upstream structures — whether the upstream structures on which this Form bears load stand within this Form's working domain, and to what degree their external credibility currently reaches;  
(3) the instantiation state — whether an executable, reproducible concrete instance has been obtained;  
(4) the external-connection state — whether real-object mapping, external benchmark back-testing, or empirical connection has been completed.

The four states may not substitute for one another. A downstream Form's dependence on established upstream structures does not by itself lower the downstream Form's standing within its task domain; but when the downstream Form claims real-world explanatory power, empirical efficacy, or external applicability, its external credibility is constrained by the external credibility of all load-bearing upstream structures. Where the upstream has not yet obtained the corresponding external verification, the downstream may not present internal formal standing as external endorsement of the whole dependence chain.

Let A be the standing of this Form, B the completion of instantiation, and C the completion of external connection; in general none of ¬B ⇒ ¬A, A ⇒ B, or A ⇒ C may be inferred. A later-stage result propagates back to the corresponding level only where it actually breaches the original Form's conclusions, refutes its load-bearing premises, withdraws its upstream dependence, or breaches its real-world mapping or interface claim; the mere incompleteness of a later-stage task constitutes no negation by propagation.

Part Nine: The Unified Review Specification

Review has twelve steps in fixed order:

Step One: review the task — whether the text's proposition to be proven, proof objective, completion condition, and breach method are all four present and each independent; where instantiation, external back-testing, or real-world realization is involved, whether these are layered apart from the original Form's task, whether the standing of this Form, the credibility of upstream structures, instantiation, and external connection are distinguished, and whether an earlier-stage theory is negated in reverse by an incomplete later-stage task or the formal standing of an earlier stage is passed off as closure of a later-stage responsibility;  
Step Two: review the premises — whether every premise is sourced or declared as an assumption;  
Step Three: review the layer — whether the text's place in the four-layer system of form-text / group form / integrative forms / whole book is correct;  
Step Four: review the form-kind — assign the kind by object + proposition to be proven + proof objective (the assignment may use the decision tree of Canon Chapter 3, Section IV, directly as its operating procedure), and check that the assignment is correct and that it is one text, one form;  
Step Five: review the logic — whether the derivation skips, circles, or hides premises; whether a proposition still awaiting proof has been disguised as an ordinary proven premise by qualification, labeling, turning into a state, or turning into an interface; whether conditional conclusions keep condition closure in abstracts, general formulas, interfaces, group diagrams, and downstream citations;  
Step Six: review the derivation — whether the derivation space is fully unfolded; for a General Form, whether the first round launches only from the frozen proposition to be proven, the task-side non-degeneracy conditions, the Entry Conditions, lawfully inherited items, and declared H; whether the whole text forms a connected recursive structure governed by the same irreducible task. Check round by round whether each later round takes everything already obtained as known, whether it arises only from a point of non-closure explicitly left earlier, and whether every fork points back to a common gap. If the first round launches from pre-listed structural items, a result tuple, or later-obtained structure, or if a section does not use what was previously obtained, can be reordered arbitrarily without damaging the argument, or allows a heading to determine a structural item before questioning has forced it out, the text is section-driven and does not pass;  
Step Seven: review closure — whether definitions are closed and the decomposition of the proposition sufficient;  
Step Eight: review formal load-bearing — whether formulas and symbols bear their loads consistently, and the correspondence table and read-back are complete;  
Step Nine: review the boundary — whether the range of validity is declared, and limits and grading are in place; wherever an event, state, responsibility, or predicate at a point is read off an inf, sup, limit, or critical boundary, whether attainment of the boundary has been proven; for a text in the Phase-Transition Form, three further checks: whether the same-phase explanation has been excluded, whether the kind of claim has been declared, and whether the type of change in the maintenance standing of the prior phase has been declared; for a text in the Grading Form, three further checks: whether the type has been declared, whether the non-emptiness of each level or the non-degeneracy of each dimension has been accounted for, and whether the aggregation theorem is proven where an overall grade is claimed; for a text in the Stability Form, four further checks: whether the domain of permitted variation is declared in advance, whether the fixed-object level and the existence level are kept apart, whether recovery is timed from the first actual failure with failure preceding and return following, and whether "sustained/global/guaranteed" keeps its original quantifier level;  
Step Ten: review the breach — whether the breach method is operable, and whether known breach attempts remain unanswered;  
Step Eleven: review the interfaces — whether inputs and outputs with other Forms match the master table of Part Six, Chapter Four; whether the H of conditional outputs propagates completely along the interfaces; where real-world explanation, empirical determination, executable solution, or a claim of external advantage is involved, whether the obligations of instantiation and external verification have been declared and kept apart from the state of theoretical standing;  
Step Twelve: review the naming — whether the title states the form-body (object + form-kind); whether the non-main-text blocks are placed at the tail; whether terms, numbering, and version marks follow the book-wide rules.

Only passage through all twelve steps, step by step, finalizes a text; failure at any step returns the text to that step for revision, with no step skipped.

Chapter Two: Reverse Review

Once this chapter is finalized, every form-text of the book is checked back:

(1) each text passes the twelve steps of Part Nine;  
(2) where a text does not conform, the text is revised, not the specification — after sealing the specification is frozen, and every conflict is resolved by revising the text;  
(3) a place where the specification itself proves inexecutable, discovered in reverse review, is the sole permitted ground for revising the specification before sealing, and the revision must be entered in the version notes.

Chapter Three: The Sealing Procedure

The dynamical necessity of the sealing institution is given by Canon Chapter 7, Necessity Form of Sealing: revision must be closable under control, or citation cannot be completed and results cannot sediment. The procedure has six steps:

(1) terminology freeze — the book-wide glossary finalized;  
(2) numbering freeze — Form Chapter numbers and H-group numbers finalized;  
(3) interface freeze — the master table of Part Six, Chapter Four finalized;  
(4) template freeze — the six-field templates of Parts Three and Four finalized;  
(5) version number — this chapter's version fixed;  
(6) sealing declaration — thereafter every change goes through the upgrade procedure; no unrecorded modification may be made on a sealed text.

Conclusion

The general principles:

A Form acts on the proposition to be proven, not on the object.

One text, one governing form-kind; auxiliary forms do not determine a principal-form text.

Whatever is unproven must be declared; whatever is declared must be numbered; every conclusion must be breachable; what cannot be breached is not a Form.

Integration does not replace local proof; citation takes the Form as its unit; after sealing, texts are revised, not the specification.

Appendix: Candidate-Test Record of the Stability Form

1\. Independent task to be proven — passed. What the Stability Form asks is not the object's first existence, necessary constitution, formal representation, necessary consequence, or inversion knowability, but whether an already-established qualification, structure, or object is preserved under permitted variation, whether it can return after exit, and at which quantifier level preservation occurs.

2\. Cannot be naturally absorbed by existing form-kinds — passed. The Necessity Form can express the outer implication of some stability propositions, but does not bear the domain of permitted variation, object identity, substitution, recovery timing, or trajectory quantifiers; the Limit Form bears only the boundary and degeneration beyond it, not preservation, substitution, or return; the Reachability Form proves the existence of a lawful path, which is not stability, still less guaranteed stability over all lawful trajectories.

3\. Independent derivation procedure — passed. The Stability Form has the independent procedure "original qualification — domain of permitted variation — recomputation of the qualification — level of preservation — substitution relations — dynamic trajectories — failure and recovery — stability boundary — quantifier level — stability theorem."

4\. Independent completion condition — passed. The Stability Form must independently close object identity, the variation domain, the recomputation rule, the level of preservation, recovery posteriority, timing from first failure, sustained verification, trajectory coverage, boundary attainment, and quantifier level, none of which can be replaced by the completion conditions of an existing auxiliary form.

5\. Independent breach method — passed. Substituting the domain of permitted variation, conflating fixed and substitute objects, exchanging ∀t∃x and ∃x∀t, pseudo-recovery without prior failure, resetting the deadline by a later failure, passing off instantaneous reappearance as stable recovery, passing off a single path as guaranteed stability, and passing off an unattained boundary as the actual failure point are all operable breaches peculiar to the stability task.

6\. Cross-object reuse — passed. Fixed-solution/solution-set/trajectory stability of laws, the maintenance of prior and posterior phases, perturbation–recovery of fields, and the cross-time stable persistence of theoretical texts all exhibit the same task structure of "an established qualification preserved or returning under permitted variation"; Lyapunov stability, asymptotic stability, and structural stability in external formal theory provide back-tests of the same type.

Ruling: all six items pass together. Stability is formally elevated to the fifteenth auxiliary form, assigned to the persistence-and-stability group; no sixth principal form arises.

Reason for the change. First, "account" was the wrong register. This chapter already distinguishes four strengths of requirement — proof (exclusivity, completeness, uniqueness), argument (unavoidability, coexistence, reachability, localization), declaration (object, condition group, strength, gap), and construction or characterization (criterion, discriminating observation, generative process). A text in the Mathematical Form that declared some relation a partial order while it was in fact intransitive could not be breached under the former clauses, unless that error happened to produce a contradiction inside the formal system. The four clauses make the assignment real.
