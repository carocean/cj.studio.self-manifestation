# Necessity Form of the Proposition to Be Proven

General Proposition

The manner of proof is determined by the proposition to be proven, not by the object of study.

Canon Chapter 1 established this sentence as a normative principle (Part Two, Chapter One); this chapter proves it as a theorem. The object's determination of the manner of proof is insufficient (Theorem One); the proposition's determination of the manner of proof is sufficient (Theorem Two); therefore the power of determination lies with the proposition (Theorem Three). This is the philosophical foundation on which the five principal forms stand, and the root from which the whole Canon system grows.

This chapter is a principal-form text of the Necessity Form: its direct output is a conclusion that cannot fail to hold under the given definitions and specifications.

I. Purpose

Research in the history of science is organized by object as a matter of course: one studies consciousness and writes a theory of consciousness; one studies gravity and writes a theory of gravity; one studies life and society and founds the life sciences and the social sciences. Disciplines divide by object, methods are inherited along disciplinary lines, and it is rare for anyone first to ask: what exactly am I proving?

This chapter proves that this "what exactly am I proving" is the variable that determines the institution of proof. The object does not determine it; the proposition does.

II. Core Proposition

For any well-formed basic theoretical task T = (D, P, G, O): the task's manner of proof — its principal-form assignment, its completion condition, its breach method — is a function of the proposition to be proven G together with the proof objective, and not a function of the object D.

Same object, propositions of different directions: the institutions of proof necessarily differ. Different objects, propositions of the same direction: the institutions of proof are necessarily the same.

III. Operator Mapping

The mapping of this chapter lands on the conceptual institution established by Canon Chapter 1; the mapping is at the conceptual level. The objects of the argument are mapped one by one as follows:

the object D — the domain of objects studied (Canon Chapter 1, Part Two, Chapter One);  
the proposition to be proven G and the proof objective — the two items that determine the form-kind (same chapter);  
the manner of proof — the latter two of the Form's four items: the completion condition and the breach method (Canon Chapter 1, Part One, Chapter One), together with the principal-form specifications that bear them (Part Three);  
normalization — single-task normalization (Canon Chapter 3, Section VIII, restriction (4));  
the direct theoretical output — the output a task must directly deliver on completion (Canon Chapter 3, Section III).

IV. Premises and Constraints

(1) The four items of a Form: the proposition to be proven, the proof objective, the completion condition, the breach method (Canon Chapter 1, Part One, Chapter One).

(2) Each of the five principal forms has a determinate completion condition and breach method, specified form by form in Canon Chapter 1, Part Three; these specifications constitute a mapping: principal form → (shape of completion condition, shape of breach method).

(3) The five completion conditions and breach methods are pairwise incompatible: breaching a General Form requires a system that lacks N while O still stands; breaching a Necessity Form requires a counterexample in which C holds and R does not occur; breaching a Mathematical Form requires an internal contradiction or a read-back conflict; breaching a Possibility Form requires a refutation of existence; breaching an Inversion Form requires discernibility beyond the bound or indiscernibility within it — the five counterexample shapes cannot be substituted for one another (Canon Chapter 1, Part Three; Canon Chapter 3, Theorem Two).

(4) The decision tree: a proposition's direct theoretical output falls uniquely into one of the five classes (Canon Chapter 3, Theorems One and Three, on conditions Canon-3-H1 through H3, cited under their original numbers by the gap-citation rule).

V. The Theorem Group

Theorem One: the Object-Insufficiency Theorem

The object does not determine the manner of proof.

Proof. Take any object D that can carry at least two classes of propositions. With the Observer as the example, construct five propositions: what must the Observer minimally possess (constitutive output); how is the Observer precisely represented and run (formal output); why does the Observer necessarily arise (consequence output); can the Observer be realized (witness output); how, from external behavior, is it determined whether a system has an Observer (identification output). The five propositions concern one and the same D; their direct outputs fall into the five classes (Premise 4), and the corresponding completion conditions and breach methods are pairwise incompatible (Premise 3). Hence under one and the same object there exist well-formed tasks whose manners of proof differ. If the object determined the manner of proof, one object could correspond to only one institution of proof — contradicting the construction. So the object's determination of the manner of proof is insufficient.

(A degenerate object — one that can carry only a single class of propositions under the seven admissions — is the boundary case of this theorem, declared as Canon-2-H1; see Section IX. For the objects that actually appear in the book — Objectification, the Observer, the Recognition-Realm, the Field, Law, the Social — all five classes of propositions are constructible; for the corpus see Canon Chapter 1, Part Ten.)

Theorem Two: the Proposition-Sufficiency Theorem

Under single-task normalization, the proposition to be proven, together with the proof objective, sufficiently determines the manner of proof.

Proof. Suppose two tasks have the same proposition to be proven and the same proof objective. The proposition's direct theoretical output is assigned uniquely by the decision tree (Premise 4), and the assignment yields the principal form uniquely; the principal form yields the shape of the completion condition and of the breach method uniquely through the specifications of Canon Chapter 1, Part Three (Premise 2). In the whole chain G → output type → principal form → (completion condition, breach method), the object D appears as the argument of no step. So tasks with the same proposition have the same manner of proof: the proposition determines sufficiently, and this determination is independent of the object.

Theorem Three: the Determination Theorem

The variable that determines the manner of proof is the proposition to be proven, not the object.

Proof. By Theorem Two the proposition is sufficient, and no step of the chain of determination G → output type → principal form → (completion condition, breach method) takes the object as its argument; by Theorem One the object is insufficient. What suffices determines; what does not suffice does not; and the object appears nowhere on the chain — the power of determination lies with the proposition to be proven.

Same object, propositions of different directions — the institutions necessarily differ (the construction of Theorem One proves it); different objects, propositions of the same direction — the institutions are necessarily the same (the chain of Theorem Two depends only on the output type). The General Form of the Observer and the General Form of Objectification share exactly the same shapes of completion condition and breach method; the book's corpus attests it directly (Canon Chapter 1, Part Ten, Chapter One).

VI. Boundary Cases and Structural Breakdown Conditions

The conclusions of this chapter fail in the following cases, given explicitly:

(1) Degenerate objects: an object that carries only a single class of propositions under the seven admissions — the "one object, several proofs" construction fails for it, and Theorem One holds for it vacuously rather than substantively (Canon-2-H1);  
(2) The strictness of the decision tree negated: if any of Canon-3-H1 through H3 is negated, unique assignment degrades and Theorem Two retreats from sufficiency to empirical sufficiency;  
(3) The specification mapping altered: if an upgrade of Canon Chapter 1, Part Three changes the correspondence of completion-condition or breach-method shapes, Premise 2 fails and this chapter falls pending with it;  
(4) The discovery of a well-formed form-text whose completion condition and breach method are in fact determined by the object rather than the proposition — a direct breach of Theorem Three.

Case (4) is the breach method of this chapter: construct or discover such a form-text, and this chapter is breached.

VII. Interpretation (not part of the formal structure)

What Theorem Three states is a methodological turn: from object-centered to task-centered.

Object-centered research first claims an object, then chooses its methods along disciplinary habit; task-centered research first asks "what exactly am I proving," selects the institution of proof by the assignment of the proposition, and then submits to review under the corresponding completion condition and breach method. Disciplinary boundaries, in this view, are boundaries of objects, not boundaries of proof: the five institutions of proof run through all disciplines.

The first sentence of Canon Chapter 1's Conclusion — a Form acts on the proposition to be proven, not on the object — was a norm before this chapter, and is a theorem after it.

VIII. Summary Formula

Under single-task normalization, for any well-formed basic task T = (D, P, G, O):

Manner-of-proof(T) = Φ(output-type(G))

The mapping Φ is given by Canon Chapter 1, Part Three; D is not an argument of Φ.
