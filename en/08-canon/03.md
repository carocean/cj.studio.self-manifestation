# Classification Form of Proof Directions

General Proposition

Every basic theoretical task that passes the seven admission requirements and can independently determine the principal task of a form-text can be divided, completely and without overlap, into five classes according to the theoretical output it must directly deliver; these five classes correspond exactly to the five principal forms: the General Form, the Mathematical Form, the Necessity Form, the Possibility Form, the Inversion Form.

What this chapter proves is not that "all provable propositions in human language absolutely fall into five classes," but strict completeness within a restricted domain; the domain, the classification axis, and the normalization conditions are given explicitly below, one by one. Canon Chapter 1, Part Two, Chapter Two has declared the five principal forms empirically complete; this chapter advances empirical completeness to strict completeness within the restricted domain.

I. Premises

(1) The definition of a Form: a formal organization bearing one complete theoretical task, with all four items present — an independent proposition to be proven, an independent proof objective, an independent completion condition, an independent breach method (Canon Chapter 1, Part One, Chapter One).

(2) The seven admission requirements (Canon Chapter 1, Part One, Chapter Three).

(3) The identification of form-components, non-forms, the comparative meta-form, and the integrative forms (Canon Chapter 1, Parts One and Five).

(4) One text, one form: a formal form-text can have only one governing form-kind and one principal task (Canon Chapter 1, Part Six, Chapter Two).

All four premises come from the sealed Canon Chapter 1 v1.0 and are not re-proven here.

II. Prior Facts Fixed

Two facts are fixed first and used directly in the proofs below:

Fact One: a basic theoretical task must change the current state of the theory; changing the state of the theory means adding an established conclusion that other Forms can cite (the fifth admission requirement). Activity that does not change the theory's state — explanation, commentary, recording, translation, arrangement — belongs to the non-forms under Canon Chapter 1, Part One, Chapter Four, and does not enter the objects of this classification.

Fact Two: a basic theoretical task must deliver an output. Its completion condition must, under Canon Chapter 1, Part Eight, Chapter One, be operationally verifiable, and what is verified is the delivered output; without an output there is no completion condition to speak of, and the task fails the seventh admission requirement.

III. The Objects of Classification

What this chapter classifies is not all linguistic propositions, nor all research activity, but:

the basic theoretical tasks that pass the seven admission requirements and can independently bear the principal task of a formal form-text.

Basic means non-attached: the task's proposition to be proven does not depend for its standing on attachment to another Form's established structure. Attached tasks — the tasks borne by auxiliary-form texts (Canon Chapter 1, Part Six, Chapter Two: a standalone auxiliary-form text's conclusions depend on the standing of the principal form it attaches to) — are excluded from 𝒯 by definition; their proof direction can still be read off by the decision tree of Section IV, but their form-kind is determined by the specification of the auxiliary form they belong to. This chapter is itself an attached task (a standalone Classification Form text) and does not lie in 𝒯: the system handles its own case consistently.

All such tasks form the set 𝒯. Every task T ∈ 𝒯 comprises at least four items:

T = (D, P, G, O)

where D is the domain of objects studied; P the prior premises; G the target to be proven; O the theoretical output that must be directly delivered on completion.

By Canon Chapter 1, Part Two, Chapter One: the form-kind is determined by the proposition to be proven and the proof objective, not by the object. This chapter brings that principle into classifiable shape: the form-kind is determined by the type of O, not by D.

IV. The Classification Criterion

The classification axis is fixed as a single axis:

what type of theoretical output must this task directly generate in order to complete its target?

Discipline, object, style of writing, mathematical tooling, and chapter titles are not permitted as classification axes; "directly" means that this output is itself what the completion condition verifies, not an intermediate toward some other output.

The internal structure of the single axis is given by the decision tree below. The decision tree is not an enumeration: every fork is a two-way or three-way division, the exhaustiveness of every fork is argued separately, and wherever exhaustiveness cannot be had unconditionally, its premise enters the H-group explicitly (Section X).

The decision tree:

First fork: is this output an expressive system, or an assertion?

A theoretical output either provides an expressive apparatus — symbols, state spaces, equations, rules of operation and solution — which does not itself assert a proposition but carries assertions; or it is an assertion. What provides the expressive apparatus is the formal output. The fork turns on "whether it asserts a proposition," not on "whether it can be breached": an expressive system can equally be breached, on the standard of consistency and read-back (Canon Chapter 1, Part Three, Chapter Two). (The exhaustiveness of this fork is H1; see Section X.)

Second fork (assertion side): is this assertion an existence assertion, or an implication assertion?

An assertion either asserts "an object satisfying the conditions exists" (∃), or asserts "if one class of cases holds, another holds" (⇒). The existence assertion is the witness output. A universal non-implicational assertion ("every X has property φ") is rewritten in implicational shape "if X, then φ" and joins the implication side; an isolated singular factual assertion does not constitute a theoretical task and fails the first and fourth admission requirements. (The exhaustiveness of this fork is H2.)

Third fork (implication side): does this implication run with the direction of generation, or against it?

The frame of reference is the book's chain of generation: conditions → structure → operation → results → observation. Inference with the direction of generation — from conditions, structure, and dynamics toward results — is forward implication, the consequence output; inference against the direction of generation — from a later link of the chain back toward an earlier one — is backward implication. Forward and backward are fixed by the chain of generation, not by the order of writing. For implications between same-level links (for example, structure A ⇒ structure B), whatever infers from a given antecedent toward what thereupon stands is read as forward without exception; backward is reserved for inference from "standing" or "results" back to "indispensable constitution" or "internal candidates".

Fourth fork (backward side): does the backward inference land on being, or on knowing?

Backward implication has only two readings: one lands on the object itself — from "the object stands" back to "what the object cannot lack within," and the output is constitution: the ontic reading, the constitutive output; the other lands on the knower's side — from "the observed results" back to "which internal candidates remain," and the output is the bound of identifiability: the epistemic reading, the identification output. The landing point of a backward inference is either the object or the knower; the ontic and epistemic readings exhaust backward implication. (This exhaustiveness is H3.)

V. The Definition of Each Class

By the decision tree, direct theoretical outputs fall into exactly five classes:

Class One: the constitutive output — the structure an object cannot lack in order to stand: O ⇒ N. Corresponds to the General Form.

Class Two: the formal output — the precise representation, state space, relations, evolution, and solution system of an established structure: S ↦ M. Corresponds to the Mathematical Form.

Class Three: the consequence output — what cannot fail to happen under the given premises, structure, and dynamics: C ⇒ R. Corresponds to the Necessity Form.

Class Four: the witness output — an object, model, path, or realization witness satisfying the conditions: ∃X, C(X). Corresponds to the Possibility Form.

Class Five: the identification output — the internal candidates that observed results can constrain, recover, or exclude: x ∈ H⁻¹(y). Corresponds to the Inversion Form.

VI. The Assignment Theorem

Theorem One: the Five-Direction Assignment Theorem

For any T ∈ 𝒯, its direct theoretical output belongs to one of the five classes.

Proof. By Facts One and Two, T delivers an output and the output changes the state of the theory. Send that output through the decision tree of Section IV: the first fork divides it into expressive system (formal output) or assertion; the second divides assertions into existence assertions (witness output) or implication assertions; the third divides implications into forward (consequence output) or backward; the fourth divides the backward into the ontic reading (constitutive output) or the epistemic reading (identification output). Every leaf of the tree corresponds to one and only one class of output; any activity that falls into no leaf belongs, by Fact One, to the non-forms, or, by the identifications of Canon Chapter 1, to form-components, the comparative meta-form, or the integrative forms — none of which lie in 𝒯. On the condition that the three fork exhaustivenesses H1, H2, H3 hold, the assignment is complete.

VII. The Proof of Mutual Exclusivity

Theorem Two: the Direct-Principal-Task Exclusivity Theorem

On the single axis of "direct theoretical output," one form-text cannot have two principal forms.

Proof. The five leaves of the decision tree arise from four incompatible forks: an expressive system is not an assertion; an existence assertion is not an implication assertion; forward is not backward; the ontic landing is not the epistemic landing. The five output classes are therefore pairwise incompatible — one output cannot belong to two classes.

Consider next several outputs within one form-text. Suppose one text delivers both Oᵢ and Oⱼ, i ≠ j. There are only two cases. First, one of the outputs is an intermediate toward completing the other — a General Form uses equations to force out the minimal structure, and the equations are mathematical form-components that do not make the text a Mathematical Form; a Necessity Form exhibits, within its derivation, a trajectory satisfying the conditions, and the trajectory is a derivational intermediary that does not make the text a Possibility Form; a Possibility Form verifies its construction with an observational criterion, and the criterion is an operational form-component that does not make the text an Inversion Form. An intermediate is not a "direct" output and does not enter the classification axis. Second, both outputs are non-demotable, each with its own independent proposition to be proven, completion condition, and breach method — then by the seven admission requirements they are two independent theoretical tasks, and by Canon Chapter 1, Part Six, Chapter Two they must be split into two form-texts or one group form.

Therefore a well-formed form-text has exactly one principal form at the level of the direct principal task. Exclusivity does not say that one text cannot contain several kinds of work; it says that there cannot be two principal tasks that simultaneously determine the text's standard of completion and of breach.

VIII. The Proof of Completeness

Theorem Three: the Completeness Theorem of Basic Proof Directions

Under the following four restrictions:

(1) the objects of classification are only the independent basic theoretical tasks that pass the seven admission requirements;  
(2) the classification axis is only the task's direct theoretical output;  
(3) form-components, auxiliary forms, the comparative meta-form, and the integrative forms do not pose as principal forms;  
(4) composite tasks must be split into group forms;

the five principal forms 𝓕 = {General, Mathematical, Necessity, Possibility, Inversion} constitute a complete classification of basic theoretical tasks:

∀T ∈ 𝒯, ∃! F ∈ 𝓕 such that F determines T's direct target to be proven, its completion condition, and its breach method.

Proof. Existence is given by Theorem One: every T's direct output falls into one of the five classes, each corresponding to a principal form. Uniqueness is given by Theorem Two: on the single axis the five classes are pairwise incompatible, and normalization condition (4) excludes double principal tasks. ∃! holds. On the condition that H1, H2, H3 hold, completeness is strict within the restricted domain.

IX. Dissolution of Candidate Tasks

Common tasks that appear to fall outside the five classes are assigned one by one (consistent with the assignment table of Canon Chapter 1, Part Six, Chapter One):

Sufficiency (N ⇒ O): forward implication; the Necessity Form.  
Biconditional characterization (O ⇔ N): the General Form proves O ⇒ N, the Necessity Form proves N ⇒ O, the Equivalence Form confirms the two directions; a group form and a combined form, not a sixth principal form.  
Impossibility (¬∃X, C(X)): the negative result of an existence assertion, still in the witness direction, handled by the breach method of the Possibility Form.  
Uniqueness (∃!X, C(X)): existence plus review by the Equivalence Form or the Indistinguishability Form; no new basic direction.  
Stability: taking an already-established qualification Q as its premise, it asks whether Q is preserved within a declared domain of permitted variation, whether it can return after exit, and at which quantifier level preservation occurs — its premise is an established result, so by the definition of Section III it is an attached task, does not enter this chapter's decision tree, and is assigned to the Stability Form (Canon Chapter 1 v2.2, Part Four, Chapter Fifteen; the persistence-and-stability group). Its outer implicational shape C\_perturb ⇒ R\_remain, if extracted on its own as a basic task, falls into the cell of the Necessity Form; this shows that stability produces no sixth basic direction, not that the stability task itself is borne by the Necessity Form.  
Invariance: T ∈ 𝒢 ⇒ I(Tx) = I(x) — necessary preservation under transformation conditions; forward implication, the Necessity Form; comparison of invariants across expressions calls the Equivalence Form. Invariance remains a candidate under watch in Canon Chapter 1; should it later pass candidate testing and become an auxiliary form, it is handled as stability is: the direction-cell is unchanged, and the bearer of the task is upgraded.  
Optimality: proving "none better exists" is the Limit or Necessity Form; constructing the optimal object is the Possibility or Construction Form.  
Causal explanation: from what a structure is generated belongs to the Source Form (auxiliary); where conditions necessarily generate the result, the Necessity Form governs with the Source Form auxiliary.  
Statistical and probabilistic laws: the direct output is "under the given conditions the distribution of results cannot be otherwise"; the distributional fact, as R under forward implication, is the Necessity Form; writing distribution-level necessity as single-case necessity is exactly what the Necessity Form’s Prohibited Writing forbids.  
Comparison: the comparative meta-form.  
Organization: the integrative forms.

Note that this section's treatment of stability and invariance separates two things: the placement of the basic proof direction, and the bearer of the task. The former is closed by this chapter's decision tree — the outer implicational shape of a preservation-type proposition can fall only into the cell of the Necessity Form, so no sixth basic direction exists; the latter is decided by the six-point candidate test of Canon Chapter 1 — the stability task, taking an established qualification as its premise, is an attached task and was elevated to the fifteenth auxiliary form in Canon Chapter 1 v2.2, while invariance remains on the watch list. The strict completeness of Theorem Three of this chapter concerns only the basic tasks within the restricted domain; the addition or removal of auxiliary-form members does not touch it.

X. Gap Declaration

The strict completeness of this chapter rests on three fork exhaustivenesses, all entered explicitly into the H-group:

Canon-3-H1: exhaustiveness of the expression/assertion fork — "a theoretical output is either an expressive system or an assertion" depends on the definitional closure of "theoretical output" in Canon Chapter 1; the discovery of an output shape that is neither expressive apparatus nor assertion yet passes the seven admissions negates H1. Status: awaiting verification; responsibility with this chapter's upgrades.

Canon-3-H2: exhaustiveness of the existence/implication fork — depends on the two reductions "universal non-implicational assertions can be rewritten implicationally; isolated singular assertions fail admission." Status: awaiting verification; responsibility with this chapter's upgrades.

Canon-3-H3: exhaustiveness of the ontic/epistemic readings of backward implication — depends on "a backward inference lands either on the object or on the knower." Status: awaiting verification; responsibility with this chapter's upgrades.

The mathematical side (given the decision tree, the five classes are exclusive and complete) is proven; the three exhaustivenesses are the physical side of this chapter, declared separately under Canon Chapter 1, Part Seven, Chapter Three. If any of H1, H2, H3 is negated, Theorem Three retreats from strict to empirical completeness, and the statement of Canon Chapter 1, Part Two, Chapter Two stands as it is.

XI. Breach Conditions

The completeness theorem of this chapter is breached by any one of the following discoveries:

(1) a basic theoretical task passing the seven admissions whose direct output belongs to none of constitution, form, consequence, witness, identification;  
(2) an unsplittable single task that can stand only under the completion conditions of two principal forms at once;  
(3) an existing principal form that can be entirely absorbed by the other four, retaining no independent proof direction;  
(4) that a second basic classification axis must be introduced to avoid systematic omission or overlap;  
(5) an alleged "auxiliary form" that in fact independently covers a new basic proof direction.

These five are also the verification checklist for cross-disciplinary back-testing: send the mature proofs of mathematics, physics, computer science, biology, and engineering through the decision tree one by one; the appearance of any one of the five is a breach.

XII. Final Expression

Under the restricted domain (the seven admissions, single-task normalization) and the classification axis (direct theoretical output):

𝒯 → {constitution, form, consequence, witness, identification} → {General, Mathematical, Necessity, Possibility, Inversion}

The division is exclusive, complete, and uniquely assigning (∃!); the conditions of strictness are H1, H2, H3.
