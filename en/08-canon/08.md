# Construction Form of Proposition-Establishment

General Proposition

There exists an explicit procedure of proposition-establishment: given any object-level problem as input, it outputs a set of normalized propositions to be proven; every output proposition is definitionally closed, single-task, and uniquely assigned to one of the five basic proof directions.

An object-level problem is one that names only the object of study and a preliminary intent, without yet stating a provable proposition (examples: studying AI consciousness; studying gravity; studying the Observer). Canon Chapter 2 has proven that what determines the manner of proof is the proposition, not the object; this chapter constructs the procedure that turns object-level problems into propositions — establishment first, selection after, the two steps composing the complete navigation of theory.

I. Condition-Group Declaration

The conditions the procedure must satisfy, given one by one:

C1: universal input — it accepts any object-level problem, requiring only the object's name and a preliminary intent;  
C2: normalized output — every output proposition is definitionally closed and single-task (Canon Chapter 3, Section VIII, restriction (4));  
C3: unique assignment — every output proposition falls uniquely, via the decision tree, into one of the five directions (Canon Chapter 3, Theorems One and Three);  
C4: coverage of directions — the procedure omits no direction in which the object can carry a proposition (relative completeness: where a direction admits no proposition, the empty yield is recorded, not feigned);  
C5: executability — the steps are finite, and a researcher can execute them without this chapter's author explaining (the operability standard of Canon Chapter 1, Part Eight, Chapter One).

II. The Construction Procedure

The procedure has five steps:

Step One: fix the object and the intent. Write down the object D and the preliminary intent in one sentence. The intent may be vague — the procedure's task is precisely to take the vagueness apart.

Step Two: the five-question unfolding. Put five standard questions to D, each yielding one candidate proposition:

Question One: what must D minimally possess in order to stand? (candidate in the constitutive direction)  
Question Two: how is D's established structure precisely represented, run, coupled, solved? (candidate in the formal direction)  
Question Three: under what structure, dynamics, and conditions can what result concerning D not fail to happen? (candidate in the consequence direction)  
Question Four: can a D satisfying what conditions stand, or be realized? (candidate in the witness direction)  
Question Five: from D's external results, what at most can be known of its interior? (candidate in the identification direction)

Step Three: closure check. Check each candidate for definitional closure: does every concept the proposition involves have an established definition? Where one is missing, establish the definition first (a semantic-component task) or mark the candidate as awaiting closure; nothing proceeds while unclosed.

Step Four: admission filtering. Pass each candidate through the seven admissions (Canon Chapter 1, Part One, Chapter Three). What fails is routed by its nature: what lacks an independent proposition goes to component-level problems; what does not change the state of the theory goes to non-form topics; what offers no resistance is set aside. Filtering is selection, not failure — directions with empty yield are recorded as they are.

Step Five: normalization and registration. Normalize each surviving proposition to a single task (composite propositions split into group form tasks); assign each through the decision tree and register its form-body; mark its present standing on the maturity ladder (Canon Chapter 1, Part Seven, Chapter One) — posed, definitionally closed, or already provable in shape. A registered proposition is frozen as the task's proposition to be proven: altering the proposition during proof is changing the task, which requires returning to this procedure and re-establishing; propositions may not drift in mid-proof. The procedure terminates and outputs the proposition set.

III. Item-by-Item Verification

Verify against the condition group item by item, with "studying AI consciousness" as the running example:

C1 verified. Input "studying AI consciousness": object D = the AI system, intent = to understand its consciousness. A well-formed input.

C2, C3 verified. The five-question unfolding yields five candidates:

Question One yields: what structure must an AI system minimally possess in order to have consciousness? — constitutive output, a General Form candidate;  
Question Two yields: how is that structure precisely represented and run? — formal output, a Mathematical Form candidate;  
Question Three yields: under what conditions does the phenomenon of consciousness necessarily arise? — consequence output, a Necessity Form candidate;  
Question Four yields: can an AI consciousness satisfying the condition group stand, and be realized? — witness output, a Possibility Form candidate (the realization strength belonging to the establishment group);  
Question Five yields: from external behavior and observation, what at most can be determined of the system? — identification output, an Inversion Form candidate.

Each of the five is single-task; the assignment is given by the decision tree and is unique (conditional on Canon-3-H1 through H3). One and the same "AI consciousness" is taken apart into five research tasks whose completion conditions and breach methods differ pairwise — the construction of Canon Chapter 2, Theorem One, reappearing here as an operation.

C4 verified. The five questions correspond one to one with the five directions (the five output classes of Canon Chapter 3, Section V are exactly the answer-types of the five questions), so no direction is omitted; where a direction yields nothing (the degenerate-object case, Canon-2-H1), the procedure records the empty yield as it is — coverage is relatively complete, never feigned.

C5 verified. The five steps are finite; the checking basis of each step (the decision tree, the seven admissions, the maturity ladder) consists of already fixed provisions, checkable clause by clause, requiring no presence of this chapter's author.

IV. The Construction Theorem

The procedure of proposition-establishment exists and satisfies the condition group C1 through C5.

Proof. The procedure is explicitly given (Section II); C1 through C5 are verified item by item (Section III); of these, C3 is conditional on Canon-3-H1 through H3, and the relative completeness of C4 takes the strict completeness of the five directions as premise. The construction stands.

Interpretation (not part of the formal structure)

The Entry-Gate Lemma explains why the Construction Form of Proposition-Establishment is not a formality placed before proof, but the way the form system opens. The most common failure of traditional research is not faulty derivation but proof begun too early — experimenting, modeling, and debating before the problem is fixed. The first step of the procedure is not proving but freezing the proposition to be proven. In this sense, a Form is not a method used after proof has begun; it is a method that presses the researcher into discovering the real proposition to be proven.

## Appendix 1: the Dissolution Procedure for Misaligned Debates

Each side of a debate runs the establishment procedure and states explicitly the proposition it is actually proving; then compare. If the propositions are the same, the debate is real and is tried under the form-body specification of that proposition. If the propositions differ, the debate is misaligned — one side proves an identification proposition at the level of behavior (Question Five), the other refutes a constitutive proposition at the level of being (Question One): neither strikes the other. Once the misalignment is explicit, it is handled by the Dissolution Form: localize the source of the misplacement (usually an object-level problem never taken apart), and the debate dissolves into two independent research tasks.

## Appendix 2: the Entry-Gate Lemma

Proposition: while the proposition to be proven is not established, the form system cannot start.

Proof. Of a Form's four items, the completion condition and the breach method are functions of the proposition to be proven together with the proof objective (the Proposition-Sufficiency Theorem of Canon Chapter 2: the chain of determination G → output type → principal form → completion condition and breach method). With no proposition established, the function has no argument, and the completion condition and breach method cannot be fixed; the four items are incomplete, and the first admission (an independent theoretical problem) and the seventh (a breachable conclusion) fail — by Canon Chapter 1, Part One, no Form can stand. So for input without a proposition to be proven, the gate of the form system does not open: this is not a stipulation of the procedure but a corollary of the four-item definition. The traditional sequence "notice a phenomenon → study the object → start analyzing" can bypass establishment precisely because its standard of completion is not tied to a proposition; the form system's standard of completion is tied to the proposition, and cannot be bypassed.
