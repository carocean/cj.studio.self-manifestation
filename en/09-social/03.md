# Institutional Lock-in

This chapter argues a central proposition:

To acknowledge that a social field has been established is necessarily to acknowledge that deeply sedimented Familiar Route structures exist within it; the expression of these Familiar Routes at the institutional level is institutional lock-in — institutions are not merely objects passively absorbed by inertia, but actively suppress the generation of alternative structures through the deepening of their own Familiar Routes, forming an independent self-reinforcing mechanism.

We seek to identify the minimal dynamic conditions for institutional lock-in and the structural reasons for the failure of institutional reform.

We first state the result.

The core conditions for institutional lock-in:

Lock(Inst, F\_S) = 1

if and only if:  
Familiarity(Inst, F\_S) ≥ θ\_lock (institutional Familiar Route depth exceeds the lock-in threshold)  
∧ FlowShare(Inst, F\_S) ≥ θ\_flow (institutional flow-share exceeds the minimum lock-in flow threshold)  
∧ dFamiliarity(Inst)/dt ≥ 0 (institutional Familiar Route is not in decay)

Where θ\_lock is the institutional lock-in threshold and θ\_flow is the minimum lock-in flow threshold — an institution with high Familiarity but very low FlowShare is a marginalized institution that does not constitute genuine lock-in, because it cannot suppress alternative structures from acquiring continuation flow. Note: Shock \< T\_replace(F\_S) is a precondition for reform failure under lock-in, not a criterion for whether an institution is locked in — institutional lock-in is a structural property of the institution itself, independent of whether external disturbance is currently occurring.

In a single sentence

Institutional lock-in is not the result of interest-group protection,  
but a dynamic inevitability by which high-Familiarity institutions actively suppress the generation of alternative structures within the social field;  
reform that does not change Familiar Routes does not change institutions.

## Section 1. Purpose

Social Chapter 2 demonstrated social inertia: high K\_{F\_S} causes new inputs to be absorbed into old Co-Nuclei. But social inertia is a field-level global mechanism — it explains why change is difficult, but does not explain why certain specific structures — institutions — are harder to change than others, nor why after reform institutions often do not merely “return to their original state” but become harder to change than before the reform.

Institutional lock-in is a more precise mechanism than social inertia. It is not passive absorption but active suppression — high-Familiarity institutional structures actively lower the probability of generating alternative structures, and through self-reinforcement deepen their Familiar Routes further after being disturbed.

This chapter provides the precise dynamic mechanism of institutional lock-in, explaining why institutional reform failure is not contingent but structurally inevitable; why some institutions become stronger after reform attempts; and why only disturbances exceeding a specific threshold can genuinely change institutions.

## Section 2. Core Proposition

Political science and institutional economics typically attribute institutional lock-in to: historical path dependence (North), protection by vested interests, and institutional complementarity (mutual dependence among institutions). These explanations point to the correct phenomena but offer no dynamic equations: why is path dependence so stable? Why does interest protection continue to work? Why does complementarity make the whole harder to change than its parts?

The position of On Self-Manifestation is:

Institutional lock-in is a dynamic inevitability once institutional Familiar Routes exceed the lock-in threshold in a social field — institutions are not protected by external forces but actively suppress the generation of alternative structures through the self-reinforcement of their Familiar Routes.

This definition carries three key implications:

First, institutional lock-in does not require interest groups to exist. Even when no one actively maintains an institution, a high-Familiarity institution will still self-sustain through field dynamics — because Structural Bias causes the resources, attention, and action capacity within the field to automatically converge toward existing institutional Familiar Routes.

Second, the real obstacle to institutional reform is not resistance but the cost of generating alternative structures. In a field with high Familiarity(Inst), generating an alternative structure requires overcoming not only the existence of the current institution, but the fact that the institution’s Familiar Routes have already occupied a large portion of the social field’s Residual Capacity Ratio (φ\_{F\_S}), making it extremely difficult for alternative structures to form stable independent differentials.

Third, institutional lock-in is asymmetric: the harder an institution is to change, the stronger its rebound when disturbed. This is because a high-Familiarity institution, upon receiving a Shock, will experience a temporary decline in dFamiliarity/dt, but as long as Shock \< T\_replace, the self-reinforcing loop of the Familiar Routes will cause it to deepen further after the disturbance dissipates, often exceeding its pre-disturbance level.

## Section 3. Operator Mapping

This chapter introduces the following new definitions on the basis of Social Chapters 1 and 2 operators:

Institution: denoted Inst, representing concrete rules, procedures, organizational structures, or behavioral norms that have formed stable Familiar Routes within the social field. An institution is the name given to social field Familiar Routes as recognized within Information-Transformation — it is not an independent object, but the structured expression of high-Familiarity paths within the field.

Institutional Familiar Route depth: Familiarity(Inst, F\_S, t), corresponding to Familiarity(q\_i) in Field Chapter 11, representing the continuation priority of institution Inst within social field F\_S.

Familiarity(Inst, F\_S, t) = Φ(δ\_Inst, Reentry\_Inst, Z\_Inst, FlowShare\_Inst)

Where: δ\_Inst is the sedimentation depth of the institutional path; Reentry\_Inst is the re-entry frequency of the institutional path; Z\_Inst is the continuation resistance of the institutional path; FlowShare\_Inst is the flow-share proportion of the institutional path within the social field.

Institutional lock-in operator: Lock(Inst, F\_S), indicating whether institution Inst has reached a lock-in state within social field F\_S.

Lock(Inst, F\_S) = 1

if and only if: Familiarity(Inst, F\_S) ≥ θ\_lock ∧ FlowShare(Inst, F\_S) ≥ θ\_flow ∧ dFamiliarity(Inst)/dt ≥ 0

Where θ\_lock is the institutional lock-in threshold and θ\_flow is the minimum lock-in flow threshold — a marginalized institution with high Familiarity but very low FlowShare does not constitute genuine lock-in. Note: Shock \< T\_replace(F\_S) is a precondition for reform failure under lock-in, not a criterion for whether an institution is locked in.

Institutional replacement cost: RepCost(Inst, F\_S), representing the minimum disturbance intensity required to generate a new institution capable of replacing the functions of Inst within the current social field.

RepCost(Inst, F\_S) ∝ Familiarity(Inst, F\_S) · CoreDepth(Core\_S) / φ\_{F\_S}

Note: when φ\_{F\_S} → 0, RepCost tends toward its maximum value and the institution enters a near-irreplaceable state — when the social field is completely solidified, ordinary reform input cannot generate stable alternative differentials. This limit corresponds to the pre-compressive-death state of Field Chapter 18.

Institutional flow-share: FlowShare(Inst, F\_S), representing the proportion of continuation flow within the social field directed toward the institutional path, with 0 ≤ FlowShare ≤ 1. The higher FlowShare, the less continuation flow available to alternative structures and the higher the difficulty of generating them.

Institutional lock-in intensity: LockIntensity(Inst, F\_S), representing the degree to which institutional lock-in suppresses the generation of alternative structures.

LockIntensity(Inst, F\_S) ∝ Familiarity(Inst, F\_S) · FlowShare(Inst, F\_S)

## Section 4. Dynamic Equations

Institutional Familiar Route deepening equation (citing Field Chapter 11):

dFamiliarity(Inst)/dt ∝ Reentry\_Inst · (1 − Z\_Inst) · FlowShare\_Inst

That is, the more frequently the institutional path is re-entered, the lower its resistance, and the higher its flow-share, the faster the Familiar Route deepens.

Institutional lock-in self-reinforcement equation:

dLockIntensity/dt ∝ dFamiliarity(Inst)/dt · FlowShare + Familiarity · dFlowShare/dt

Where dFlowShare/dt \> 0 when Familiarity(Inst) \> θ\_lock — once an institution exceeds the lock-in threshold, its flow-share automatically rises, forming positive feedback: the deeper the Familiar Route, the higher the flow-share, and the faster the Familiar Route deepens.

Institutional replacement cost dynamic equation:

dRepCost/dt ∝ dFamiliarity/dt · CoreDepth/φ\_{F\_S} + Familiarity · CoreDepth · (−dφ\_{F\_S}/dt) / φ\_{F\_S}²

Both terms are positive — Familiar Route deepening and declining Residual Capacity Ratio both raise the institutional replacement cost. This explains why the older the institution and the more solidified the social field, the harder reform becomes — not because the institution becomes more important, but because the replacement cost keeps rising.

Institutional lock-in self-reinforcing loop:

Familiarity(Inst) ↑ → FlowShare(Inst) ↑ → available continuation flow for alternative structures ↓ → difficulty of generating alternative structures ↑ → Reentry\_Inst ↑ → Familiarity(Inst) deepens further → LockIntensity ↑

Post-lock-in disturbance rebound equation:

If Shock \< T\_replace(F\_S) and AltPath fails and FlowReturn(Inst) \> 0, after the institution is disturbed:

Familiarity(Inst, t+τ) \> Familiarity(Inst, t)

That is, after a disturbance dissipates, the institutional Familiar Route typically exceeds its pre-disturbance level — because the disturbance briefly activates alternative paths, but when the alternative paths cannot stabilize, continuation flow rushes back to the old institutional path, raising both its FlowShare and Reentry.

## Section 5. Three Levels of Institutional Lock-in

Institutional lock-in manifests in three levels within the social field, each corresponding to a different range of Familiarity and a different degree of reform difficulty:

Level One: Soft lock-in (θ\_soft ≤ Familiarity \< θ\_lock). The institution has relatively high Familiar Route depth but has not yet exceeded the lock-in threshold. Reform is technically feasible at this level — the disturbance intensity need not exceed T\_replace, but only the current value of RepCost. Many successful incremental reforms in history are at this level. Observable criterion: after institutional reform, the new institution can operate stably without triggering strong rebound.

Level Two: Hard lock-in (Familiarity ≥ θ\_lock and LockIntensity high). The institution has exceeded the lock-in threshold and actively suppresses the generation of alternative structures. Incremental reform fails at this level — any input below T\_replace will be absorbed and trigger rebound. Only disturbances exceeding T\_replace can trigger genuine institutional replacement. Most “failed reforms” in history are at this level — reformers attempt to change hard-locked institutions with soft disturbances. Observable criterion: within a few years after reform, the institution drifts back to its pre-reform form and becomes harder to change than before.

Level Three: Deep lock-in (Familiarity ≥ θ\_lock and CoreDepth(Core\_S) very high). The institutional Familiar Routes are highly fused with the social Co-Nucleus, and the institution itself has become a component of Core\_S. Even when Shock \> T\_replace, the new institution formed after fracture is still constrained by δ\_res^C(F\_S) and grows in the direction of the old nucleus. The historically most resistant institutions (such as feudal systems, religious structures, and bureaucratic systems) are at this level. Observable criterion: even after revolution, the new institution differs in form but is highly similar in substantive structure to the old institution.

## Section 6. Theorems

Theorem One: Institutional Lock-in Inevitability Theorem

In a social field satisfying SocialGenesis = 1, with operating duration ≥ τ\_lock (institutional lock-in time threshold, τ\_lock ≥ τ\_S), and with at least one path satisfying FlowShare \> 0 ∧ Reentry \> 0, there necessarily exists at least one institution that has reached a hard lock-in state.

Proof outline: Social Chapter 1 proved K\_{F\_S} \> 0; Social Chapter 2 proved Inertia \> 0; by dFamiliarity/dt ∝ Reentry · (1−Z) · FlowShare, in a continuously operating social field, high-continuation-frequency paths necessarily accumulate sufficient depth; by Field Chapter 11’s Familiar Route measure equation, under conditions of no replacement-level disturbance and FlowShare \> 0 ∧ Reentry \> 0, dFamiliarity/dt \> 0 and Familiarity trends upward continuously; by Social Chapter 2’s Compression Inevitability, K\_{F\_S} trending upward causes φ\_{F\_S} to trend downward and RepCost to rise; after operating duration ≥ τ\_lock, Familiarity necessarily exceeds θ\_lock and FlowShare exceeds θ\_flow, and hard lock-in necessarily appears. ∎

Theorem Two: Reform Rebound Theorem

When an institution is in a hard lock-in state (Lock(Inst) = 1), if Shock \< T\_replace(F\_S) and the alternative path cannot stabilize (AltPath fails) and the old institutional path can re-receive continuation flow (FlowReturn(Inst) \> 0), then the reform attempt will not only fail but will cause Familiarity(Inst) to rise after dissipation, making the institution harder to change than before the reform.

Proof outline: when Shock \< T\_replace, the disturbance briefly activates alternative paths but they cannot stabilize (Field Chapter 22 absorption-type disturbance definition); AltPath fails → continuation flow returns to the institutional path (FlowReturn \> 0) → FlowShare(Inst) ↑ → Reentry\_Inst ↑ → dFamiliarity/dt ↑ → Familiarity(Inst, t+τ) \> Familiarity(Inst, t); reform rebound is not merely “returning to the original state” but further solidification of the institution. Note: if FlowReturn(Inst) ≈ 0 (e.g., when the institution has been thoroughly marginalized within the social field), the institution does not rebound after reform dissipates but simply fades — this theorem does not apply in that case. ∎

Theorem Three: Deep Lock-in Directional Constraint Theorem

When an institution reaches a deep lock-in state (Familiarity ≥ θ\_lock ∧ CoreDepth(Core\_S) very high), even when Shock \> T\_replace triggers institutional fracture, the generation direction of the new institution remains constrained by δ\_res^C(F\_S) and structurally tends toward resembling the old institution. Note: this theorem establishes a constraint on the direction of generation, not strict irreversibility — in rare civilizational-scale catastrophes, the δ\_res^C constraint may be fully broken, but these are exceptional cases.

Proof outline: by Field Chapter 18, Death\_C leaves δ\_res^C, constraining new field generation along the direction of the old nucleus (Birth-Death Closure Theorem); a deeply locked institution has become a component of Core\_S; the δ\_res^C produced by Core\_S’s Death\_C constrains the generation direction of the new Core\_S; therefore the structural characteristics of the new institution are constrained by the old nucleus and cannot fully depart from the form of the old institution. ∎

## Section 7. Boundary Cases and Structural Breakdown Conditions

Boundary Case One (Nascent institution): If institution Inst has just formed within the social field (Familiarity ≈ 0), then institutional lock-in does not hold and the institution can be replaced at low cost. A nascent institution has shallow Familiar Routes and low flow-share, making its replacement cost approach zero. This is a boundary condition and does not overturn Theorem One — Theorem One guarantees the existence of at least one locked-in institution within the social field, not that all institutions are locked in.

Breakdown Case Two (Supra-threshold disturbance): If Shock \> T\_replace(F\_S), a hard-locked institution can be genuinely fractured and institution Inst undergoes replacement. This is a structural breakdown condition corresponding to Social Chapter 12 (Conditions for Revolution). Note: T\_replace is very high for deeply locked institutions — historically, only civilizational-scale catastrophes have sometimes provided sufficient disturbance intensity. This condition does not overturn Theorem Two but delineates its applicable boundary: Theorem Two holds strictly when Shock \< T\_replace.

External Coupling Case Three (External institutional transplant): If an external field with high φ\_{F\_S} transplants a new institution into a low-φ\_{F\_S} social field through forced strong coupling (e.g., colonization, forced modernization), the old institutional lock-in can be temporarily broken. However, historical evidence shows (corresponding to Social Chapter 2, External Coupling Case Three) that old institutions tend to return in stronger form once forced coupling weakens. External transplantation does not overturn the theorems but reveals the δ\_res^C rebound mechanism of deeply locked institutions.

## Section 8. Summary Formula

Institutional lock-in dynamic general formula:

SocialGenesis = 1 ∧ operating duration ≥ τ\_lock ∧ ∃ q: (FlowShare\_q \> 0 ∧ Reentry\_q \> 0) ⇒ ∃ Inst: Lock(Inst, F\_S) = 1 (locked-in institution necessarily exists)

Familiarity(Inst) ↑ → FlowShare(Inst) ↑ → RepCost(Inst) ↑ (institutional lock-in self-reinforcement)

Shock \< T\_replace ∧ Lock(Inst) = 1 ∧ AltPath fails ∧ FlowReturn(Inst) \> 0 ⇒ Familiarity(Inst, t+τ) \> Familiarity(Inst, t) (Reform Rebound Theorem)

Familiarity(Inst) ≥ θ\_lock ∧ CoreDepth very high ⇒ Death\_C(Inst) → δ\_res^C constrains new institution direction (deep lock-in directional constraint)

RepCost(Inst) ∝ Familiarity · CoreDepth / φ\_{F\_S} (the older the institution and the more solidified the field, the higher the replacement cost)

Genuine institutional transformation requires Shock \> T\_replace(F\_S) — not better policy design, broader consensus, or greater political will; under conditions where Shock \< T\_replace, all such factors will be absorbed by the self-reinforcing loop of institutional lock-in.
