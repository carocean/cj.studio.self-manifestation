# Economic Flow Bias

This chapter argues a central proposition:

To acknowledge that material exchange paths exist within a social field is necessarily to acknowledge that the distribution of continuation flows across these paths is not uniform, but systematically biased toward high-Familiarity material paths — this continuation flow bias generates economic flow bias (EconFlow), and the positive feedback of economic flow bias forms material-flow concentration; the economy is not an independently operating resource system, but the material-path manifestation of the social field’s biased flow structure.

We seek to identify the dynamic equations of economic flow bias, and the structural reasons for material-flow concentration, market lock-in, and redistribution backflow.

We first state the result.

The core equation of economic flow bias:

EconFlow(P, F\_S) = ∑\_{f\_i ∈ F\_S} Cpl(f\_i → P) · FlowShare(f\_i → P, F\_S)

EconFlow(P) \> θ\_econ ⇒ positive feedback triggered, material path P enters self-reinforcing concentration state

In a single sentence

Material-flow concentration is not a peculiarity of capitalism,  
but a structural tendency of Familiar Route priority continuation in finite social fields;  
any redistribution that neither changes the Familiarity structure of material paths nor sustains Shock above T\_replace  
will ultimately be absorbed by the biased flow loop.

## Section 1. Purpose

Social Chapter 6 demonstrated opinion flow concentration: continuation flows form self-organizing concentration structures on topic paths after exceeding the critical threshold. But topic paths are only one type of path on which continuation flows can concentrate within the social field. There exists another, more fundamental type of path within the social field — material exchange paths: individual fields exchange material resources, labor, services, and symbolic assets through these paths.

The distribution of continuation flows across these material paths follows exactly the same dynamic laws as topic paths: high-Familiarity paths receive more continuation flows, Familiarity deepens further, forming positive feedback. This is economic flow bias.

Economic flow bias is not the “market failure” or “capital concentration” of economics, but the material-layer structural unfolding of the social field’s biased flow structure. Understanding this requires first dissolving the concept of “economy” back into its dynamic substrate: economic phenomena are not an independent object of analysis, but the material-layer expression of Familiar Route priority continuation on material paths within finite social fields.

This chapter provides the complete dynamic mechanism of economic flow bias, explaining why material concentration tendencies recur across different social formations; why pure resource redistribution cannot sustainably change the biased flow structure; and why technological revolutions can sometimes genuinely break material path lock-in while other transformations cannot.

## Section 2. Core Proposition

Economics typically attributes wealth concentration to: differences in marginal returns to factors of production (neoclassical), self-expanding capital accumulation (Marx), technological monopoly (Schumpeter), and institutional capture (new institutional economics). These explanations all point to correct phenomena, but offer no unified dynamic equation: why do these mechanisms recur across different social formations and institutional environments?

The position of On Self-Manifestation is:

Economic flow bias is the systematic bias of social field continuation flows on material paths generated by ΔFamiliarity — not a consequence of any particular institutional arrangement, but the material expression of the Familiar Route Priority Law in finite social fields. Wherever a social field contains material exchange paths and differences in Familiarity between paths exist, economic flow bias systematically tends to appear.

This definition carries three key implications:

First, economic flow bias is independent of institutional form. Capitalism, socialism, tribal exchange, AI platform economies, the tribute systems of ancient empires — as long as a finite social field exists, as long as Familiarity differentials exist among material paths, biased flow will appear and concentration will occur. Institutions can change the speed, form, replacement threshold, and compression strength of biased flow, but as long as Familiarity differentials and material continuation flow competition persist within a finite social field, institutions cannot eliminate the biased flow tendency itself — this is not to say institutions are meaningless, but that the effective scope of institutional intervention is the parameters of biased flow, not the existence of biased flow.

Second, wealth concentration is positive feedback, not linear accumulation. High-Familiarity material paths receive more continuation flows → paths deepen further → more continuation flows preferentially select these paths → concentration accelerates. This is a phase-transition structure, not merely a description of cumulative advantage, but a structural tendency supported by dynamic equations.

Third, the real obstacle to redistribution is the Familiarity structure, not the quantity of resources. Changing only the distribution ratio of material resources without changing the Familiarity structure of material paths, and with Shock failing to exceed T\_replace(P) over the long term, means continuation flows will return to old paths after redistribution, and concentration will re-emerge. This explains why repeated poverty relapse, industrial backflow, and wealth reconcentration recur across institutions and across history.

## Section 3. Operator Mapping

This chapter introduces the following new definitions on the basis of Social Chapters 1 through 6 operators:

Material path: denoted P, representing the set of stable continuation paths formed around material exchange (resources, labor, services, symbolic assets) within the social field. Material paths are the material cross-section of the social field’s Familiar Route structure — the formation of P depends on the historical coupling depth between individual fields and material exchange activities. Note: material paths are not equivalent to monetary paths or capital paths, nor are they equivalent to specific industries or single commodity paths, but rather stable path sets within the social field capable of carrying material continuation flows — EconFlow covers material continuation flows including but not limited to: labor flows, population migration, supply chains, infrastructure investment, logistics, data flows, platform user flows, flows of technical talent, and accumulation of industrial capability. Monetary and capital flows are merely the symbolically representable portion; any continuation flow connection between an individual field and a material path falls within the measurement scope of EconFlow.

Economic flow bias intensity: EconFlow(P, F\_S, t), representing the total intensity of continuation flow directed toward material path P within social field F\_S at time t.

EconFlow(P, F\_S) = ∑\_{f\_i ∈ F\_S} Cpl(f\_i → P, t) · FlowShare(f\_i → P, F\_S, t)

Where: Cpl(f\_i → P) is the coupling intensity between individual field f\_i and material path P; FlowShare(f\_i → P) is the proportion of f\_i’s total output flowing toward P, with 0 ≤ FlowShare ≤ 1, and ∑\_P FlowShare(f\_i → P) = 1. FlowShare uses a Type-B definition — a pure flow distribution proportion independent of coupling intensity (consistent with Social Chapter 6). Note: the allocation structure of FlowShare in multi-path competition is jointly determined by Familiarity, Compat, AvgCpl, and other parameters; its normalization-competition relationship is formally given by Field Chapter 11’s Familiar Route Priority Law and is directly cited here without further development.

Material path Familiar Route depth: Familiarity(P, F\_S, t), representing the overall Familiar Route depth of material path P within the social field. Note: the generation conditions for Familiarity are formally given by Field Chapter 11’s Familiar Route measure equation, jointly determined by four components: sedimentation depth (δ), re-entry frequency (Reentry), continuation resistance (Z), and flow-share proportion (FlowShare); this structure is directly cited here without further development.

dFamiliarity(P)/dt ∝ EconFlow(P) · φ\_{F\_S} − Decay\_P · Familiarity(P)

Where Decay\_P is the natural dissipation rate of the material path.

Economic flow bias critical threshold: θ\_econ, representing the value of EconFlow(P) beyond which positive feedback is triggered and the material path enters a self-reinforcing concentration state.

θ\_econ ∝ φ\_{F\_S} / K\_{F\_S}

(Structurally fully parallel to θ\_opinion — in highly path-stabilized social fields the material concentration threshold is lower and more easily exceeded)

Material path flow-share: FlowShare(P, F\_S), representing the proportion of continuation flows within the social field directed toward material path P. ∑\_P FlowShare(P, F\_S) ≤ 1.

Economic suppression effect: EconSuppressEffect(P, P’, F\_S), representing the degree to which high-EconFlow material path P suppresses the continuation flows of competing material path P’.

EconSuppressEffect(P, P’) ∝ max(0, FlowShare(P, F\_S) − θ\_econ\_sup) · K\_{F\_S}

Where θ\_econ\_sup is the economic suppression activation threshold (structurally parallel to θ\_sup in Social Chapter 6).

## Section 4. Dynamic Equations

Economic flow bias formation equation:

When EconFlow(P) \< θ\_econ (pre-bias phase):

dEconFlow(P)/dt ∝ OutFlow\_mean · Compat(P, F\_S) − Decay\_P · EconFlow(P)

Continuation flows compete among multiple material paths and EconFlow(P) is at a low-level dynamic equilibrium.

When EconFlow(P) \> θ\_econ (post-bias trigger):

dEconFlow(P)/dt ∝ EconFlow(P) · \[Familiarity(P, F\_S) · φ\_{F\_S} − Decay\_P\]

Note: in this equation φ\_{F\_S} appears as the field’s remaining writable capacity — the higher φ\_{F\_S}, the more easily new material paths obtain continuation flow support within the social field; the lower φ\_{F\_S} (the more solidified the field), the harder it is for formed biased flows to be replaced, and the harder it is for new material paths to gain a foothold.

Economic flow bias self-reinforcing loop:

EconFlow(P) ↑ → Familiarity(P, F\_S) ↑ → more individual fields preferentially select material path P (Familiar Route Priority Law) → FlowShare(P) ↑ → EconFlow(P) rises further

Simultaneously: EconSuppressEffect(P, P’) ↑ → competing material path P’ FlowShare ↓ → P’ continuation flows exhausted

Linkage between economic flow bias and stratum Familiar Routes:

By Social Chapter 4, individual fields with higher SFR(f\_i) (higher strata) have higher FamOverlap with the social field’s high-Familiarity material paths; the higher FamOverlap → the stronger StrCpl(f\_i → P\_high) → EconFlow tends to flow toward the material paths occupied by higher-stratum individual fields.

This is the coupling dynamic between economic flow bias and stratum solidification: stratum Familiar Route differentials (ΔStratum) directly translate into material path FlowShare differentials, and the two mutually reinforce each other.

dΔEconFlow(P\_high, P\_low)/dt ∝ \[AvgStrCpl(F\_high → P\_high) − AvgStrCpl(F\_low → P\_low)\] · φ\_{F\_S} − EconPolicyCorrection(F\_S)

Where AvgStrCpl(F\_high → P\_high) is the mean stratum coupling intensity between the high-stratum individual field group and high-Familiarity material paths, and AvgStrCpl(F\_low → P\_low) is the mean stratum coupling intensity between the low-stratum individual field group and low-Familiarity material paths; EconPolicyCorrection(F\_S) does not represent policy will itself, but the effective intensity with which policy actually changes FlowShare(P), Cpl(f\_i → P), or Familiarity(P).

## Section 5. Three Forms of Economic Flow Bias

Economic flow bias manifests in three observable forms of solidification within the social field:

Form One: Path monopoly bias. The EconFlow of a single material path exceeds θ\_econ and continuously self-reinforces, eventually occupying the vast majority of the social field’s continuation flows, forming platform monopolies, sector monopolies, and urban gravitational pull. Core mechanism: the Familiar Route Priority Law causes continuation flows to automatically surge toward the deepest material paths, requiring no subjective monopolization intent. Observable criterion: a small number of material paths continuously increase their FlowShare while competing paths continuously decline.

Form Two: Geographic concentration bias. Material continuation flows systematically flow toward the material paths of specific geographic regions (core cities, industrial belts, ports), causing Familiarity differentials between regional material paths to continuously expand. The mechanism is identical to path monopoly, operating along the geographic dimension. Observable criterion: talent, capital, and infrastructure continuously concentrate toward existing high-Familiarity nodes, while peripheral regional material paths accelerate in dissipation (Decay\_P rises).

Form Three: Intergenerational solidification bias. High-Familiarity material paths are transmitted across generations through Social Chapter 4’s stratum Familiar Route mechanism — high-stratum individual fields transmit their high FamOverlap with material paths to the next generation, maintaining the material path biased flow structure across generations. Observable criterion: the intergenerational correlation of material paths (the correlation coefficient between parental material path Familiarity and offspring material path Familiarity) remains persistently high.

## Section 6. Theorems

Theorem One: Economic Flow Bias Formation Tendency Theorem

In any social field satisfying SocialGenesis = 1, given material path Familiarity asymmetry (∃ P\_i, P\_j: Familiarity(P\_i, t\_0) ≠ Familiarity(P\_j, t\_0)) and non-zero material continuation flow input (∃ P: EconFlow(P, t\_0) \> 0), the system systematically tends toward the appearance of at least one material path P whose EconFlow exceeds θ\_econ, forming economic flow bias concentration.

Proof outline: by Field Chapter 11’s Familiar Route Priority Law, in multi-path competition, high-Familiarity paths systematically acquire higher FlowShare; by θ\_econ ∝ φ\_{F\_S} / K\_{F\_S}, K\_{F\_S} trending upward causes θ\_econ to decline; under conditions where average Cpl does not tend to zero and participating individual field scale does not decline, FlowShare(P) ↑ pushes EconFlow(P) ↑; therefore the system systematically tends toward the appearance of some material path P whose EconFlow exceeds the continually declining θ\_econ, and biased flow concentration forms. ∎

Theorem Two: Redistribution Backflow Theorem

In a social field where biased flow has already formed (EconFlow(P) \> θ\_econ), any redistributive intervention that does not change the Familiarity structure of material paths (changing only the instantaneous distribution ratio of EconFlow without changing Familiarity(P)), when the intervention intensity is below the replacement threshold T\_replace(P), will cause continuation flows to flow back to old material paths after the intervention dissipates; the biased flow recovers and, under conditions where FlowReturn(P) \> 0, may be stronger than before the intervention.

Proof outline: by Social Chapter 3’s Reform Rebound Theorem, AltPath fails ∧ FlowReturn \> 0 ⇒ Familiarity(P, t+τ) \> Familiarity(P, t); changing only the resource allocation quantity without changing Familiarity(P) → the FlowShare advantage of old material paths is unchanged → continuation flows flow back after intervention dissipates → EconFlow(P) recovers and may exceed pre-intervention levels; therefore redistribution that neither touches the Familiarity structure nor sustains Shock above T\_replace over the long term systematically tends toward backflow to old paths and triggers biased flow rebound. ∎

Theorem Three: Technological Path Replacement Theorem

When a new technology provides a material path P’’ whose Shock \> T\_replace(P), the old material path P fractures, economic flow bias is redistributed, and a new biased flow structure forms; but the new biased flow structure remains constrained by the old path residual (δ\_res^C) and structurally tends toward partial resemblance to the old biased flow.

Proof outline: by Field Chapter 22’s fracture-type disturbance, Shock \> T\_replace triggers path fracture and old material path EconFlow collapses; when continuation flows are redistributed they preferentially enter the next-highest Familiarity path (Familiar Route Priority Law); by Field Chapter 18’s residual constraint, δ\_res^C constrains the generation direction of new paths; therefore technological revolution can genuinely break old biased flow structures, but new biased flow structures do not start from zero, being constrained by old nucleus residuals. ∎

## Section 7. Boundary Cases and Structural Breakdown Conditions

Boundary Case One (High-φ\_{F\_S} nascent social field): If the social field is in a high-φ\_{F\_S} state (nascent markets, post-revolution reconstruction periods), θ\_econ is systematically high, material paths are difficult to form stable biased flows, and economic opportunities are relatively dispersed. The historically higher stratum mobility in nascent markets is consistent with this prediction. This is a boundary condition supporting Theorem One (θ\_econ declines as K\_{F\_S} ↑) and does not overturn it.

Breakdown Case Two (External forced restructuring): If an external field injects Shock exceeding T\_replace into the social field through forced coupling (e.g., wartime destruction, revolution, or forced industrialization following colonization), old material paths fracture and economic flow bias can be temporarily broken. But by the residual constraint (δ\_res^C), new biased flow structures remain influenced by the old nucleus and often re-differentiate within a few generations. This is a structural breakdown condition corresponding to Theorem Three and does not overturn Theorem One.

Breakdown Case Three (Technological rupture creating new paths): Technological ruptures such as the printing press, the Industrial Revolution, the internet, and AI have provided new material paths with Shock \> T\_replace, genuinely fracturing old biased flow structures. But the new biased flows solidify again within decades (new platform monopolies, new sector concentrations, new geographic cores), confirming Theorem Three: replacement occurs, but biased flow systematically tends to re-form.

## Section 8. Summary Formula

Economic flow bias dynamic general formula:

SocialGenesis = 1 ∧ ∃ P\_i, P\_j: Familiarity(P\_i, t\_0) ≠ Familiarity(P\_j, t\_0) ∧ ∃ P: EconFlow(P, t\_0) \> 0 ∧ θ\_econ declines as K\_{F\_S} ↑ ⇒ system systematically tends toward ∃ P: EconFlow(P) \> θ\_econ (Economic Flow Bias Formation Tendency Theorem)

EconFlow(P) \> θ\_econ ⇒ Familiarity(P) ↑ → FlowShare(P) ↑ → EconFlow(P) accelerates (biased flow positive feedback)

ΔStratum(f\_i, f\_j) ↑ → ΔEconFlow(P\_high, P\_low) ↑ (stratum Familiar Route differentials directly translate into material path biased flow differentials)

Redistributive intervention ∧ Familiarity(P) unchanged ∧ Shock \< T\_replace(P) ∧ AltPath fails ∧ FlowReturn(P) \> 0 ⇒ EconFlow(P, t+τ) ≥ EconFlow(P, t), tending toward strengthening when FlowReturn remains positive (Redistribution Backflow Theorem)

Shock(P’’) \> T\_replace(P) ⇒ old biased flow fractures ∧ δ\_res^C constrains new biased flow direction (Technological Path Replacement Theorem)

The fundamental path to changing economic flow bias is not redistributing the quantity of resources, but changing the Familiarity structure of material paths — and the disturbance intensity required to change Familiarity structure is determined by T\_replace(P), not by policy will; technological rupture is one of the few mechanisms in history that genuinely breaks old biased flow structures, but technological rupture itself is not a fairness mechanism — it is only a path replacement mechanism: it can fracture old biased flows, but it cannot prevent new biased flows from forming.
