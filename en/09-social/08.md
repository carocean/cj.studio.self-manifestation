# Consensus Formation

This chapter argues a central proposition:

To acknowledge that a social field has been established is necessarily to acknowledge that multiple individual fields are engaged in sustained coupling within it; this sustained coupling systematically tends, over sufficient time, to produce deep overlap in the Familiar Route structures among individual fields, forming a Social Co-Nucleus (Core\_S); consensus is not the statistical result of opinions, but the manifestation of the Social Co-Nucleus within the social field — Consensus(F\_S) = Manifestation(Core\_S).

We seek to identify the dynamic equations of consensus formation, and the conditions for determining consensus depth, the consensus replacement threshold, and the relationship between consensus and truth-status.

We first state the result.

The core equation of consensus formation:

dδ(Core\_S)/dt ∝ CrossCpl\_mean · FamOverlap\_mean · φ\_{F\_S} − Decay(Core\_S)

Consensus(F\_S) = Manifestation(Core\_S), consensus depth ∝ δ(Core\_S)

In a single sentence

Consensus is not what the majority agrees on,  
but the manifestation of the Co-Nucleus structure formed by multiple individual fields

consensus depth is determined by δ(Core\_S), not by the distribution of opinions.

## Section 1. Purpose

Social Chapter 6 demonstrated opinion flow concentration: continuation flows form self-organizing concentration structures on topic paths with high OpinionFlow. But opinion flow concentration does not equal consensus formation — this is the most important prior distinction of this chapter:

OpinionFlow(T) ↑ ≠ δ(Core\_S) ↑

Public opinion can surge (high OpinionFlow) while consensus remains entirely unchanged (δ(Core\_S) unchanged); conversely, consensus can be extremely deep (high δ(Core\_S)) while opinion activity is extremely low (OpinionFlow low) — deep cultural identity, religious belief, and national narrative can exist stably for centuries without any eruption of opinion activity.

Sociology typically treats consensus as “public opinion” or “collective belief,” attributing consensus formation to communication, negotiation, and socialization processes. These explanations point to correct phenomena, but offer no dynamic equation: why is some consensus extremely hard to change even in the face of extensive counter-evidence? Why does some consensus suddenly collapse without any apparent discussion? Why do old ideas persist stubbornly after revolution?

This chapter answers from sedimentation field dynamics: the stability of consensus comes from the historical depth of δ(Core\_S), not from the current distribution of opinions; changing consensus requires changing the Co-Nucleus structure itself, not changing opinion expression.

## Section 2. Core Proposition

The position of On Self-Manifestation is:

Consensus is the manifestation of the Social Co-Nucleus (Core\_S) within the social field — not the aggregation of majority opinions, but the deep Familiar Route overlap structure formed by multiple individual fields through sustained coupling; the strength of consensus is determined by δ(Core\_S), the stability of consensus is determined by K\_Core, and the conditions for changing consensus are determined by T\_replace(Core\_S).

This definition carries three key implications:

First, consensus and opinion are independent variables. OpinionFlow measures the current concentration intensity of continuation flows on topic paths; δ(Core\_S) measures the historical sedimentation depth of the Co-Nucleus — the two can vary in the same direction or in opposite directions. Opinion eruption can accelerate the formation of shallow-layer Familiarity within the Co-Nucleus, but genuinely changing δ(Core\_S) requires sustained deep coupling, not short-term flow concentration.

Second, consensus does not equal truth-status. A Co-Nucleus with high δ(Core\_S) can contain erroneous content (worldviews, religious doctrines, and ideologies widely accepted in history but later falsified). The stability of the Co-Nucleus comes from structural depth, not from the correctness of content — this is the fundamental distinction between consensus and knowledge, to be given a boundary clarification at the end of the Summary Formula.

Third, the true resistance to changing consensus is K\_Core, not “conservatives.” K\_Core ∝ δ(Core\_S) · Ω(Core\_S) — the deeper the Co-Nucleus and the more structurally complete, the stronger its resistance to replacement; this is structural resistance, not the result of any person or group actively maintaining it.

## Section 5. Three Structural Forms of Consensus

Consensus manifests in three observable structural forms within the social field, depending on the combination of δ(Core\_S) and Frag(F\_S):

Form One: Explicit strong consensus (high δ, low Frag). Co-Nucleus depth is high and social field internal fragmentation is low; consensus is highly visible in the public sphere and opposing views cannot obtain sufficient continuation flow support. Observable criterion: heterogeneous views are rapidly suppressed within the social field without requiring explicit power intervention — the assimilation-dominant state of Social Chapter 5. Examples: core values in highly homogeneous societies, worldviews in traditional religious societies.

Form Two: Implicit strong consensus (high δ, low ConsensusVisible). Co-Nucleus depth is high but visibility is low — consensus exists in the form of default assumptions, implicit norms, and “self-evident” truths that need not be explicitly expressed. Observable criterion: when someone violates the consensus, strong social reaction is triggered, yet almost no one had explicitly discussed the norm beforehand. Examples: pragmatic rules of language, deep social etiquette, unwritten power hierarchies.

Form Three: Explicit weak consensus (low δ, high Frag). Co-Nucleus depth is low and fragmentation is high; multiple competing Sub-Cores exist within the social field, each forming local concentration but none achieving unified consensus at the full-field level. Observable criterion: the same issue triggers completely opposite senses of “obvious correctness” in different groups — each sub-field believes its own position is the consensus. Examples: political issues in highly polarized societies, value disputes during multicultural conflict periods.

## Section 6. Theorems

Theorem One: Consensus Deepening Tendency Theorem

In a social field satisfying SocialGenesis = 1, CrossCpl\_mean \> 0, and FamOverlap\_mean not declining, under conditions where CrossCpl\_mean · FamOverlap\_mean · φ\_{F\_S} \> Decay(Core\_S), δ(Core\_S) tends to rise over time and consensus systematically tends to deepen.

Proof outline: by dδ(Core\_S)/dt ∝ CrossCpl · FamOverlap · φ\_{F\_S} − Decay, when the positive term exceeds the decay term, δ monotonically trends upward; by Social Chapter 1’s social field formation conditions, SocialGenesis = 1 guarantees CrossCpl\_mean \> 0; FamOverlap\_mean not declining is an explicit premise (note: K\_{F\_S} trending upward does not automatically guarantee FamOverlap ↑ — in polarized societies K ↑ may accompany Frag ↑, causing FamOverlap among Sub-Cores to actually decline; this condition must hold independently); therefore under conditions where all the above are satisfied and fragmentation is not excessive, consensus systematically tends to deepen. ∎

Theorem Two: Consensus Does Not Equal Truth-Status Theorem

High δ(Core\_S) (deep consensus) does not entail that the content of Core\_S is true — the stability of the Co-Nucleus comes from structural depth, not from the correctness of content; truth-status influences consensus only indirectly through real-world feedback (the injection of Seed and Shock) and does not directly determine δ.

Proof outline: by the Co-Nucleus deepening equation, dδ/dt is determined by CrossCpl · FamOverlap · φ, with no direct functional relationship to the truth-status of Core\_S’s content; historically, Co-Nuclei maintaining high δ over extended periods have contained large amounts of content later falsified (geocentrism, creationism, various ideologies); truth-status can influence consensus indirectly by triggering Shock (real-world feedback forcibly injecting new differentials incompatible with the old Co-Nucleus) to lower δ or trigger replacement, but Shock itself is also constrained by T\_replace. Therefore high δ does not entail truth-status, and the two are independent. ∎

Theorem Three: Consensus Replacement Threshold Theorem

The minimum disturbance intensity required to change social consensus (cause Core\_S to undergo structural replacement) is T\_replace(Core\_S) ∝ K\_Core ∝ δ(Core\_S) · Ω(Core\_S); any information input, persuasion attempt, or policy intervention below this threshold will be absorbed by Core\_S without changing the consensus structure; after consensus fracture, δ\_res^C constrains the growth direction of the new consensus.

Proof outline: by Field Chapter 22, when Shock \< T\_replace, disturbances are absorbed by the field; by T\_replace ∝ K\_Core ∝ δ · Ω, Co-Nuclei with higher δ have higher replacement thresholds; by Field Chapter 18, Death\_C produces δ\_res^C, and the growth direction of the new Co-Nucleus is constrained by old nucleus residuals; therefore changing deep consensus requires supra-threshold disturbance, and the new consensus does not begin growing from zero. ∎

## Section 7. Boundary Cases and Structural Breakdown Conditions

Boundary Case One (Rapidly formed shallow consensus): In a high-φ\_{F\_S} nascent social field, CrossCpl may be extremely high while historical sedimentation is shallow, making it possible to quickly form apparently high ConsensusVisible, but δ(Core\_S) remains low and T\_replace is also low — such consensus forms easily and collapses easily. Ideological consensus in the early stages of revolutions and early beliefs in nascent religious movements fit this pattern. This is a boundary condition and does not overturn Theorem One.

Boundary Case Two (Local consensus in highly fragmented society): If Frag(F\_S) is extremely high, multiple mutually independent Sub-Cores form within the social field; each Sub-Core’s δ may be very high, but Core\_S at the full-field level is actually fractured. In this case “consensus” holds only within sub-fields, and unified consensus does not exist across sub-fields. This is the extreme form of explicit weak consensus and does not overturn Theorem One (Theorem One addresses Co-Nucleus deepening within sub-fields), but delimits the theorem’s applicable range.

Boundary Case Three (Truth-status triggering rapid consensus replacement): If real-world feedback is extremely strong (such as major natural disasters, technological failures, or military defeat), Shock far exceeding T\_replace can be provided in a short time, triggering rapid replacement even when δ(Core\_S) is very high. Japan’s rapid post-World War II worldview restructuring is a representative case. This is a structural breakdown condition supporting Theorem Three (T\_replace can be exceeded by sufficiently strong Shock) and does not overturn it.

SocialGenesis = 1 ∧ CrossCpl\_mean \> 0 ∧ FamOverlap\_mean not declining ∧ CrossCpl · FamOverlap · φ \> Decay(Core\_S) ⇒ dδ(Core\_S)/dt \> 0 (Consensus Deepening Tendency)

Consensus(F\_S) = Manifestation(Core\_S), consensus depth ∝ δ(Core\_S) (Consensus Manifestation Equation)

OpinionFlow(T) ↑ ≠ δ(Core\_S) ↑ (opinion flow concentration does not equal consensus formation)

δ(Core\_S) ↑ ≠ True(Core\_S) (consensus depth does not equal content truth-status)

Shock \< T\_replace(Core\_S) ⇒ information input absorbed by Co-Nucleus, consensus unchanged (Consensus Replacement Threshold Theorem)

Shock \> T\_replace(Core\_S) ⇒ Core\_S fractures ∧ δ\_res^C constrains new consensus direction

The fundamental path to changing consensus is not better arguments or more discussion, but changing δ(Core\_S) itself — and changing δ(Core\_S) requires either sustained structural shocks exceeding T\_replace, or sustained cross-field deep coupling over a long enough period to gradually replace the old FamOverlap structure with new coupling patterns.

Note: the relationship between consensus and truth-status is an important boundary clarification of Social Chapter 8. This chapter demonstrates that the stability of consensus comes from structure, not from truth-status — this is not to say “truth does not exist” or “consensus is truth,” but to say: at the social field dynamic level, the maintenance mechanism of consensus is independent of the truth-status of content; truth-status influences consensus evolution indirectly through real-world feedback mechanisms (Seed, Shock), but this belongs to the discussion of Social Chapter 17 (Social Evolution Law).
