# State Structure

This chapter argues a central proposition:

Social Chapter 10 demonstrated the generation of the power field: in a social field with finite continuation slots and differential candidate continuations, PowerPos differentials systematically tend to expand, power structure tends to form, and δ(Power) systematically tends to deepen. This chapter addresses the structural transition that occurs after the power field deepens past a threshold — State formation: when δ(Power) exceeds the institutionalization threshold, and the continuation slot allocation rule itself also undergoes sedimentation (δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \> θ\_rule), a wholly new structure appears within the social field — the rule of rules; the State is not the power field itself, but the institutional sedimentation of the power field, the condition in which the continuation slot allocation rule has been solidified into a stable structure.

We seek to identify the dynamic conditions for State formation, and the equations for determining the relationship between the State and the power field, the institutional continuity of the State, and the conditions for State destabilization.

We first state the result.

The core condition for State formation:

StateGenesis(F\_S) = 1 ⟺ δ(Power) \> θ\_state ∧ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \> θ\_rule

In a single sentence

The mark of a State’s appearance is not that someone rules,  
but that even how ruling proceeds has been regulated —  
the State is the institutional sedimentation of the power field, not the power field itself;  
the PowerCore can change hands and the State still endures,  
until the rule structure itself collapses.

## Section 1. Purpose

Social Chapter 10 provided the generation conditions and deepening mechanism of the power field. But the existence of a power field does not equal the existence of a State — historically there have been many social formations with highly concentrated power fields but no State structure: warlord rule, tribal chieftaincy, criminal organizations. These formations all have a high-PowerPos core field, but no State.

What they lack is not power, but: the sedimentation of the continuation slot allocation rule itself — the regularization, institutionalization, and transferability of the question “how is governance conducted.”

State formation means that two things occur simultaneously: first, some PowerCore’s δ(Power) exceeds the minimum depth required for power structure to operate stably; second, the continuation slot allocation rule formed around this power structure itself deepens into a stable structure — no longer dependent on specific PowerCore members as individuals, but dependent on the continuation inertia of the rules themselves.

This is a structural transition, not a quantitative change. Before the State appears, power belongs to “someone”; after the State appears, power belongs to “a rule-structure.”

This chapter explains why the State emerges (formation conditions); why the State is more stable than the power field (institutional continuity); and under what conditions the State destabilizes (separation of Legitimation Core and Enforcement Core), providing a structural entry point for Social Chapter 12’s revolutionary disturbance conditions.

## Section 2. Core Proposition

The position of On Self-Manifestation is:

The State is the institutional structure produced when the power field deepens beyond the institutionalization threshold and the continuation slot allocation rule itself undergoes sedimentation — not an extension of some PowerCore, but the structure produced when PowerCore’s long-term slot occupation solidifies “how to allocate continuation slots” as a rule into a new sedimentation structure; the stability of the State comes from the depth of δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation), not from the strength of the current PowerCore.

This definition carries three key implications:

First, State formation requires dual sedimentation. High δ(Power) alone is insufficient to produce a State — warlords, criminal organizations, and tribal chiefs can have extremely high δ(Power), but without δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \> θ\_rule (sedimentation of the rule itself), the result is only coercive power, not a State. The mark of a State is the appearance of the rule of rules: the rule governing how continuation slots are allocated itself becomes an object of sedimentation.

Second, PowerCore succession does not equal State replacement. Since the State’s stability comes from δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) rather than the strength of the current PowerCore, PowerCore succession (elections, coups, dynastic change) does not trigger State replacement as long as the rule structure itself is not disrupted. The succession of US presidents, France’s repeated partisan alternations, and the dynastic transitions throughout history are all instances of PowerCore succession; some rule structures (such as institutional frameworks, judicial systems, administrative conventions) maintained continuity while other rule structures (such as the source of legitimacy and the mode of power authorization) underwent replacement — this is precisely why historians dispute “whether dynasty X constitutes the same State as dynasty Y”: what they are disputing is exactly which parts of the rule structure continued and which were replaced.

Third, the fundamental cause of State destabilization is the collapse of the rule structure, not the weakening of power. When the Legitimation Core and Enforcement Core undergo structural separation, and the decay rate of δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) exceeds its deepening rate, the rule structure begins to lose its capacity to sustain PowerCore succession, and the State enters a destabilization state — this is one of the structural preconditions for revolution.

## Section 3. Operator Mapping

This chapter introduces the following new definitions on the basis of Social Chapter 10 operators:

Continuation slot allocation rule: Rule\_SlotAllocation(F\_S), representing the stable rule structure within the social field governing “how to allocate continuation slots.” Rule\_SlotAllocation is a meta-level structure — it does not directly allocate continuation slots, but specifies the procedures, authority, and legitimacy sources for allocating them. Note: Rule\_SlotAllocation initially originates from the repeated continuation of PowerCore — PowerCore occupies continuation slots over extended periods, and its mode of occupation is gradually recognized, accepted, and solidified by the social field into rules; but once δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) exceeds θ\_rule, the rule structure acquires relatively independent continuation capacity, no longer fully dependent on the existence of the current PowerCore — the rule itself becomes a new sedimentation structure that in turn constrains the scope of PowerCore’s behavior. This is the dynamic turning point of “Power creates rules, rules in turn constrain Power.”

Rule sedimentation depth: δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation), representing the historical sedimentation depth of the continuation slot allocation rule within the social field. The higher δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation), the harder the rule is to replace and the stronger the State’s institutional continuity.

State formation condition: StateGenesis(F\_S) = 1, representing that a State structure has already appeared within the social field, with formation condition:

StateGenesis(F\_S) = 1 ⟺ δ(Power) \> θ\_state ∧ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \> θ\_rule

That is, the power field depth exceeds the institutionalization threshold, and the sedimentation depth of the continuation slot allocation rule itself exceeds the rule stabilization threshold. Note: this assumes Rule\_SlotAllocation possesses minimum executability — if rules exist but are unenforceable (such as nominal constitutions, hollowed-out laws), the result is only a nominal institution, not a complete State structure; nominal institutions correspond to states where δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) exceeds θ\_rule but enforcement penetration is extremely low — the State form exists but substantive function is absent.

State strength: K\_State(F\_S), representing the State structure’s capacity to resist replacement and collapse.

K\_State ∝ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) · Ω(Rule\_SlotAllocation) · PowerCoreCpl

That is, the deeper and more complete the rule structure and the stronger the power-Co-Nucleus coupling, the higher the State strength.

State replacement threshold: T\_replace(State), representing the minimum disturbance intensity required to trigger genuine replacement of the State structure. T\_replace(State) ∝ K\_State ∝ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) · Ω(Rule\_SlotAllocation). Note: T\_replace(State) is typically far higher than T\_replace(Power) — replacing the PowerCore is far easier than replacing the State structure.

State fragmentation: Frag(State), representing the structural fragmentation degree within the State, determined by the degree of separation between the Legitimation Core and Enforcement Core:

Frag(State) ∝ Δ(LegitimationCore, EnforcementCore) = \|PowerPos(Legitimation) − PowerPos(Enforcement)\|

Note: Frag(State) is defined by Δ, and the dynamic sources of Δ are of two types: first, social field fragmentation from Social Chapter 9 (Frag(F\_S) ↑) differentially eroding the supporting bases of the Legitimation Core and Enforcement Core; second, external disturbances differentially shocking the two cores (e.g., economic crises weakening the Enforcement Core, ideological crises weakening the Legitimation Core). Frag(State) is not self-generating but is the result of social field fragmentation and external disturbances propagating to the State level.

State residual: δ\_res^S, representing the institutional residual of the old State structure within the social field after replacement, constraining the growth direction of new State structures.

## Section 4. Dynamic Equations

Rule structure deepening equation:

dδ(Rule\_SlotAllocation)/dt ∝ PowerPos\_mean · FlowShare(Power) · φ\_{F\_S} · LegitimacyOverlap − Decay(Rule)

Where LegitimacyOverlap := FamOverlap(PowerCore, Core\_S), representing the Familiar Route overlap between PowerCore and the Social Co-Nucleus Core\_S — that is, how much of PowerCore’s continuation priority derives from its deep embedding in the consensus structure. Note: the distinction between LegitimacyOverlap and PowerCoreCpl: PowerCoreCpl is the total coupling intensity between PowerCore and Core\_S (encompassing all dimensions of structural linkage), while LegitimacyOverlap is the sub-quantity specifically denoting the consensus overlap component (the FamOverlap dimension). The two are directionally consistent but differ in granularity; this chapter uses LegitimacyOverlap as the driver term in the rule structure deepening equation to emphasize the direct contribution of consensus embedding to rule legitimacy. Decay(Rule) ∝ Frag(State) · (1 − Recompression\_S) is the rule structure decay term.

Note: rule structure deepening depends on PowerCore’s slot occupation, but simultaneously requires LegitimacyOverlap to be sufficiently high — pure coercive-suppression power (low PowerCoreCpl) can sustain high δ(Power), but cannot produce high δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation), because there is no consensus basis to support rule legitimacy. This explains why warlord rule cannot stably evolve into a full State without an additional consensus-integration process.

State continuity equation:

PowerCore succession ⇒ State continuity, if and only if δ(Power) ≥ θ\_state ∧ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) does not fall below θ\_rule during the succession process

That is, as long as the rule structure’s sedimentation depth is maintained above the threshold and the power field’s minimum continuation capacity is not lost, PowerCore succession (elections, coups, dynastic change) does not trigger State replacement. The continuity of the State is the continuity of the rule structure, not the continuity of the PowerCore.

State destabilization equation:

Frag(State) ↑ ⟺ Δ(LegitimationCore, EnforcementCore) ↑

dδ(Rule\_SlotAllocation)/dt \< 0 if and only if Decay(Rule) \> PowerPos\_mean · FlowShare · φ · LegitimacyOverlap

State destabilization tendency ⟺ dδ(Rule\_SlotAllocation)/dt \< 0 and δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) trending toward θ\_rule (dynamics of competing rule structure ascent addressed in Social Chapter 12)

## Section 5. Three Structural Forms of the State

The State manifests in three observable institutional forms within the social field, depending on the combination of LegitimacyOverlap and δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation):

Form One: Consensus-embedded State (high LegitimacyOverlap, high δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation)). The rule structure is deeply embedded in the Social Co-Nucleus; the continuation slot allocation rule is taken for granted by the vast majority of individual fields in the social field. Observable phenomena: PowerCore succession does not trigger a legitimacy crisis; State rules are actively obeyed rather than merely coercively enforced; individual fields internalize State rules into their own Familiar Route structure (high FamOverlap(f\_i, Rule)). Examples: mature democratic states, stable periods of traditional dynasties, classical city-states.

Form Two: Coercive-maintenance State (low LegitimacyOverlap, persistently high K\_{Power}/K\_{F\_S}). The rule structure lacks consensus foundation; the State depends on the Enforcement Core’s coercive suppression to maintain slot allocation. Observable phenomena: high maintenance costs (large-scale surveillance, enforcement institutions, stability expenditure); high rule compliance rate but low internalization rate; PowerCore succession easily triggers short-term destabilization. Examples: colonial regimes, occupation governments, some authoritarian states.

Form Three: Rule-competitive State (multiple competing rule structures, high Frag(State)). Multiple mutually competing continuation slot allocation rule systems exist within the State, each supporting different PowerCore candidates without any achieving integration. Observable phenomena: frequent constitutional crises; internal conflicts within the legal system; mutual obstruction between executive and legislative institutions; different regions effectively applying different rules. Examples: federal crises, constitutional transition periods, institutional fractures on the eve of civil war.

## Section 6. Theorems

Theorem One: State Formation Tendency Theorem

In a social field satisfying StateGenesis = 0 (State has not yet appeared) but δ(Power) \> θ\_state, if LegitimacyOverlap persistently remains positive and PowerPos\_mean · FlowShare · φ · LegitimacyOverlap \> Decay(Rule), then δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) systematically tends to rise and the social field systematically tends to satisfy StateGenesis = 1.

Proof outline: by the rule structure deepening equation, when the positive term exceeds the decay term δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) trends upward; by LegitimacyOverlap \> 0, PowerCore’s continuation flow advantage can convert into rule structure sedimentation momentum; once the continuation slot allocation rule begins sedimenting, its Familiar Route effect (rules repeatedly obeyed → rules deepen → more individual fields accustom themselves to obeying rules → rules deepen further) forms positive feedback; therefore under conditions where power is sufficiently deep and consensus foundation exists, State formation systematically tends to occur. ∎

Theorem Two: State Continuity Theorem

In an already-formed State (StateGenesis = 1), under the premise that the power field’s minimum continuation capacity has not been lost (δ(Power) ≥ θ\_state), PowerCore succession does not trigger State replacement if and only if δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) is maintained above θ\_rule during the succession process; the historical continuity of the State is the continuity of the rule structure, not the continuity of the PowerCore.

Proof outline: by the dual-condition definition of StateGenesis, State establishment depends only on δ(Power) \> θ\_state ∧ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \> θ\_rule; PowerCore succession only changes the field currently occupying continuation slots, without directly changing the sedimentation depth of the rule structure; as long as δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) ≥ θ\_rule is maintained during the succession, StateGenesis = 1 continues to hold; therefore PowerCore succession ≠ State replacement, unless the succession process simultaneously triggers rule structure collapse. ∎

Theorem Three: State Destabilization Tendency Theorem

In an already-formed State (StateGenesis = 1), if Frag(State) continuously rises and Decay(Rule) \> PowerPos\_mean · FlowShare · φ · LegitimacyOverlap, then δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) systematically tends to decline and the State systematically tends toward destabilization. When δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) trends below θ\_rule, StateGenesis tends toward zero and the State enters the pre-replacement state. How competing rule structures ascend and seize integrative status belongs to the domain of revolutionary disturbance dynamics, developed in Social Chapter 12.

Proof outline: by dδ(Rule)/dt ∝ PowerPos · FlowShare · φ · LegitimacyOverlap − Decay(Rule), when Decay exceeds the deepening term δ trends downward; by Frag(State) ∝ Δ(LegitimationCore, EnforcementCore), the separation of the Legitimation Core and Enforcement Core causes Decay(Rule) to rise and LegitimacyOverlap to decline, doubly accelerating δ’s decline; by the StateGenesis dual condition, δ(Rule) \< θ\_rule triggers StateGenesis = 0; therefore Frag(State) persistently rising under insufficient correction systematically tends toward State destabilization. ∎

## Section 7. Boundary Cases and Structural Breakdown Conditions

Boundary Case One (Persistent strong rule without consensus foundation): Under certain historical configurations (some late-stage empires, occupation regimes), δ(Power) is extremely high but LegitimacyOverlap remains persistently low, and the State remains in coercive-maintenance form unable to evolve into consensus-embedded form. In this case δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) depends on coercive maintenance; once the Enforcement Core weakens, the rule structure rapidly collapses. This is a boundary of Form Two and does not overturn Theorem One (Theorem One requires LegitimacyOverlap to remain persistently positive, which is not satisfied here).

Boundary Case Two (External Shock preserving rule continuity): Even when PowerCore undergoes violent succession (revolution), if the new PowerCore actively inherits part of the old rule structure’s framework (such as inheriting a constitution or preserving the judicial system), δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) may not fall below θ\_rule and the State maintains formal continuity. Partial institutional inheritance after the French Revolution and institutional transplantation during Japan’s Meiji Restoration fit this pattern. This is a boundary condition supporting Theorem Two and does not overturn it.

Breakdown Case Three (Dual destabilization triggered simultaneously): Under some historical circumstances, internal fragmentation and competing rule structure ascent occur simultaneously, forming combined destabilization — the old rule structure collapses rapidly under dual internal and external pressure, and the new State structure rebuilds quickly under high-φ\_{F\_S} conditions. The rapid State reconstruction following empire collapse (such as the European state system reconstitution after World War I) fits this pattern. This is a structural breakdown condition supporting Theorem Three and does not overturn it.

## Section 8. Summary Formula

State structure dynamics general formula:

δ(Power) \> θ\_state ∧ LegitimacyOverlap \> 0 ∧ deepening term \> Decay(Rule) ⇒ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) ↑ ⇒ systematically tends toward StateGenesis = 1 (State Formation Tendency Theorem)

StateGenesis = 1 ∧ PowerCore succession ∧ δ(Power) ≥ θ\_state ∧ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) ≥ θ\_rule ⇒ State continuity (State Continuity Theorem)

Δ(LegitimationCore, EnforcementCore) ↑ ∧ Decay(Rule) \> deepening term ⇒ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) ↓ ⇒ StateInstability ↑ (State Destabilization Tendency Theorem)

δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \< θ\_rule ⇒ StateGenesis = 0 (State structure no longer holds; dynamics of competing rule structure ascent addressed in Social Chapter 12)

δ\_res^S constrains the growth direction of new State structures — old institutional residuals mean no State replacement begins from a structural zero point

When the separation degree Δ between the Legitimation Core and Enforcement Core continuously rises, Frag(State) ↑, and the rule structure begins to lose its capacity to sustain PowerCore succession. This is the dynamic starting point of State destabilization and the structural entry point for revolutionary conditions. When Δ(LegitimationCore, EnforcementCore) exceeds the critical threshold, and the social field’s consensus collapse (Social Chapter 9) and power field turbulence (Social Chapter 10) simultaneously act on the State structure, the disturbance conditions required for revolution tend toward maturity. This is what the subsequent chapter addresses.
