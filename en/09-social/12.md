This chapter argues a central proposition:

Social Chapter 9 demonstrated consensus collapse, Social Chapter 10 demonstrated power field dynamics, and Social Chapter 11 demonstrated State destabilization. This chapter addresses the convergence of these three lines — Revolution: revolution is not the replacement of the PowerCore, but the replacement of the continuation slot allocation rule (Rule\_SlotAllocation) that the State depends upon by a competitive rule structure; revolution is not social disorder, but the structural reset of StateGenesis — the old rule structure collapses and the new rule structure redeepens under multiple residual constraints.

Chapter 11: PowerCore succession ≠ State replacement

## Section 1. Purpose

Political science typically defines revolution as: the replacement of class rule or the ruling class structure (Marx), the violent overthrow of government (Weber), or the collapse of State capacity (Skocpol). These definitions all correctly point to certain manifestations of revolution, but offer no sufficient conditions: why do some periods of consensus collapse and State destabilization fail to produce revolution (the prolonged stateless conditions of Libya and Somalia)? Why are some mere PowerCore successions called “revolution” (actually coups)? Why do traces of the old order always persist after revolution?

From sedimentation field dynamics, these questions have a unified answer:

Revolution triggering requires four dynamic lines to be simultaneously satisfied — social-layer consensus dominance loss (Social Chapter 9), State-layer rule-bearing collapse (Social Chapter 11), competition-layer new rule structure formation of independent continuation capacity (RuleGenesis), and supra-threshold external disturbance (Shock\_Rev \> T\_replace(State)).

Missing any single line produces instead: “disorder” (social fracture but State still intact), “coup” (PowerCore succession but rule structure unchanged), “failed State” (old order collapsed but new rule structure unformed), or “reform” (new rule attempts but disturbance insufficient to break the replacement threshold).

Only when all four lines converge does what constitutes revolution in the complete sense occur — the structural replacement of Rule\_SlotAllocation.

This chapter provides the complete dynamic mechanism of revolution triggering, explaining why revolution is a structurally rare event (four lines simultaneously satisfied is extremely difficult); why revolution cannot zero out history (residual constraints); and why some societies stabilize quickly after revolution while others fall into prolonged turmoil (the path dependence of φ\_{post-Rev}).

## Section 2. Core Proposition

The position of On Self-Manifestation is:

Revolution in this chapter refers to the entire rule structure replacement process, comprising two phases: RevolutionOnset (revolution initiation — four conditions triggered, old rule structure begins to collapse) and RevolutionComplete (revolution completion — new rule structure exceeds θ\_rule and new power field exceeds θ\_state, achieving StateGenesis\_new = 1). A potentially extended transition period may exist between the two. If RevolutionOnset occurs but RevolutionComplete is not achieved, the result is revolution failure (corresponding to Configuration Three).

Revolution is the dynamic process in which the old Rule\_SlotAllocation’s sedimentation depth collapses below θ\_rule, and a competitive rule structure Rule\_k having formed independent continuation capacity (RuleGenesis(Rule\_k) = 1) replaces the old rule structure under supra-threshold disturbance as the basis for new StateGenesis — the mark of revolution is not who takes power, but that the rule itself governing “how continuation slots are allocated” has undergone structural replacement.

This definition carries three key implications:

First, revolution ≠ coup. A coup is PowerCore succession with rule structure remaining continuous — the old Rule\_SlotAllocation’s δ has not fallen below θ\_rule and StateGenesis continues to hold. Revolution is Rule\_SlotAllocation replacement — the old StateGenesis no longer holds, and the new rule structure reacquires StateGenesis = 1 during reconstruction.

Second, revolution is a threshold event of four converging lines. Historically, the vast majority of political crises satisfy only one or two lines — consensus collapse but State intact (culture wars); State destabilized but no competitive rule (stateless civil war); competitive rule present but disturbance insufficient (reform movement failure); disturbance sufficient but the remaining three conditions not simultaneously satisfied (coup rather than revolution). Four lines converging simultaneously is an extremely rare structural confluence.

Third, revolution is not the zero-out of history. Since δ\_res^S (old State structure residual), δ\_res^C (old Co-Nucleus residual), and δ\_res^P (old power field residual) simultaneously exist, the new rule structure after revolution never starts from zero — it grows under the constraint of multiple historical residuals, explaining why revolution often “devours its own children”: structural conflict between the new rule structure and old residuals is unavoidable.

## Section 3. Operator Mapping

This chapter introduces the following new definitions on the basis of Social Chapter 11 operators:

Competitive rule structure: Rule\_k, representing the competitive continuation slot allocation rule that deepens within the social field during the period of old Rule\_SlotAllocation disintegration. Rule\_k may originate from: revolutionary ideology, imported institutional templates, historically suppressed alternative rule systems, technologically driven new organizational forms, etc.

Competitive rule genesis condition: RuleGenesis(Rule\_k) = 1, representing that the competitive rule structure has already formed independent continuation capacity, with formation condition:

RuleGenesis(Rule\_k) = 1 ⟺ δ(Rule\_k) \> θ\_pre-rule ∧ Exec(Rule\_k) \> θ\_exec ∧ LegitimacyOverlap\_k \> 0

Where θ\_pre-rule \< θ\_rule — RuleGenesis uses a lower pre-formation threshold than the State rule establishment threshold, representing that the competitive rule has formed independent continuation capacity but has not yet reached the State level; the rule continues deepening after revolution is complete, exceeding θ\_rule to trigger StateGenesis\_new = 1. Note: merely possessing oppositional ideology does not constitute RuleGenesis = 1 — actual continuation slot allocation rules must have begun operating (even if only locally), and the rule must have some consensus basis; coercive organizations or warlord groups with LegitimacyOverlap\_k = 0 do not satisfy RuleGenesis = 1 and constitute only PowerCore competitors rather than rule replacement candidates.

Revolutionary critical fragmentation: Frag\_crit, representing the minimum Frag(State) value required for State destabilization to enter an irreversible process — when Frag(State) \> Frag\_crit, the rule structure’s decay rate persistently exceeds its deepening rate and the State enters irreversible destabilization. Frag\_crit ∝ K\_State (the stronger the State, the greater the fragmentation required to enter the revolutionary zone, the higher the critical threshold).

Revolutionary disturbance: Shock\_Rev, representing the external disturbance required to trigger structural collapse of Rule\_SlotAllocation. Shock\_Rev \> T\_replace(State) is the necessary disturbance condition for revolution triggering.

Post-revolutionary social-field Residual Capacity Ratio: φ\_{post-Rev}, representing the writable capacity of the social field after revolution completion, determining the deepening speed of the new rule structure. The higher φ\_{post-Rev}, the more easily the new rule structure deepens rapidly; the lower it is, the more severe the fragmentation.

Multi-layered residual constraint: ResidualConstraint = δ\_res^S + δ\_res^C + δ\_res^P, representing the comprehensive constraint intensity of all historical residuals on the growth direction of the new rule structure after revolution. ResidualConstraint \> 0 is a universal condition — absolute revolution does not exist.

DominanceLoss(Core\_S) ∧ Frag(State) \> Frag\_crit ∧ RuleGenesis(Rule\_k) = 1 ∧ Shock\_Rev \> T\_replace(State) ⇒ RevolutionOnset (revolution initiated)

Condition Four (disturbance exceeds threshold): Shock\_Rev \> T\_replace(State)  
Internal or external disturbance intensity exceeds the old rule structure’s replacement threshold, triggering StateGenesis = 0.

Conditions One through Four simultaneously satisfied ⇒

δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) ↓ → δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \< θ\_rule → StateGenesis = 0 (old rule structure collapses)

Where ω\_S, ω\_C, ω\_P are the directional weights of each type of residual; the new rule structure cannot fully escape the inertial trajectory of the old rule structure — explaining why revolutionary periods typically retain large amounts of old institutional elements even when revolutionary ideology explicitly demands their elimination.

## Section 5. Three Structural Configurations of Revolution

Revolution manifests in three observable structural configurations in history, depending on the combination of φ\_{post-Rev} and ResidualConstraint:

Configuration One: Rapid reconstruction (high φ\_{post-Rev}, high RuleGenesis(Rule\_k)). High social field Residual Capacity Ratio, new rule structure already sufficiently prepared; post-revolution rapidly enters the new rule deepening phase, and new State structure forms within a relatively short period. Characteristics: short power vacuum after revolution; new rule structure legitimacy rises rapidly; old residuals exist but are rapidly covered. Historical cases: the Meiji Restoration, constitutional construction following American founding.

Configuration Two: Long-term competitive (low φ\_{post-Rev}, multiple competing Rule\_k). After RevolutionOnset, multiple competitive rule structures simultaneously exist without any achieving integration; the social field enters a long-term multi-core competitive state. Characteristics: multiple regimes in parallel after revolution; civil war sustained; each competing Rule\_k has local RuleGenesis = 1 but full-field integration cannot be achieved. Historical cases: the warlord fragmentation of early Republican China, prolonged civil wars following some post-colonial independence.

Configuration Three: Revolution failure (old rule collapsed, RuleGenesis(Rule\_k) = 0 or extremely weak). RevolutionOnset has triggered (old rule structure collapsed), but no competitive rule structure has formed independent continuation capacity — the social field reverts to pure PowerCore competition, no State, only coercive power. Note: this is not “a form of revolution” but a failure outcome in which RevolutionComplete cannot be realized after RevolutionOnset due to the absence of RuleGenesis — the old order is dead, the new order unborn. Characteristics: only sustained power contestation with no rule reconstruction; external intervention typically cannot compensate for the absence of RuleGenesis. Contemporary cases: Libya (post-2011), Somalia (post-1991) during certain periods.

## Section 6. Theorems

Theorem One: Revolutionary Four-Condition Sufficiency Theorem

When the following four conditions are simultaneously satisfied, revolution (structural replacement of Rule\_SlotAllocation) systematically tends to occur: (1) DominanceLoss(Core\_S); (2) Frag(State) \> Frag\_crit; (3) RuleGenesis(Rule\_k) = 1; (4) Shock\_Rev \> T\_replace(State). In the typical revolutionary path as defined in this chapter, the absence of any single condition typically produces only a coup, disorder, reform, failed State, or an incomplete rule-replacement process. Note: the four conditions are sufficient conditions; other triggering paths in history are not excluded.

Proof outline: by the StateGenesis dual condition, State replacement requires δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \< θ\_rule; condition (4) provides supra-threshold disturbance; condition (2) ensures the rule structure is in irreversible decay; condition (1) ensures the consensus support for the old rule has collapsed (otherwise even supra-threshold disturbance would be absorbed and recovered through consensus support); condition (3) ensures the new rule structure already has independent continuation capacity (otherwise the old rule’s collapse only produces a power vacuum); the four conditions together ensure StateGenesis(old) = 0 occurs and, under conditions where φ\_{post-Rev} is sufficient, trends toward StateGenesis(new) = 1 completing the replacement process (if φ\_{post-Rev} is extremely low, StateGenesis(new) = 1 may be delayed or unrealizable, corresponding to Configuration Three). ∎

Theorem Two: Revolutionary Residual Constraint Theorem

After any revolution is completed, ResidualConstraint = δ\_res^S + δ\_res^C + δ\_res^P \> 0, and the growth direction of the new rule structure is constrained by old order residuals — absolute revolution (completely zeroing out historical sedimentation) does not exist; the degree of thoroughness of a revolution is inversely proportional to the magnitude of ResidualConstraint.

Proof outline: by Field Chapter 18, Death\_C in any sedimentation field produces δ\_res \> 0; the old State structure (δ\_res^S), old Co-Nucleus (δ\_res^C), and old power field (δ\_res^P) all produce non-zero residuals after StateGenesis = 0; the three types of residuals constrain the growth path of the new rule structure from different directions; therefore ResidualConstraint \> 0 holds universally and absolute historical zero-out is dynamically impossible. ∎

Theorem Three: Revolution vs. Coup Distinction Theorem

In a social field where StateGenesis = 1 already holds, PowerCore succession takes two forms: (a) if Rule\_SlotAllocation maintains structural continuity (δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) ≥ θ\_rule), the result is a coup — PowerCore changes hands but the State is not replaced; (b) if Rule\_SlotAllocation undergoes structural replacement (δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \< θ\_rule ∧ RuleGenesis(Rule\_k) = 1), the result is revolution — the State structure is replaced along with it.

PowerCore succession ∧ δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) ≥ θ\_rule ⇒ Coup  
Rule\_SlotAllocation replacement ∧ RuleGenesis(Rule\_k) = 1 ⇒ Revolution

Proof outline: by Social Chapter 11’s State Continuity Theorem, PowerCore succession does not equal State replacement as long as the rule structure remains continuous; by the StateGenesis dual condition, State replacement requires δ(Rule\_SlotAllocation) \< θ\_rule; therefore the distinction between the two is entirely determined by whether the rule structure undergoes replacement, independent of whether PowerCore changes hands. ∎

Theorem Four: Post-Revolutionary Convergence Tendency Theorem

Whether the social field quickly forms a new State structure after revolution is determined by φ\_{post-Rev}: if φ\_{post-Rev} is high (sufficient social field writable capacity), the new rule structure systematically tends toward rapid deepening and new StateGenesis = 1 tends toward rapid realization; if φ\_{post-Rev} is low, multiple competitive rule structures mutually suppress each other, new rule deepening speed trends toward zero, and the social field systematically tends toward fragmentation or prolonged statelessness.

Proof outline: by the new rule structure deepening equation, dδ(Rule\_k)/dt ∝ PowerPos\_k · FlowShare\_k · φ\_{post-Rev} · LegitimacyOverlap\_k − Decay(Rule\_k); the higher φ\_{post-Rev}, the higher the deepening rate; when multiple Rule\_k compete, low φ\_{post-Rev} causes each Rule\_k’s deepening rate to fall below the decay term, locking competition in stalemate; high φ\_{post-Rev} allows some Rule\_k to quickly surpass θ\_rule and achieve integration; therefore φ\_{post-Rev} is the key structural parameter determining whether new order can be established after revolution. ∎

## Section 7. Boundary Cases and Structural Breakdown Conditions

Boundary Case One (High fragmentation but no revolution): If conditions one and two are satisfied (consensus collapse + State destabilization) but condition three (no competitive rule formed) or condition four (insufficient disturbance) is not, only prolonged statelessness or multipolar power competition will result, not revolution. Some contemporary Middle Eastern states fit this pattern. This is a boundary condition supporting Theorem One (four conditions minus one) and does not overturn it.

Boundary Case Two (High Shock but no revolution): If disturbance is extremely strong (Shock \>\> T\_replace) but condition one (consensus not yet lost) is not satisfied — old Co-Nucleus depth is high, the vast majority of individual fields in the social field still recognize the legitimacy of the old rule — the old rule may absorb the supra-threshold disturbance and recover under high consensus support, and revolution does not occur. Some historical cases of external invasion or economic collapse not triggering revolution fit this pattern. This is a boundary condition supporting Theorem One and does not overturn it.

Boundary Case Three (Revolution “succeeds” but residuals dominate): If RevolutionOnset triggers but ResidualConstraint is extremely high, the new rule structure rebuilds under the strong constraint of old residuals, and the new State ultimately formed is substantially highly similar to the old State — revolution completed formal replacement but the continuity of dynamic structure far exceeded the revolutionaries’ expectations. The emergence of the Napoleonic Empire after the French Revolution and the Soviet state apparatus’s inheritance of the old imperial bureaucracy fit this pattern. This is a structural breakdown condition supporting Theorem Two (residual constraints universally present) and does not overturn it.

Revolution is the first complete convergence of all dynamic lines from the preceding eleven Social Chapters: social fracture (Chapter 9) × power field turbulence (Chapter 10) × State loss of bearing capacity (Chapter 11) × replacement rule established × supra-threshold disturbance = structural replacement of the rule structure; revolution is not the restart of history, but structural reconstruction under multiple residual constraints.
