# Social Evolution Laws

This chapter argues a central proposition:

Social Chapters 1 through 17 established the complete dynamics of social field genesis, operation, State genesis, revolution, civilization genesis, civilization death, cultural sedimentation, historical residuals, intergenerational transmission, and other mechanisms. But these chapters primarily addressed dynamic states at specific moments or triggering conditions for specific events (revolution, civilization death). A larger question remains unaddressed: how does the social field evolve on long time scales? Why is history not a random walk, yet not linear progress either, but exhibits structured trajectories — why do some civilizations repeatedly cycle between the same institutional forms, why do some societies always return to similar states after external shocks, why do the characteristics of dead civilizations always reappear in their successors?

This chapter’s answer is: social evolution is a historically biased trajectory under the joint action of current dynamic pressure (CurrentDynamics), historical residual bias (Bias(δ\_res^Civ)), and unpredictable perturbations (Noise), expressible as a unified social evolution master equation. History is not purely random because the historical residual bias term is nonzero; history is not determined by historical residuals alone because Noise ≠ 0 and CurrentDynamics remains active.

We seek to identify the dynamic equations of social evolution, four evolution laws, and structural criteria for evolutionary trajectories.

We first state the result.

Social evolution master equation:

dCore\_S/dt = f(CurrentDynamics) + Bias(δ\_res^Civ) · BiasStrength + Noise

Four evolution laws: Path Dependence Law, Evolutionary Inertia Law, Evolutionary Convergence Law, Evolutionary Bifurcation Law.

In a single sentence

History is not a random walk, nor linear progress,  
but a biased trajectory jointly determined by current pressure, historical bias, and perturbation —  
historical residuals cause societies with different starting points to diverge;  
sedimentation inertia makes change slower than expected;  
similar current dynamics cause different civilizations to converge;  
sufficient pressure causes society to bifurcate into multiple trajectories.

## Section 1. Purpose

Social Chapters 1 through 17 provided numerous local mechanisms: how consensus forms (Chapter 9), how revolution occurs (Chapter 12), how civilization dies (Chapter 14), how historical residuals bias (Chapter 16). But these mechanisms were handled independently without integration into a unified evolutionary framework.

Three naive views of social evolution exist historically, each with defects: linear progress theory (society advances in a single direction) cannot explain civilization death and regression; cyclical theory (history repeats periodically) cannot explain civilization’s cumulative change; random theory (history is the result of contingent events) cannot explain history’s structural regularities.

On Self-Manifestation’s answer is: social evolution is a bias-perturbed evolution process — “bias” comes from historical residual bias and sedimentation inertia, “perturbation” comes from unpredictable perturbation terms and the nonlinearity of current dynamics. These three jointly produce the structurally observable regularities in history: path dependence, inertia, convergence, bifurcation.

This chapter formalizes these four regularities as evolution laws, provides the dynamic mechanism of each law, and establishes an isomorphic relationship with the Field Chapters’ Field Evolution Laws.

## Section 2. Core Proposition

The position of On Self-Manifestation is:

Social Evolution is a historically biased trajectory under the joint action of three dynamic forces — current dynamic pressure (CurrentDynamics: including power field, consensus pressure, economic drift, external shocks, etc., from Social Chapters 1 through 12), historical residual bias (Bias(δ\_res^Civ): from Social Chapter 16), and unpredictable perturbations (Noise: individual contingency, natural disasters, technological disruptions, etc.); these three jointly determine the direction and speed of Core\_S evolution, and none can be removed alone; history is not purely random because Bias ≠ 0, history is not determined by historical residuals alone because Noise ≠ 0 and CurrentDynamics remains active, history has structure because f(CurrentDynamics) has stable convergence pressure toward the Stable Core Region.

This definition carries three key implications:

First, the Social Evolution Laws are the realization of the Field Evolution Laws in the social layer. The Field Chapters’ Field Evolution Laws give the general evolution equation of sedimentation fields as dδ/dt = SourceTerm + InertiaConstraint + PerturbationTerm; the Social Evolution Laws realize these three terms as f(CurrentDynamics) + Bias(δ\_res^Civ) + Noise — the source term is determined by current social dynamics, the inertia constraint is determined by historical sedimentation depth, and the perturbation term is determined by unpredictable events. Social evolution is not a new dynamic mechanism but a mapping of the Field Evolution Laws to the Core\_S layer.

Second, the four evolution laws each correspond to one historical pattern. The Path Dependence Law corresponds to “historical starting points determine long-term trajectories”; the Evolutionary Inertia Law corresponds to “why so many things didn’t change after revolution”; the Evolutionary Convergence Law corresponds to “why industrialization/bureaucratization/marketization appears across civilizations”; the Evolutionary Bifurcation Law corresponds to “why religious schisms, national splits, and ideological splits occur after threshold pressure.”

Third, evolutionary trajectory does not equal evolutionary purpose. Social evolution implies no directional purpose (no value judgments of “progress” or “regression”) — dCore\_S/dt only describes direction and speed, without evaluating the goodness or badness of destinations.

## Section 3. Operator Mapping

This chapter introduces the following new definitions on the basis of Social Chapter 17 operators:

Current dynamic term: f(CurrentDynamics), representing the direct contribution of power field, consensus, economic drift, external shocks, and other factors in the current social field to the evolutionary direction of Core\_S, derived from the dynamic mechanisms of Social Chapters 1 through 12.

Evolutionary inertia coefficient: InertiaCoeff(Core\_S) := δ(Core\_S), representing the current sedimentation depth of Core\_S — the deeper the sedimentation, the lower the rate of change of Core\_S under the same pressure.

Stable Core Region: StableCoreRegion(F\_S), representing the structural region toward which Core\_S tends to converge under given CurrentDynamics, historical residual bias (Bias), and field capacity constraints, defined as:

StableCoreRegion(F\_S) := { Core\_S \| under current constraints, Ω(Core\_S) relatively high ∧ compression relatively stable ∧ continuation cost relatively low (local optimum) }, holding under current dynamic conditions and capacity constraints

Note: StableCoreRegion is not an external destination but a locally stable region under current constraint conditions — the social field tends toward it not because of any purpose, but because this region has the lowest continuation friction; it is an endogenous convergence result of field dynamics, unrelated to teleology.

That is, in a finite capacity-constrained social field under persistent dynamic pressure, the field naturally tends toward the Core\_S region where continuation cost is relatively lower, Familiar Route sedimentation depth is relatively deeper, and compression structure is relatively stable — this is directly derived from Field Chapters 7 (sedimentation), 11 (Familiar Routes), and 13 (K\_f compression), without needing to introduce external Attractor concepts; convergence is an endogenous result of field dynamics. StableCoreRegion is isomorphic with Field Chapter 23’s “Equilibrium 1” (high K\_f, low φ\_f stable state); its specific form is determined by SDC\_f: societies with higher SDC\_f can maintain stability at higher sedimentation levels; societies with lower SDC\_f correspond to shallower sedimentation at stable states. By this: societies with different SDC\_f will converge to different StableCoreRegion forms even when CurrentDynamics converge — explaining why industrialization, bureaucratization, and marketization produce institutionally similar but substantively distinctive forms across civilizations. The transfer mechanism of StableCoreRegion is developed in Social Chapter 19.

Evolutionary bifurcation threshold: θ\_fork, representing the minimum pressure intensity that destabilizes Core\_S and splits it into multiple trajectories — when Pressure \> θ\_fork, the current Core\_S structure cannot maintain a single stable state and society enters multi-trajectory evolution.

Bifurcation set: Fork(Core\_S) = {Core\_S1, Core\_S2, …}, representing the set of multiple competing trajectories that Core\_S may enter after bifurcation.

## Section 4. Dynamic Equations

Social evolution master equation:

dCore\_S/dt = f(CurrentDynamics) + Bias(δ\_res^Civ) · BiasStrength + Noise

Where f(CurrentDynamics) is the current dynamic driving term (sign and magnitude determined by the specific mechanisms of Social Chapters 1 through 12); Bias(δ\_res^Civ) · BiasStrength is the historical residual bias term (see Social Chapter 16); Noise is the unpredictable perturbation term, zero mean but nonzero variance.

Evolutionary inertia equation (under simplified conditions ignoring Bias and Noise, focusing on the inertia term):

dCore\_S/dt ∝ f(CurrentDynamics) / δ(Core\_S)

That is, under the same current dynamic pressure, the deeper Core\_S’s sedimentation, the lower its rate of change — sedimentation depth, as the evolutionary inertia coefficient, reduces the efficiency of any external pressure in changing Core\_S. Note: this explains why after revolution, societies undergo dramatic changes in institutional form while behavioral patterns, value defaults, and ritual habits often lag by several generations before structural change — Core\_S’s sedimentation depth buffers short-term shocks.

Path dependence equation:

If δ\_res^Civ(A) ≠ δ\_res^Civ(B), then even if f(CurrentDynamics\_A) ≈ f(CurrentDynamics\_B), after sufficiently long time Trajectory(Core\_S\_A) ≠ Trajectory(Core\_S\_B)

Note: the premise here is that Bias(δ\_res^Civ(A)) and Bias(δ\_res^Civ(B)) do not completely cancel in direction — if two different residuals happen to produce biases of completely opposite direction and equal magnitude, they can theoretically cancel, and the Path Dependence Law fails in this boundary case; under general conditions this cancellation does not hold, and the law holds.

That is, social fields with different historical residuals will systematically diverge in long-term trajectories even when current dynamics are similar — the accumulated difference in historical residual bias terms causes trajectory divergence.

Evolutionary convergence equation:

If f(CurrentDynamics\_A) → f(CurrentDynamics\_B) and δ\_res^Civ(A) ≈ δ\_res^Civ(B), then Trajectory(Core\_S\_A) → StableCoreRegion ← Trajectory(Core\_S\_B)

That is, under conditions where current dynamics converge and historical residuals are similar, different social field trajectories tend toward the same Stable Core Region — this is the dynamic explanation for cross-civilizational institutional convergence (industrialization, bureaucratization, marketization, educational system expansion).

Evolutionary bifurcation equation:

If Pressure(Core\_S) \> θ\_fork, then:

dCore\_S/dt no longer converges to a single trajectory ⇒ Core\_S → Fork(Core\_S) = {Core\_S1, Core\_S2, …}

That is, when social pressure exceeds the bifurcation threshold, the original Core\_S structure destabilizes and society enters multi-trajectory competitive evolution — different individual fields or subgroups begin deepening along different trajectories; the competitive outcome in the bifurcation set is determined by subsequent dynamics.

## Section 5. Four Evolution Laws

Law One: Path Dependence Law

Social evolutionary trajectories are systematically constrained by historical residual bias — social fields with different historical residuals will systematically diverge in long-term evolutionary trajectories even when current dynamic structures are similar; history cannot be structurally erased, because δ\_res^Civ \> 0 ∧ FamOverlap \> 0 implies persistent nonzero bias.

Observable implication: two societies that underwent similar institutional reforms (same CurrentDynamics changes) but originated from different civilizational residuals (different δ\_res^Civ) will exhibit systematically different results within a few generations after reform.

Field calibration: trajectory divergence rate is proportional to \|Bias(δ\_res^Civ(A)) − Bias(δ\_res^Civ(B))\|, where Bias ∝ FamOverlap · δ\_res^Civ (Social Chapter 16); δ\_res^Civ can be estimated by weighted summation of cultural layer δ\_res(i) (Social Chapter 15) — therefore the relative magnitude of trajectory divergence rate can in principle be rank-ordered across civilizations through cultural layer proxy variable comparisons (language preservation rate, ritual participation rate, narrative awareness rate, value identification rate).

Formation condition: δ\_res^Civ(A) ≠ δ\_res^Civ(B) (difference in historical residuals exists) ∧ Bias directions do not completely cancel.  
Failure condition: when δ\_res^Civ → 0 (historical residuals structurally exit), bias disappears and path dependence weakens to random drift.

Law Two: Evolutionary Inertia Law

Social evolutionary speed is inversely proportional to Core\_S sedimentation depth — the deeper the sedimentation, the lower Core\_S’s rate of change under equivalent external pressure; short-term shocks (such as single revolutions or single policy changes) alter Core\_S’s surface components (ValueLayer, NarrativeLayer), while altering RitualLayer and LanguageLayer requires sustained cross-generational pressure; civilizational inertia is not resistance but sedimentation buffering.

Observable implication: the more historically deeply sedimented a civilization, the greater the time and pressure required for its reform; the more historically shallowly sedimented a society (newly established colonies, post-war reconstruction states), the faster its institutional transformation speed, but also less stable.

Field calibration: from Field Chapter 23, fields in the compression-rigidification phase (Stage 2) satisfy persistently declining φ\_f and rising K\_f — the social field’s evolutionary vitality can be quantified as Vitality ≈ φ\_f · (1−K\_f) · Rc; deeper δ(Core\_S) ↔ higher K\_f ↔ lower Vitality; reform pressure required ∝ T\_replace ∝ SDC\_f · K\_f (Field Chapter 22) — hence the Evolutionary Inertia Law can use T\_replace as a quantifiable proxy: societies with higher T\_replace require stronger external shocks for reform, with greater inertia.

Formation condition: δ(Core\_S) · Ω(Core\_S) \> 0 (both Co-Nucleus sedimentation depth and structural integrity nonzero; if sedimentation depth exists but structural integrity has collapsed, i.e. Ω → 0, inertia also fails).  
Failure condition: when δ(Core\_S) · Ω(Core\_S) → 0 (social field extremely shallow or fragmented), evolutionary inertia disappears and Core\_S’s rate of change tends toward complete determination by current dynamics.

Law Three: Evolutionary Convergence Law

Under conditions where current dynamics converge and historical residuals are similar, trajectories of social fields with different starting points tend to converge on a common Stable Core Region — this is not a value judgment about cultural convergence, but similar CurrentDynamics structures producing similar sedimentation pressures, causing different social fields’ Core\_S evolutionary directions to tend toward alignment.

Observable implication: societies with converging industrialization pressure (similar CurrentDynamics) tend to produce similar bureaucratic structures, urbanization patterns, and mass education systems, even when departing from different civilizational residuals; globalization accelerates the convergence of CurrentDynamics, thereby accelerating cross-civilizational convergence toward StableCoreRegion.

Field calibration: StableCoreRegion corresponds to Field Chapter 23’s “Equilibrium 1” (high K\_f, low φ\_f stable state) — when CurrentDynamics converge, multiple social fields tend toward the same compression equilibrium state; the specific form of StableCoreRegion is determined by SDC\_f — societies with similar SDC\_f will converge to similar structural forms; societies with large SDC\_f differences will converge to different stable states even when CurrentDynamics converge; hence SDC\_f is the key proxy variable for predicting convergence endpoints.

Formation condition: f(CurrentDynamics\_A) → f(CurrentDynamics\_B) and δ\_res^Civ differences trending smaller.  
Failure condition: when δ\_res^Civ differences are sufficiently large, the Path Dependence Law dominates and the Convergence Law fails — the difference in civilizational residuals exceeds the pull of current dynamic convergence.

Law Four: Evolutionary Bifurcation Law

When social pressure exceeds the bifurcation threshold (Pressure \> θ\_fork), the original Core\_S structure destabilizes and society enters multi-trajectory competitive evolution — bifurcation is not random, but determined by competitive Core structures already existing before bifurcation (serving as sub-optimal Familiar Routes under low pressure, becoming candidate trajectories under bifurcation); trajectory selection after bifurcation is jointly determined by subsequent CurrentDynamics and Noise.

Observable implication: religious schisms, ideological splits, and national divisions are typically triggered when long-accumulated pressure (competitive narrative or value systems with high δ\_res) exceeds social field integration capacity (θ\_fork determined by δ(Core\_S)); post-bifurcation societies typically retain large amounts of pre-bifurcation common residuals for a period, until new trajectories are sufficiently deep.

Field calibration: θ\_fork corresponds to T\_replace in Field Chapter 22 — θ\_fork ≈ T\_replace(Core\_S) ∝ SDC\_f · K\_f; where K\_Core (social layer operator, = δ(Core\_S) · Ω(Core\_S)) corresponds to field K\_f, and SDC\_f corresponds to the social field’s differential tolerance capacity; the two are connected through the mapping K\_Core ∝ K\_f, hence θ\_fork ∝ K\_Core ∝ δ(Core\_S) · Ω(Core\_S) and θ\_fork ∝ SDC\_f · K\_f are the same proportional relationship expressed in social-layer and field-layer terms. Predicting whether a society is approaching bifurcation is equivalent to predicting whether its K\_f is approaching the critical value at which T\_replace can be exceeded by actual Shock.

Formation condition: Pressure \> θ\_fork (≈ T\_replace(Core\_S)) ∧ at least two candidate competing Core structures exist.  
Failure condition: if pressure exceeds threshold but no candidate competing Core structure exists, bifurcation degrades to social disintegration (Social Chapter 17 Form Three) or revolution failure (Social Chapter 12 Form Three).

## Section 6. Theorems

Theorem One: Social Evolution Structural Non-Reversibility Theorem

Core\_S’s historical evolutionary trajectory is irreversible — at any moment t, Core\_S(t) carries all historical evolutionary sedimentation from t₀ to t; it is impossible to completely “reset” Core\_S to an earlier state through any current dynamic operation, which can only bias its future evolutionary direction.

Proof outline: by the social evolution master equation, dCore\_S/dt contains the Bias(δ\_res^Civ) term; as long as δ\_res^Civ \> 0 and FamOverlap \> 0, the bias term is nonzero; δ\_res^Civ is constituted by historical sedimentation and cannot be zeroed out through current dynamic operations (see Social Chapter 16’s Structural Exit condition: only δ\_res^Civ → 0 can exit, which requires extremely long natural dissipation); therefore Core\_S’s evolutionary trajectory is irreversible within any finite time, and until Structural Exit is complete, past sedimentation persistently biases the future direction (see Social Chapter 16’s Structural Exit condition). ∎

Theorem Two: Evolutionary Trajectory Separation Theorem

If two social fields have the same Core\_S at moment t₀ but subsequently undergo different historical residual updates (different δ\_res^Civ), then after sufficiently long time the distance between Trajectory(Core\_S\_A) and Trajectory(Core\_S\_B) systematically tends to increase — social fields with “the same starting point, different histories” systematically tend toward long-term divergence (strong Noise, forced assimilation, or globalizing convergence may cancel differences within finite time, but do not change the long-term tendency toward divergence).

Proof outline: by the Path Dependence Law, different δ\_res^Civ produce different Bias terms; accumulated Bias term differences cause the direction of dCore\_S/dt to produce persistent separation; by the path dependence equation, trajectory separation rate is proportional to \|Bias(δ\_res^Civ(A)) − Bias(δ\_res^Civ(B))\|; therefore social fields with different historical residuals systematically diverge. ∎

Theorem Three: Evolutionary Inertia and Bifurcation Threshold Relationship Theorem

The social field’s bifurcation threshold θ\_fork is proportional to the current Core\_S’s combined sedimentation and structural integrity: θ\_fork ≈ T\_replace(Core\_S) ∝ K\_Core ∝ δ(Core\_S) · Ω(Core\_S) — societies with deeper sedimentation require higher pressure to enter bifurcation evolution; societies with shallower sedimentation more easily bifurcate under lower pressure.

Proof outline: by the Evolutionary Inertia Law, dCore\_S/dt ∝ Pressure / δ(Core\_S); Core\_S’s stability depends on whether dCore\_S/dt exceeds some critical destabilization value — when Pressure is large enough to cause dCore\_S/dt to exceed the stability threshold, Core\_S destabilizes and enters bifurcation; since dCore\_S/dt ∝ Pressure / δ(Core\_S), the Pressure\_crit required for the same destabilization condition ∝ δ(Core\_S); therefore θ\_fork ≈ T\_replace(Core\_S) ∝ K\_Core ∝ δ(Core\_S) · Ω(Core\_S), sedimentation depth determines the social field’s bifurcation resistance. ∎

## Section 7. Boundary Cases and Structural Breakdown Conditions

Boundary Case One (Strong Noise overrides path dependence): Under extreme circumstances (large-scale natural disasters, nuclear-war-level technological shocks, extraterrestrial civilization contact), the Noise term can substantially suppress the Bias term, causing short-term evolution to deviate from path dependence predictions. This shows that the Path Dependence Law fails when Noise \>\> Bias — historical residuals’ bias force can be temporarily suppressed by sufficiently large perturbations. This is a boundary condition, does not overturn Theorem One (Theorem One describes long-term trends; short-term Noise does not change historical sedimentation depth itself).

Boundary Case Two (Forced assimilation rapidly zeroes out δ\_res^Civ): Systematic cultural elimination (such as certain colonial policies) rapidly suppresses δ\_res^Civ through Pathway C (Social Chapter 14 acute collapse), significantly weakening the Path Dependence Law’s bias force within a few generations. This shows that the strength of the Path Dependence Law is directly related to the maintenance mechanism of δ\_res^Civ; if historical residuals are themselves systematically destroyed, path dependence weakens. This is a boundary condition, supports the Path Dependence Law (condition is δ\_res^Civ \> 0), and does not overturn it.

Boundary Case Three (Convergence Law and Path Dependence Law simultaneously holding): Some societies (such as Meiji Restoration Japan) maintained high δ\_res^Civ (deep CivilCore residuals) while CurrentDynamics converged (industrialization pressure), manifesting as institutional convergence toward StableCoreRegion while maintaining path-dependent differentiated trajectories in cultural substance — this shows that the Convergence Law and Path Dependence Law can simultaneously hold but act at different layers (ValueLayer/NarrativeLayer converge; RitualLayer/LanguageLayer remain separated). This is a boundary condition showing that the four laws act at different layers and do not mutually negate each other.

## Section 8. Summary Formula

Social evolution dynamics general formula:

dCore\_S/dt = f(CurrentDynamics) + Bias(δ\_res^Civ) · BiasStrength + Noise (social evolution master equation)

dCore\_S/dt ∝ f(CurrentDynamics) / δ(Core\_S) (Evolutionary Inertia Law; deeper sedimentation, slower change)

δ\_res^Civ(A) ≠ δ\_res^Civ(B) ⇒ Trajectory(A) ≠ Trajectory(B) (Path Dependence Law; different historical residuals imply systematically diverging trajectories)

f(CurrentDynamics\_A) → f(CurrentDynamics\_B) ∧ δ\_res^Civ differences trending smaller ⇒ Trajectory → StableCoreRegion (Evolutionary Convergence Law; converging current dynamics imply trajectory convergence)

Pressure \> θ\_fork ⇒ Core\_S → Fork(Core\_S) (Evolutionary Bifurcation Law; pressure exceeding threshold implies trajectory bifurcation)

θ\_fork ≈ T\_replace(Core\_S) ∝ K\_Core ∝ δ(Core\_S) · Ω(Core\_S) (Evolutionary Inertia and Bifurcation Threshold Relationship Theorem; deeper sedimentation and higher structural integrity imply higher bifurcation threshold)

The Social Evolution Laws are the synthesis of all mechanisms in Social Chapters 1 through 17 — CurrentDynamics from Social Chapters 1 through 12, Bias from Social Chapter 16, inertia from Core\_S sedimentation depth (throughout all chapters); the evolution laws provide a structural framework for determining the long-term trajectory of any social field, while preserving acknowledgment of short-term unpredictability (Noise).
